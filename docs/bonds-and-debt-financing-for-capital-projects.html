Bonds and Debt: How Transit Systems Finance Big Projects

Building a mile of subway costs $1–2 billion. No transit agency has that much cash on hand. Instead, agencies issue bonds — borrowing money from capital markets and repaying it over 20–30 years. Understanding how this debt works explains why agencies often feel cash-poor even when they're building expensive new infrastructure.

How Municipal and Revenue Bonds Work

Transit agencies use primarily two types of bonds. General obligation (GO) bonds are backed by the taxing power of a government entity — usually a county or special district — and are repaid from dedicated tax revenue. Revenue bonds are backed by a specific income stream, such as sales tax revenue or (for the MTA) congestion pricing proceeds. Both are sold to investors in the municipal bond market and typically carry lower interest rates than taxable bonds because the interest is federal income tax exempt.

The MTA is one of the largest issuers of revenue bonds in the U.S. municipal market, with over $40 billion in outstanding debt. Bond ratings from Moody's and S&P affect the interest rate: a downgrade can increase borrowing costs by millions annually.

Debt Service and Operating Budget Pressure

Debt service — annual principal and interest payments on outstanding bonds — competes directly with operating expenditures for revenue. The MTA spends approximately $3 billion annually on debt service, representing about 15% of its total budget. This is money that cannot go to bus service frequency, subway maintenance, or worker salaries.

When agencies issue new bonds to fund capital projects, they must identify revenue streams to cover the new debt service. If those revenue streams don't materialize (e.g., projected ridership growth doesn't occur), the debt service becomes a structural operating deficit.

Federal TIFIA and Other Loan Programs

The Transportation Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act (TIFIA) program provides federal loans to transit and transportation projects at below-market interest rates. TIFIA loans can cover up to 33% of project cost and are particularly valuable for agencies with revenue bonds, because the federal interest rate subsidy can save tens of millions over the life of the loan.

Several major transit projects have used TIFIA loans, including Denver's Eagle P3 commuter rail, Los Angeles's Wilshire/Western subway extension, and the Purple Line in Maryland. TIFIA doesn't eliminate debt burden but reduces its cost.

Debt Limits and Financial Sustainability

Agencies face legal and practical limits on debt. Bond covenants typically require that revenue coverage ratios (revenue available for debt service divided by debt service) stay above 1.25–1.50x — meaning available revenue must be at least 25–50% more than the annual debt payment. Dropping below this threshold triggers default provisions and can increase borrowing costs.

Financial sustainability requires that capital investment today doesn't crowd out operating budgets tomorrow. Agencies that build expensive new infrastructure without planning for its operating costs — adding service to new lines while cutting existing service to pay for operations — are mortgaging ridership for capital.

Key Takeaways
  • The MTA has over $40 billion in outstanding bond debt and spends ~$3 billion annually (15% of budget) on debt service.
  • Revenue bonds are backed by specific income streams (sales tax, congestion pricing) rather than general taxing power.
  • TIFIA federal loans provide below-market financing for major transit projects, covering up to 33% of project cost.
  • Bond covenants require revenue-to-debt coverage ratios of 1.25–1.50x, creating hard fiscal constraints on new borrowing.
