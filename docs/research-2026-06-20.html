Executive Summary

Today's twelve papers cluster around three live fault lines in transit research: how to evaluate big policy interventions without clean control groups (NYC congestion pricing), how to plan service into areas with no data to justify it (transit deserts, justice-informed AMoD), and how AI is starting to take over both the analytical backend (graph transformers, data architectures) and the rider-facing frontend (ChatPlanner, TransitLM) of transit systems. A second thread, less flashy but arguably more consequential, concerns measurement itself: minimum-end satisfaction heuristics and GIS accessibility metrics show that how agencies measure success shapes which problems get fixed.

Emerging Themes

Foundation models and LLMs are migrating from generic NLP/forecasting use cases into transit-specific tasks: counterfactual demand forecasting, conversational route planning, and even map-free route generation.

Equity and justice are increasingly built into the optimization objective itself (CVaR-constrained transit-desert planning, justice-informed AMoD) rather than measured after the fact.

Resilience research is shifting from single-mode hardening toward structural, multimodal interconnectedness and coordinated cross-mode disruption response as the more durable strategy.

Paper Analyses
Paper 01 · arXiv (2606.17530)

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
Using time-series foundation models to build probabilistic counterfactuals, the authors find NYC's congestion pricing program lifted bus and subway ridership system-wide while overall travel demand fell only modestly and unevenly across neighborhoods.
Why It Matters

This is the first rigorous, uncertainty-aware before/after evaluation of America's first cordon tolling scheme, supplying the empirical ammunition (or caution) other US cities will cite when debating their own congestion pricing programs.

Key Insights
  • Time-series foundation models generate calibrated counterfactual demand forecasts when no clean control group exists
  • Bus and subway ridership rose significantly above the no-policy baseline after January 2025
  • Reductions in overall travel demand concentrated inside the Congestion Relief Zone, but transit gains spread beyond Manhattan's core
  • Socio-demographic analysis shows uneven adaptation: some neighborhoods absorbed the shift far better than others
  • The forecasting framework is reusable for evaluating other system-wide interventions lacking natural experiments
Methodology

Probabilistic counterfactual forecasting with pretrained time-series foundation models applied to bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data, cross-referenced against neighborhood socio-demographics.

Results

Statistically significant post-policy increases in bus and subway ridership relative to forecasted no-policy demand; modest net decline in total travel demand, geographically concentrated and unevenly distributed by neighborhood.

Limitations

Counterfactual forecasts are model-derived rather than observed, so findings inherit whatever bias the foundation model carries; only one year of post-policy data is available so far.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies in cordon-pricing candidate cities (Boston, Chicago, LA) can use this framework to project mode-shift before committing political capital, and equity offices can flag at-risk neighborhoods early.

Who Should Care

City DOTs and transit agencies considering congestion pricing, transportation economists, equity and accessibility planners

Comparison to Prior Work

Earlier congestion-pricing evaluations (London, Stockholm, Singapore) relied on cleaner control groups or simpler before/after comparisons; this is among the first to apply foundation-model counterfactuals to a US case with this level of spatial granularity

Paper 02 · arXiv (2606.15315)

ChatPlanner: A Large Language Model Framework for Personalized Public Transit Routing

Tingting Yang, Chenhao Xue, Jun Chen
ChatPlanner fine-tunes an LLM with retrieval-augmented generation so riders can describe trip preferences in plain language and get routes that actually reflect what they asked for.
Why It Matters

Most trip planners only optimize time or transfers; ChatPlanner is an early concrete demonstration that natural-language preference capture (e.g. 'avoid stairs,' 'minimize walking in the heat') can be wired directly into a transit routing objective function.

Key Insights
  • Fine-tuned LLM + RAG extracts routing parameters and nuanced preferences from conversational queries
  • Eight rider personas and five contexts were built to create scoring standards for both fine-tuning and retrieval
  • Fine-tuning alone enforces output structure and generalizes preference patterns; RAG alone resolves ambiguous, query-specific phrasing
  • Combining both methods produced the highest accuracy for preference extraction and routing
  • Case studies show ChatPlanner surfaces alternative routes that conventional planners overlook entirely
Methodology

Fine-tuned LLM combined with retrieval-augmented generation, evaluated through three experiments: feasibility, parameter/preference extraction accuracy, and route solution-set quality.

Results

ChatPlanner reliably generates feasible routes; the fine-tuned+RAG combination outperforms either approach alone on extraction accuracy and surfaces preference-sensitive alternatives missed by standard planners.

Limitations

Evaluated on constructed personas and case studies rather than live deployment with real riders; persona/context taxonomy may not generalize to all rider populations or transit networks.

Practical Implications

Transit apps (Transit, Citymapper, agency trip planners) could integrate conversational preference capture to serve riders with mobility, comfort, or accessibility constraints that fixed filters don't cover.

Who Should Care

Transit app developers, accessibility advocates, agencies modernizing trip-planning tools

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior personalized routing work used fixed preference sliders or weighted multi-objective optimization; ChatPlanner is among the first to push natural-language preference understanding directly into the routing objective rather than a pre-filter.

Paper 03 · arXiv (2606.08849)

A Resilience-as-a-Service assessment framework for coordinated disruption response in interdependent urban transit systems

Sara Jaber, S. M. Hassan Mahdavi, Neila Bhouri, Mostafa Ameli
A KPI-driven framework combining optimization with agent-based simulation shows that coordinated, multimodal disruption response on Paris's RER B line beats single-mode fixes on cost, equity, and service continuity simultaneously.
Why It Matters

Service disruptions are a leading driver of rider distrust; this paper gives operators a quantitative way to compare disruption-response strategies on equity and emissions, not just speed of recovery.

Key Insights
  • Resilience is treated as multiple complementary dimensions (vulnerability, adaptability, robustness, responsiveness, cost, emissions, equity) rather than one score
  • The framework accounts for secondary degradation on 'helper lines' when vehicles are pulled to cover a disrupted corridor
  • Tested on the real RER B line in the Ile-de-France network
  • Coordinated multimodal response outperformed single-mode alternatives on cost and equity while staying competitive on emissions
  • Sensitivity analysis identifies which disruption conditions make coordination most valuable
Methodology

Optimization model combined with behavioral agent-based simulation, applied as a case study to the RER B commuter rail line in the Paris region.

Results

Coordinated multimodal disruption response achieved the most balanced resilience profile: higher service continuity, lower total disruption cost than single-mode responses, improved equity, and competitive emissions performance.

Limitations

Single case-study line; the helper-line degradation modeling may not transfer directly to networks with less redundancy than Ile-de-France's.

Practical Implications

Operations control centers can use this KPI framework to evaluate bus-bridging and substitution plans before a disruption occurs, rather than improvising in real time.

Who Should Care

Rail and commuter-rail operators, service planning and operations control teams, resilience researchers

Comparison to Prior Work

Earlier resilience studies typically scored a single network-topology metric; this work is novel in pairing optimization with agent-based behavioral simulation across eight resilience dimensions on a live network.

Paper 04 · Paper of the Day · arXiv (2606.08371)

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty

Polina Khoroshevskaya, Ashish Kumar Perukari
Framing transit-desert remediation as a partially observable Markov decision process with financial risk constraints, this study shows a belief-aware planner remediates more transit deserts at lower financial risk than static optimization across 25 US cities.
Why It Matters

Tens of millions of Americans live in transit deserts where no ridership data exists to justify new service, creating a chicken-and-egg planning problem; this is a concrete, deployable method for breaking that deadlock.

Key Insights
  • Formulates the planning problem as a POMDP with Conditional Value-at-Risk constraints for financial tail risk
  • Builds a prior over latent demand from demographic, land-use, and employment data, then updates it once new service is deployed
  • The belief-aware planner remediated a median of 53.6% of transit-desert tracts after five years across 25 cities
  • Outperformed static optimization by 5.0 percentage points on average, with gains in 16 of 25 cities
  • Gains were largest at moderate budgets (+9.9 points) and persisted even with 50% prior-demand miscalibration
  • Population density and existing transit density were the strongest predictors of remediation cost (R²=0.41)
Methodology

Partially observable Markov decision process with CVaR financial risk constraints, evaluated via a myopic belief-aware planner against static optimization baselines across 25 US cities using a unified operating-cost/capex/fare-revenue/subsidy financial model.

Results

Median 53.6% of transit-desert tracts remediated after five years; 5.0 percentage point average improvement over static optimization, robust to substantial prior-demand miscalibration.

Limitations

Myopic (greedy) planner rather than a fully optimal POMDP solver; results depend on the quality of demographic/land-use priors which vary by city data availability.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and MPOs can use this approach to sequence service expansions into underserved areas with bounded financial risk instead of waiting for ridership data that will never appear without service.

Who Should Care

Transit planners, MPOs, equity-focused service planning teams, transportation finance officers

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior transit-desert work was largely descriptive (identifying deserts via GIS) rather than prescriptive; this paper is notable for embedding financial risk management directly into the sequential service-expansion decision.

Paper 05 · arXiv (2606.00572)

Spatiotemporal Multi-Task Graph Transformer for Trip-Level Transit Prediction

Oluwaleke Yusuf, Adil Rasheed, Frank Lindseth
SMT-GraphFormer models a transit line's boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell time jointly as a sequence-to-sequence problem, beating stop-level tabular models on Trondheim bus data.
Why It Matters

Most ridership forecasting tools predict one stop or one metric at a time; treating an entire trip as a sequence captures how crowding and delay cascade down a line, which is exactly the dynamic that breaks naive forecasts during real-world service.

Key Insights
  • Frames trip-level transit prediction as sequence-to-sequence modeling rather than fixed per-stop regression
  • Uses graph embeddings for multi-relational stop similarity plus a context encoder for weather and temporal features
  • A multi-gate mixture-of-experts module generates task-specific decoder representations for boarding vs. alighting
  • Delay and dwell time are modeled as encoder-side surrogate tasks that improve the main predictions
  • Outperforms stop-level tabular benchmarks, with the largest gain (+0.24 R²) on alighting prediction
Methodology

Spatiotemporal multi-task graph transformer (SMT-GraphFormer) evaluated against tabular benchmarks on public bus transit data from Trondheim, Norway, with ablation studies isolating each architectural component.

Results

Outperforms stop-level tabular models across boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell-time prediction; alighting prediction improves by +0.24 R² from the sequential formulation alone.

Limitations

Validated on a single mid-size European bus network; transferability to larger, multimodal networks (e.g., subway-bus transfers) is untested.

Practical Implications

Provides a horizon-agnostic prediction backbone usable in digital-twin simulations for service planning, crew scheduling, and real-time crowding alerts.

Who Should Care

Transit data scientists, service planners building digital twins, ML researchers in spatiotemporal forecasting

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior graph-neural and transformer ridership models, which mostly handled single tasks at fixed stops, by unifying boarding/alighting/delay/dwell into one multi-task sequence model.

Paper 06 · arXiv (2605.25617)

Justice-informed Planning of Intermodal Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems under Operational Constraints

Giacomo Ganassoli, Francesco Mazzeo, Cecilia Pasquale, Silvia Siri, Mauro Salazar
Modeling Manhattan-scale robotaxi-plus-transit operations, this paper shows that pairing free public transit with autonomous mobility-on-demand achieves near-total social justice without sacrificing system efficiency, while monetary budget caps alone barely move the needle.
Why It Matters

As cities weigh robotaxi expansion against transit funding, this is rare quantitative evidence that 'free transit + AMoD' beats 'subsidized AMoD' for equity, reframing the policy debate from fleet regulation toward transit fare policy.

Key Insights
  • Network flow models characterize intermodal AMoD operations (robotaxis plus public transit and active modes) from a mesoscopic planning perspective
  • Optimization is run for both utilitarian efficiency and explicit justice-informed objectives
  • Case study built on real-world Manhattan, New York network data
  • Monetary budget limits alone significantly cap the social-justice potential of AMoD systems run as transportation network companies
  • Granting free public transit gets sufficiency levels nearly as high as a fully free intermodal AMoD system
  • Justice-informed operations can be achieved without compromising standard efficiency metrics
Methodology

Mesoscopic network flow optimization models for intermodal AMoD systems, comparing utilitarian and justice-informed objective functions on a real-world Manhattan case study.

Results

Budget constraints alone barely improve justice outcomes; combining free public transit with intermodal AMoD nearly matches the justice outcomes of a fully free system, with no loss in efficiency.

Limitations

Manhattan's dense transit network may not generalize to sprawling or transit-poor cities where AMoD has less to complement.

Practical Implications

Policymakers debating robotaxi regulation should weigh transit fare subsidies as a more powerful equity lever than capping AMoD fares or trip allowances.

Who Should Care

City transportation policy offices, AMoD/robotaxi regulators, transit equity researchers

Comparison to Prior Work

Builds on a growing AMoD optimization literature that has mostly optimized for efficiency; distinctive for directly comparing justice-informed objectives against monetary-budget-constrained alternatives in the same framework.

MEDIUM CONFIDENCE
Paper 07 · arXiv (2605.22355)

TransitLM: A Large-Scale Dataset and Benchmark for Map-Free Transit Route Generation

Hanyu Guo, Jiedong Yang, Chao Chen, Longfei Xu, Kaikui Liu, Xiangxiang Chu
TransitLM releases 13 million transit route records from four Chinese cities and shows an LLM trained on it can generate valid transit routes and ground GPS coordinates to stations without any explicit map data.
Why It Matters

If transit routing can be learned end-to-end from data instead of hand-built routing engines and map infrastructure, smaller agencies and developing-world cities without mature GIS pipelines could deploy trip planning far more cheaply.

Key Insights
  • 13M+ transit route planning records across 120,845 stations and 13,666 lines in four Chinese cities
  • Released as both a continual pre-training corpus and a three-task benchmark with complementary metrics
  • An LLM trained on TransitLM produces structurally valid routes at high accuracy
  • The model implicitly grounds arbitrary GPS coordinates to the right station without explicit coordinate-to-station mapping
  • Demonstrates route planning can be learned entirely from data, enabling map-free, end-to-end generation from origin-destination pairs
Methodology

Large-scale dataset construction (13M+ records, 4 cities) released for continual pre-training, with an LLM trained and evaluated against three benchmark tasks measuring route validity, station grounding, and overall quality.

Results

LLM trained on TransitLM achieves high structural validity in generated routes and implicitly grounds GPS coordinates to stations without explicit mapping logic.

Limitations

Trained and evaluated only on Chinese city data; map-free routing sacrifices the auditability and explainability of traditional graph-based routing engines.

Practical Implications

Could lower the cost of deploying trip-planning tools in cities with incomplete GTFS/GIS infrastructure, though agencies will need to weigh explainability tradeoffs against traditional routing engines.

Who Should Care

Transit app developers in data-sparse regions, ML researchers working on geospatial LLMs, agencies modernizing legacy routing engines

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior transit LLM efforts (e.g. ChatPlanner above) layer language understanding on top of conventional routing engines; TransitLM is distinctive for trying to replace the routing engine itself with a learned model.

Paper 08 · arXiv (2606.00057)

Data Architectures for AI-Ready Interoperable Public Transportation Ecosystems

Diego Da Silva, Raphael Y. de Camargo, Mayuri A. Morais, Amer Shalaby
This paper surveys how enterprise data-platform architectures must be adapted for public transit's unique constraints, IT/OT convergence, long asset lifecycles, multi-agency coordination, and the inability to run controlled experiments on live systems.
Why It Matters

Every AI/ML transit paper in this digest depends on clean, interoperable data pipelines; this is the unglamorous infrastructure work that determines whether those models can actually be deployed at agencies rather than just published.

Key Insights
  • Transit agencies generate heterogeneous data from AFC, APC, AVL/CAD, GTFS/GTFS-RT feeds, and proprietary platforms
  • Fragmentation and inconsistent update frequencies constrain the value of this data for planning and operations
  • Enterprise data-platform patterns exist for analogous fragmentation problems but remain underexplored in the transit domain
  • Transit systems uniquely combine IT/OT convergence, long asset lifecycles, and rigorous security requirements
  • Live operational systems preclude the controlled experimentation common in other data-platform contexts
Methodology

Conceptual/architectural analysis adapting enterprise data-platform design patterns to the constraints of public transportation data ecosystems.

Results

Identifies the structural barriers (fragmentation, IT/OT convergence, multi-agency governance) that block AI-ready data pipelines in transit agencies and proposes architectural adaptations.

Limitations

Primarily conceptual rather than an implemented and benchmarked system; real-world validation at a specific agency is not yet reported.

Practical Implications

Transit IT departments planning data-platform modernization can use this as an architectural reference before committing to vendor platforms built for other industries.

Who Should Care

Transit agency IT/data leadership, GTFS and open-data maintainers, vendors building transit data platforms

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinct from most ML-focused transit papers in this digest by addressing the infrastructure layer underneath them rather than a specific predictive model.

Paper 09 · arXiv (2605.20263)

The NetMob26 Dataset: A High-Resolution Multi-Source View of Public Bus Mobility in Niterói

Felipe Domingos, Humberto T. Marques-Neto, Bruno Pereira, Clayson Celes, Steffen Knoblauch, Vinícius F. S. Mota
The NetMob26 dataset fuses GPS telemetry, 7.2 million ticketing transactions, and socio-demographic data from one month of bus operations in Niterói, Brazil, to support transit demand and reliability research.
Why It Matters

High-quality, multi-source transit datasets from the Global South are rare; this release lets researchers test demand-forecasting and accessibility methods on a city whose conditions (informal settlements, weather variability) differ sharply from the European/North American cities most benchmarks use.

Key Insights
  • Combines GPS telemetry, ~7.2M ticketing transactions, route/stop/weather data, and socio-demographic layers
  • Covers one month of operational records (March 2026) from Niterói's bus system
  • Anonymization pipeline removes passenger identifiers and operational inconsistencies
  • Access is gated behind an NDA for challenge participants, limiting immediate open reuse
  • Supports demand forecasting, accessibility analysis, service reliability, and weather-impact research
Methodology

Multi-source data fusion and preprocessing pipeline combining bus GPS telemetry, fare-collection transactions, and auxiliary urban/weather data, released as a curated dataset for the NetMob Data Challenge.

Results

Produces a cleaned, anonymized, multi-source dataset of transit supply and passenger demand suitable for multiple downstream research tasks.

Limitations

NDA-gated access restricts use to challenge participants rather than the broader research community; covers only a single month and a single mid-size city.

Practical Implications

Researchers studying Global South transit systems gain a rare high-fidelity testbed, though restricted access limits near-term broader impact.

Who Should Care

Mobility data scientists, Global South transit researchers, NetMob Data Challenge participants

Comparison to Prior Work

Joins a small set of multi-source open transit datasets (mostly from wealthy cities); notable for its Global South focus and fare-transaction granularity.

Paper 10 · arXiv (2605.15817)

Which Moments Matter? Heuristics of Remembered Travel Experience in Public Transport

Esther Bosch, Klas Ihme, Stefan Bohmann
Analyzing 2,576 real-world transit trips across three German cities, this study finds rider satisfaction is best predicted by a 'Minimum-End' heuristic combining the worst moment of a trip and how it ended, not by the trip's average experience.
Why It Matters

Agencies pour resources into average on-time performance, but this research suggests fixing the single worst moment of a journey and improving how trips end would move customer satisfaction scores far more than uniform service improvements.

Key Insights
  • Used smartphone-based experience sampling to collect ratings every five minutes during 2,576 real trips across three German cities
  • Tested mean experience, peak-end, minimum-end, final-moment, and trip-duration heuristics against post-trip satisfaction
  • Minimum-End heuristic (most negative moment + final experience) best predicted retrospective satisfaction
  • Mean-experience, peak-end, final-moment-alone, and duration-based models all performed substantially worse
  • Both negative extremes and trip endings independently shape remembered evaluations, not an overall average
Methodology

Smartphone-based experience sampling collecting on-trip ratings every five minutes plus post-trip evaluations, analyzed with multilevel regression models comparing five competing temporal-aggregation heuristics.

Results

Minimum-End heuristic substantially outperforms mean-experience, peak-end, final-moment-only, and duration-based models in predicting post-trip satisfaction.

Limitations

Sample drawn from three German cities only; cultural and system differences may shift which heuristic dominates elsewhere.

Practical Implications

Agencies should prioritize eliminating worst-moment failures (e.g. a single crowded, hot, or delayed segment) and improving trip endings (arrival cleanliness, wayfinding) over chasing average on-time metrics to move customer satisfaction.

Who Should Care

Customer experience teams at transit agencies, behavioral researchers, marketing/satisfaction survey designers

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends 'peak-end rule' literature from psychology (Kahneman et al.) into the transit domain, and is among the first to test it against a richer set of competing heuristics with high-frequency real-trip data.

Paper 11 · Semantic Scholar / Sustainability (2024)

An Assessment of Accessibility from a Socially Sustainable Urban Mobility Approach in Mass Transit Projects: Contributions from the Northern Central American Triangle

C. Grande-Ayala
A GIS-based comparison of three BRT projects in Guatemala City, San Salvador, and Tegucigalpa finds Guatemala City's Transmetro most improved accessibility equity to clinics and schools, offering a replicable methodology for assessing social sustainability in Global South transit investments.
Why It Matters

Most BRT evaluations focus on speed and cost; this paper builds one of the few replicable frameworks for measuring whether transit investments actually narrow access inequality to essential services in resource-constrained cities.

Key Insights
  • Compares accessibility before/after BRT projects in three Northern Central American Triangle capitals (2000-2020)
  • Uses GIS modeling with transportation network, population, and urban facility layers to measure equity and spatial efficiency
  • Guatemala City's BRT project most improved access to public health clinics and educational facilities
  • Guatemala City narrowed the inequality gap more than the San Salvador and Tegucigalpa projects
  • Methodology is designed to be replicable elsewhere in the Global South despite data scarcity, using open-source GIS tools
Methodology

Comparative GIS-based accessibility analysis using equity and spatial-efficiency indicators across three BRT case studies, built from open-source software given limited regional data availability.

Results

Guatemala City's BRT project delivered the largest accessibility gains and the greatest reduction in inequality of access to clinics and schools relative to the other two NCAT capitals.

Limitations

Single-region case study (Northern Central American Triangle); 20-year before/after window may obscure shorter-term confounders like land-use changes unrelated to the BRT projects.

Practical Implications

Other Global South cities planning BRT can adopt this open-source GIS accessibility methodology to set and track social-sustainability targets, not just ridership or speed targets.

Who Should Care

BRT planners in the Global South, social-sustainability and equity researchers, development-bank transit financing teams

Comparison to Prior Work

Most prior social-sustainability transit literature is qualitative; this is distinctive for quantifying accessibility equity gains with replicable GIS indicators across a multi-city comparison.

MEDIUM CONFIDENCE
Paper 12 · Semantic Scholar / Nature Communications (2023)

Interconnectedness enhances network resilience of multimodal public transportation systems for Safe-to-Fail urban mobility

Zizhen Xu, S. S. Chopra
Network analysis of Hong Kong's public transit system shows that deeper interconnectedness among bus, rail, and ferry modes reduces topological vulnerabilities and increases attack/disruption tolerance, supporting a 'resilience-by-design' rather than 'resilience-by-intervention' strategy.
Why It Matters

With 84 citations since 2023, this is a foundational reference establishing that intermodal connectivity itself, not just individual mode hardening, is what makes transit networks safe-to-fail under climate-driven disruptions.

Key Insights
  • Studies the resilience of Hong Kong's heavily-relied-upon multimodal public transit network
  • Interconnected systems reduce topological vulnerabilities and increase tolerance to targeted disruption ('attack tolerance')
  • Greater interconnectedness improves post-disruption interoperability between modes
  • Integrating the most vulnerable individual systems into the network yields the largest robustness gains
  • Marginal benefits of further increasing intermodal transfer capacity diminish past a certain point
  • Proposes 'resilience-by-design' (structural interconnectedness) as complementary to 'resilience-by-intervention' (post-hoc fixes or new systems)
Methodology

Network-topology analysis of Hong Kong's multimodal public transportation system, modeling resilience via vulnerability, attack tolerance, and post-disruption interoperability metrics.

Results

Increased interconnectedness measurably reduces topological vulnerability and improves attack tolerance and interoperability; targeting the most vulnerable individual systems for integration yields the greatest robustness gains, though intermodal-transfer benefits taper off.

Limitations

Single-city case study (Hong Kong) with an unusually dense, mature multimodal network; results may not transfer directly to car-dependent or single-mode-dominant cities.

Practical Implications

Climate-resilience planning for transit agencies should prioritize structural integration between modes (shared infrastructure, easy transfers) as a baseline strategy, not just hardening individual lines against extreme weather.

Who Should Care

Climate-resilience planners, transit network designers, infrastructure policy researchers

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinct from the dominant 'resilience-by-intervention' literature (post-disruption response, redundancy investment) by quantifying how baseline network topology itself confers resilience.

HIGH CONFIDENCE

Contrarian Insights

Free public transit fare policy may do more for social justice in autonomous mobility-on-demand systems than regulating or capping the AMoD service itself.

Average on-time performance, the metric agencies obsess over, is a weak predictor of rider satisfaction; a single bad moment plus a bad ending dominates the memory of an entire trip.

Congestion pricing's transit ridership gains spread well beyond the tolled zone, while its travel-demand reduction stays geographically concentrated, meaning the costs and benefits of the policy land on different neighborhoods.

Actionable Takeaways

1. Agencies piloting congestion pricing should plan for transit capacity increases beyond the cordon boundary, not just within it, since ridership gains diffuse outward.

2. Customer experience teams should target the single worst moment and trip endings in service design reviews rather than chasing uniform average improvements.

3. Cities planning AMoD/robotaxi regulation should model free or subsidized transit fares as an equity lever before reaching for AMoD price caps or budget limits, which this research shows are far less effective.

4. Transit agencies investing in AI/ML forecasting tools should first invest in the underlying data-architecture problem (fragmented AFC/APC/AVL/GTFS pipelines), since model quality is bottlenecked by data interoperability.

5. Service planners addressing transit deserts can adopt risk-aware, belief-updating sequential planning instead of waiting for ridership data that will never exist without service.

Long-Term Implications

Over the next several years, expect transit planning to bifurcate into two reinforcing layers: a data/AI backend (graph transformers, foundation-model counterfactuals, LLM-trained routing) that gets cheaper and more capable, and an equity/justice layer that increasingly gets encoded directly into optimization objectives rather than bolted on as policy afterthought. The papers on resilience-by-design and multimodal interconnectedness suggest climate adaptation will push agencies toward deeper structural integration between bus, rail, and active modes, while the rider-experience research suggests customer satisfaction metrics will shift away from system-average KPIs toward moment-level and ending-focused design standards.
