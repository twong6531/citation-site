Congestion Pricing: Using Road Tolls to Fund Transit

New York City launched the first central business district congestion pricing program in the United States in 2024, charging drivers entering Manhattan below 60th Street a toll that generates funds dedicated to MTA capital improvements. The program connects two ideas — reducing traffic and funding transit — that have been theorized together for decades.

How Congestion Pricing Works

Congestion pricing charges vehicles a toll to enter a defined zone during peak hours, with tolls varying by time of day, vehicle type, and entry point. The goal is twofold: reduce traffic volumes to a manageable level and generate revenue. London introduced its Central London Congestion Charge in 2003 at £5/day (now £15+); Stockholm launched its system in 2006; Singapore has run Electronic Road Pricing since 1998.

New York's Central Business District tolling program charges most passenger vehicles $9 for peak entries into Manhattan south of 60th Street, with trucks paying higher rates. The program is projected to generate $1 billion annually net of operating costs, dedicated entirely to MTA capital bonding.

Revenue Projections and Transit Bond Capacity

The $1 billion annual revenue stream enables the MTA to issue approximately $15 billion in bonds — using the congestion pricing revenue as bond debt service. This $15 billion funds a portion of the MTA's 2020–2024 and 2025–2029 capital programs, covering Second Avenue Subway Phase 2, signal modernization, and accessibility upgrades.

Bond financing amplifies the revenue's impact: rather than spending $1 billion per year on capital projects over 15 years (which could only fund $15 billion of projects), bonding allows the MTA to commit to a larger upfront capital program and build projects sooner, capturing construction cost savings from speed.

Congestion Reduction Benefits

Congestion pricing generates transit funding while directly improving transit performance: fewer cars in the CBD means buses move faster. London's bus speeds in the congestion zone increased 20–30% in the years following the charge's introduction, reducing bus operating costs and improving reliability. Stockholm's traffic volumes in the charging zone fell 20–25% and have stayed lower for over 15 years.

New York City buses carry about 2.5 million riders daily, mostly in Manhattan and the outer boroughs. Even a 10–15% bus speed improvement from reduced traffic would translate to lower operating costs and better service for millions of riders.

Political Challenges and Equity Concerns

Congestion pricing is politically volatile. New York's program faced over a decade of planning, repeated political near-death experiences, and was briefly suspended by Governor Kathy Hochul in 2024 before being implemented. Opposition comes from outer-borough commuters who drive to work in Manhattan and from suburban politicians.

Equity analysis of New York's program found that the tolls disproportionately affect middle-income commuters who drive from outer boroughs or New Jersey because they lack transit alternatives. Mitigation measures include credits for low-income drivers and residents of the CBD tolling zone.

Key Takeaways
  • New York's CBD tolling charges most passenger vehicles $9 per entry into Manhattan below 60th Street, generating ~$1B annually.
  • London buses in the congestion zone saw speed improvements of 20–30% after the charge's introduction.
  • Stockholm's traffic volumes fell 20–25% after congestion pricing and have remained lower for over 15 years.
  • Bonding against congestion pricing revenue lets the MTA commit to ~$15B in capital projects upfront rather than spending $1B/year.
