On-Time Performance: A Flawed Metric Transit Can't Stop Using

Every transit agency publishes on-time performance statistics, and almost every agency's numbers look better than riders experience. The gap between the official figure and felt reality comes down to how 'on time' is defined — and the definitions are set by the agencies themselves.
Article 21 of 100 June 12, 2026 Transit Encyclopedia

How On-Time Performance Is Calculated

On-time performance (OTP) measures the percentage of trips that arrive within a defined window of their scheduled time. The window varies by agency and mode. New York City Subway historically used a 5-minute window for trains; the MBTA's commuter rail uses a 5-minute threshold for shorter trips and 10 minutes for longer ones. Amtrak uses 15 minutes for short-haul routes.

When an agency reports 75% OTP, it means 75% of measured departures or arrivals fell within that window. What the statistic often omits: it measures the vehicle, not the passenger. If a train runs on time but you missed it because the previous train was cancelled, your experience is not counted.

The Terminal Bias Problem

Many agencies measure on-time performance at terminal or endpoint stations, not at intermediate stops. A bus that leaves the downtown terminal on schedule but runs late through the congested middle of its route can still count as 'on time' if it arrives at the far terminal within the window — having skipped stops to recover schedule.

This creates a perverse incentive: operators can 'run hot' (skip stops or pass waiting passengers) to stay on schedule, harming riders at intermediate stops while boosting the agency's numbers. Chicago Transit Authority and SFMTA have faced rider complaints about exactly this practice on high-frequency routes.

What Riders Actually Care About

Reliability research consistently shows riders prioritize predictability over raw speed. A bus that always arrives 3 minutes late is experienced as more reliable than one that arrives anywhere from 1 minute early to 8 minutes late, even if the average is better. Variance — not just the mean — determines perceived reliability.

Some agencies have adopted excess journey time or wait time assessment as complementary metrics. Transport for London measures 'excess wait time' — the difference between the expected wait based on frequency and the actual measured wait. This captures bunching and gaps that OTP misses.

Better Metrics Emerging

The proliferation of AVL (automatic vehicle location) data and real-time apps has enabled richer performance measurement. San Francisco's SFMTA now publishes a 'gapping' metric tracking how often bunching occurs. Sound Transit measures schedule adherence at every stop on every trip, not just endpoints.

Customer satisfaction surveys offer a complementary angle. New York City Transit's monthly surveys found that perceived reliability dropped in 2017–2018 even as official OTP held steady — a signal that the metric was hiding real deterioration. The agency subsequently adopted an additional metric: the percentage of customers waiting more than twice the scheduled headway.

Key Takeaways
  • Most agencies define 'on time' with a 5–15 minute window, which can mask significant lateness.
  • Measuring OTP only at terminals hides delays that accumulate mid-route at intermediate stops.
  • Riders experience variance as unreliability — a predictably late bus feels more reliable than an unpredictably erratic one.
  • Excess wait time and customer surveys are emerging as better complements to traditional OTP metrics.
