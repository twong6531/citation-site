Executive Summary

Today's cluster pairs a hard look at car dependency itself with a series of papers questioning whether the data and models underlying transit research are as solid as they appear. A new Car Dependency Index, validated across 18 European and North American cities, finds that car dependency persists even after controlling for income — and that a simulated metro expansion in Rome would cut roughly 60,000 commuting vehicles but only in a localized way, reinforcing that piecemeal transit investment struggles to dislodge car-centric travel habits. Alongside it, three papers interrogate the foundations of mobility research: GPS-based mobility data shows source-specific, spatially structured coverage bias in Mexico; a new benchmark exposes inconsistent evaluation protocols across vehicle-trajectory generation models; and a Sweden/US study finds that transit's celebrated role in social mixing holds mainly in large metros, fading to insignificance in smaller cities. A second cluster pushes equity directly into algorithmic design — fare-free bus simulations in NYC, fairness-aware car-sharing network design in Vienna, and multi-agent reinforcement learning for transit subsidy and ride-share pricing all bake equity constraints into the optimization itself rather than measuring inequity after the fact.

Emerging Themes

The data underneath transit research is getting its own audit: Papers on GPS mobility-data bias, trajectory-generation benchmarking, and compositional mobility clustering all interrogate whether the inputs to transit models — not just the models themselves — are trustworthy.

Equity is moving from a metric to a constraint: Fare-free bus simulation, fairness-aware car-sharing network design, and multi-agent RL pricing for shared mobility all encode equity directly into the optimization objective rather than evaluating it as an afterthought.

Transit's social and environmental externalities are getting first-class measurement: New work quantifies how transit accessibility shapes social mixing, how ridership tracks fine-particulate air quality, and how isolated rail investment fails to dent car dependency at scale.

Paper 01 · Paper of the Day · arXiv (2604.01019)

Car Dependency in Urban Accessibility

Bruno Campanelli, Francesco Marzolla, Matteo Bruno, Hygor Piaget Monteiro Melo, Vittorio Loreto
A new Car Dependency Index quantifies the accessibility gap between private and public transport across 18 European and North American cities, and a Rome metro-expansion simulation shows isolated transit investments yield only localized reductions in driving.
Why It Matters

It gives planners a standardized, comparable metric for "how trapped is this city by cars" instead of relying on car-ownership rates alone, and its Rome case study delivers a sobering, quantified warning against expecting single-line transit projects to solve systemic car dependency.

Key Insights
  • The Car Dependency Index (CDI) quantifies the accessibility gap between private and public transport at fine spatial resolution across 18 cities.
  • Car dependency remains a primary driver of car ownership even after controlling for household income.
  • Spatial inequalities in car dependency are pronounced within cities, not just between them.
  • A "what-if" simulation of Rome's planned Metro C expansion predicts roughly 60,000 fewer commuting vehicles.
  • The same simulation shows the effect is localized: isolated rail extensions have limited city-wide impact.
  • The authors argue systemic, network-level transit expansion is necessary to meaningfully dismantle car-based travel systems.
Methodology

High-resolution geospatial accessibility modeling comparing private-vehicle and public-transport travel times across 18 cities, combined with a counterfactual simulation of Rome's planned metro extension.

Results

CDI reveals persistent, income-independent car dependency and pronounced intra-city spatial inequality; the Rome simulation predicts a meaningful but geographically limited reduction in commuting vehicles from a single metro extension.

Limitations

Limited to 18 cities in Europe and North America, all wealthy-country contexts; the Rome "what-if" simulation is a single case study and may not generalize to cities with different urban form or transit baselines.

Practical Implications

City planners and transit agencies seeking political buy-in for car-free zones or transit expansion can use the CDI as an objective targeting tool, but should pair single-line projects with network-level investment to avoid disappointing mode-shift results.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, transit capital-investment program officers, climate/net-zero policy teams, transportation economists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond car-ownership statistics and simple accessibility ratios used in prior research by combining a standardized cross-city index with an explicit policy counterfactual simulation.

Paper 02 · arXiv (2606.21897)

Simulating Public Transit Fare Policies in NYC: An Efficient, Socioeconomic-Aware Framework

Parker Wischhover, Hossein Amiri, Kiara Ha, Jooyoung Yoo, Lina Li, Andreas Züfle, Joon-Seok Kim
A scalable agent-based simulation of NYC fare policy finds that fare-free bus programs substantially benefit lower-income riders by increasing bus use and cutting fare burden, with modest trade-offs in revenue.
Why It Matters

Fare policy debates are usually driven by revenue projections alone; this gives agencies a socioeconomic-aware simulation tool to weigh equity gains against revenue trade-offs before committing politically and financially to a fare change.

Key Insights
  • Combines a synthetic population, agent-based simulation, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling.
  • Pricing changes modestly affect total ridership but significantly alter modal composition.
  • Impacts of fare changes are heterogeneous across income groups, not uniform city-wide.
  • Fare-free bus policy substantially increases bus usage and reduces fare burden for lower-income riders.
  • A sampling-based approach reduces computational cost while preserving city-scale aggregate accuracy.
Methodology

Agent-based simulation with a synthetic NYC population, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling, evaluated under multiple fare scenarios including fare-free buses and distance-based pricing.

Results

Fare-free bus policy delivers the largest equity benefit among scenarios tested, with modest revenue trade-offs; distance-based pricing and fare increases reshape mode share more than total ridership.

Limitations

Synthetic population and mode-choice assumptions may not capture all behavioral nuance; calibrated specifically to NYC, which has unusually dense, multimodal transit options not present in most US cities.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies considering fare-free bus pilots can use this framework's sampling-based simulation approach to model city-scale equity and revenue trade-offs before deployment.

Who Should Care

Transit agency fare-policy teams, city budget offices, transportation equity advocates.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior fare-elasticity studies, which typically estimate aggregate ridership effects, by explicitly modeling socioeconomic heterogeneity in mode-choice response.

Paper 03 · arXiv (2606.23616)

One country, multiple portraits: representativeness in GPS-based mobility data is source-specific and spatially dependent

Carmen Cabrera, Francisco Rowe, Miguel González-Leonardo, Juan Ignacio Vilchis-García, Elisa Omodei, Maribel Hernández-Rosales
Comparing Facebook and multi-app GPS mobility data against Mexico's census across 2,478 municipalities, the authors find coverage bias differs sharply by data source and is spatially clustered rather than random.
Why It Matters

A huge share of transit and urban-mobility research outside wealthy countries leans on commercial GPS mobility data as a stand-in for ground truth; this shows that choice of vendor alone can systematically distort who and where gets represented.

Key Insights
  • Facebook-based mobility data provides higher and more evenly distributed coverage than a multi-app aggregator (Veraset).
  • The multi-app aggregator concentrates users in larger, wealthier, more digitally connected places.
  • Coverage bias is spatially structured: neighboring municipalities show similar over- or under-coverage.
  • Explainable ML shows digital access and material resources drive bias in multi-app data, while demographic structure drives bias in Facebook data.
  • A substantial share of spatial variation in coverage bias remains unexplained by observed covariates.
Methodology

Comparison of population estimates from Facebook and Veraset GPS-based mobility data against the 2020 Mexican Census across 2,478 municipalities, using explainable machine learning and spatial dependence modeling.

Results

Coverage bias magnitude and spatial pattern differ substantially by data source, with both digital-access and demographic factors driving disparities, and meaningful unexplained spatial variance remaining.

Limitations

Single-country (Mexico) validation; only two commercial data sources compared; explanatory covariates available may not capture all drivers of bias.

Practical Implications

Researchers and agencies in data-scarce regions using any single GPS mobility data vendor for transit planning should explicitly test and correct for source-specific coverage bias rather than treating the data as ground truth.

Who Should Care

Mobility data scientists, disaster-response and public-health planners using mobile data, transit researchers in the Global South.

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior mobility-data validation work largely focused on single-source bias in high-income countries; this is among the first systematic multi-source comparison in a large data-scarce country.

Paper 04 · arXiv (2604.14348)

Where diverse populations gather: Transit accessibility and the spatial structure of social mixing

Yuan Liao
Using mobile phone GPS data across nine Swedish cities and three US cities, the study finds transit accessibility predicts visitor diversity at venues, but the relationship holds robustly only in the largest metro areas.
Why It Matters

Transit's role as a social equalizer is frequently invoked in large-city contexts (NYC, Stockholm); this shows the effect attenuates to insignificance in smaller cities once geography is controlled for, complicating blanket equity claims used to justify transit investment everywhere.

Key Insights
  • Transit catchment diversity positively predicts visitor diversity at points of interest in large metros.
  • In smaller Swedish cities, this association attenuates to insignificance once catchment composition, centrality, and venue density are controlled.
  • Transit-diversity hotspots concentrate in lower-diversity, lower-commercial-density venues, not already-diverse ones.
  • In US cities, hotspots are farther from transit; in Sweden, they are more central — a notable cross-national contrast.
  • Findings are consistent with transit playing a "bridging" role specifically where alternative access pathways are limited.
Methodology

2024 mobile phone GPS data analysis computing visitor diversity indices based on visitors' home-neighborhood birth-background composition, across nine Swedish cities and three US cities (New York, Washington DC, Atlanta).

Results

Transit-diversity association is robust mainly in large metropolitan areas and concentrates in lower-diversity, lower-commercial venues, with directionally different patterns between Swedish and US cities.

Limitations

Diversity measured only via birth-background composition, not full socioeconomic diversity; limited to Sweden and three specific US cities; mobile GPS data carries its own representativeness caveats (see Paper 03).

Practical Implications

Mid-size cities should not assume transit investment will automatically boost social mixing the way it appears to in large metros; targeted interventions at low-diversity, transit-accessible venues may be more effective than blanket transit expansion.

Who Should Care

Urban sociologists, transit equity planners, city governments outside major metro areas.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends segregation research, which has focused on residential and activity-space segregation, into the underexplored question of how transit infrastructure shapes social mixing at specific venues.

Paper 05 · arXiv (2606.23257)

Dynamic multi-agent deep reinforcement learning-based pricing and incentivization approach in multimodal transportation networks

Khadidja Kadem, Mostafa Ameli, Carlos Lima Azevedo, Mahdi Zargayouna, Latifa Oukhellou
Two competing reinforcement-learning agents — a public authority allocating transit incentives and a shared-mobility provider setting dynamic fares — jointly cut commuter costs ~20% and emissions ~10% while nearly doubling public transport profit.
Why It Matters

As ride-hail and micromobility services compete with public transit for the same riders, this offers a concrete decision-support mechanism for coordinating subsidy and pricing rather than letting market forces concentrate shared mobility in already-dense, already-served areas.

Key Insights
  • Shared mobility demand concentrates in high-density areas, limiting accessibility for other commuter groups.
  • A public-authority RL agent allocates spatio-temporal transit incentives to improve equity, emissions, and efficiency.
  • A shared-mobility-provider RL agent dynamically adjusts fares to maximize revenue.
  • Dynamic incentivization alone reduces congestion peaks, cuts commuter costs ~20%, and cuts emissions ~10%.
  • Combining incentivization with dynamic shared-mobility pricing nearly doubles public transport profit.
  • The two agents demonstrably balance conflicting public/private objectives rather than one dominating the other.
Methodology

Multi-agent deep reinforcement learning framework with two interacting RL agents (public authority and shared-mobility provider), evaluated via numerical experiments over a three-hour morning peak period.

Results

~20% reduction in commuter costs, ~10% reduction in emissions, near-doubling of public transit profit, and more equitable benefit distribution when incentivization and dynamic pricing are combined.

Limitations

Evaluated only on a simulated three-hour morning-peak scenario; real-world deployment would require coordination across independent private operators with their own incentives to participate.

Practical Implications

City transportation departments regulating ride-hail/micromobility alongside public transit could pilot RL-based dynamic incentive structures rather than static subsidy formulas.

Who Should Care

City DOTs, transit agency planning teams, shared-mobility regulators, transportation economists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Builds on single-agent dynamic-pricing literature by explicitly modeling the multi-stakeholder, multi-objective conflict between public authorities, private operators, and travelers.

Paper 06 · arXiv (2604.11732)

Fairness-aware Strategic Design of Station-based Electric Car-Sharing Systems

Jue Zhou, Zoha Sherkat-Masoumi, Merve Bodur
A Vienna-based bi-objective optimization framework jointly designs station locations, charger capacity, and fleet size for electric car-sharing while explicitly trading off revenue against service-rate equity across user groups.
Why It Matters

Most car-sharing system design optimizes purely for profitability, which tends to concentrate service in already-affluent neighborhoods; this gives operators and cities a concrete tool to quantify and negotiate the cost of fairness rather than leaving it to ad hoc mandates.

Key Insights
  • Operationalizes fairness via two paradigms: service-rate disparity and max-min fairness, measured by realized group service rates rather than static accessibility.
  • Uses a multi-day representative-demand setting to capture demand heterogeneity over time.
  • Develops a bi-objective trajectory-based formulation jointly optimizing revenue and service equity.
  • A branch-and-price algorithm solves single-objective variants, embedded in an exact bi-objective procedure to generate the Pareto frontier.
  • A diving-heuristic approach approximates the Pareto frontier for larger, computationally intractable instances.
  • A Vienna case study quantifies concrete trade-offs between revenue, equity, and system design choices.
Methodology

Bi-objective trajectory-based mixed-integer optimization solved via branch-and-price for exact Pareto frontiers and a diving heuristic for larger instances; validated on a real Vienna-based case study.

Results

Produces explicit Pareto frontiers and high-quality approximations showing the quantitative trade-off between profit and equitable service rates across station-based car-sharing designs.

Limitations

Single-city validation (Vienna); branch-and-price exact methods may not scale to very large station networks without the heuristic approximation; fairness defined narrowly via service-rate metrics rather than broader access dimensions.

Practical Implications

Cities negotiating car-sharing equity mandates or service-area requirements with private operators can use the Pareto frontier output to set defensible, quantified minimum service-equity targets.

Who Should Care

Car-sharing and micromobility operators, city mobility-equity regulators, operations research practitioners in shared mobility.

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinguishes itself from prior integrated strategic-operational car-sharing design models, which prioritized economic efficiency and treated equity as a static spatial accessibility afterthought.

Paper 07 · arXiv (2606.21022)

Structure-Aware Graph Multi-Task Learning for Dynamic Sparse OD Demand Prediction

Ming Xu, Jiawei Cao
SAGMTL decomposes origin-destination demand prediction into separately modeling whether a route is active and how much demand it carries once active, improving prediction on sparse, long-tailed real-world OD flows in Beijing, Chengdu, and Nanjing.
Why It Matters

Most OD flows between any two points in a city are zero or near-zero most of the time; treating this as one regression task (as most prior models do) wastes model capacity and degrades accuracy precisely on the rare, intermittent trips that matter for planning new service.

Key Insights
  • Real-world OD flows are dynamically sparse, long-tailed, and have heterogeneous zero-flow patterns.
  • SAGMTL decomposes prediction into structural-state modeling (is this connection active) and flow-intensity estimation (how much demand).
  • A node-edge collaborative representation module jointly captures regional semantics, temporal dynamics, and spatial priors.
  • A multi-constraint objective improves both sparsity awareness and structural consistency.
  • Outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across three real-world urban mobility datasets (Beijing, Chengdu, Nanjing).
Methodology

Structure-Aware Graph Multi-Task Learning framework jointly modeling regional activity states, OD connection activity, and edge-level flow intensity, evaluated against state-of-the-art baselines on three Chinese city datasets.

Results

Superior overall performance versus baselines, with explicit ablation showing that modeling regional activity, connection states, and flow intensity separately improves robustness on sparse OD prediction.

Limitations

Validated only on three Chinese cities with dense data-collection infrastructure; may require adaptation for cities with sparser automatic data collection (AVL/APC/smart-card coverage).

Practical Implications

Agencies forecasting demand for new or low-frequency routes could benefit from structure-aware models that separate "will anyone ride this" from "how many will ride this" rather than a single blended prediction.

Who Should Care

Transit demand-forecasting analysts, transportation ML researchers, agencies planning service into low-demand corridors.

Comparison to Prior Work

Improves on prior OD-prediction approaches that treat the task as a single flow-regression problem, which struggles with low-frequency, intermittent, long-tailed OD interactions.

Paper 08 · arXiv (2605.08622)

Networks of amenities reveal universal homophily and heterophily across global cities

Jianrui Wu, Baiyue He, Alec Kirkley
A Bayesian framework quantifying how amenities cluster or mix across roughly 800 global cities finds universal spatial scales of agglomeration and co-agglomeration, with heterophilic amenity mixing concentrated at walkable spatial scales.
Why It Matters

Walkability and mixed-use zoning are central to transit-oriented development debates; this provides the first cross-city quantitative evidence that beneficial amenity-mixing patterns are universal at walkable scales rather than city-specific quirks.

Key Insights
  • A novel Bayesian framework nonparametrically quantifies the spectrum of amenity mixing patterns across ~800 cities worldwide.
  • Identifies universal spatial scales of homophily (agglomeration) and heterophily (co-agglomeration) among amenity types.
  • Changes in heterophilic mixing more effectively predict neighborhood rental-value changes than amenity diversity alone, per a longitudinal case study.
  • Agglomeration economies depend largely on amenity type, not city-specific or regional context.
  • Heterophilic amenity mixing concentrates at walkable spatial scales, directly tying the finding to TOD-relevant urban design.
Methodology

Nonparametric Bayesian framework for quantifying amenity mixing patterns applied across roughly 800 global cities, paired with a longitudinal case study linking mixing changes to rental-value changes.

Results

Universal, amenity-type-dependent spatial regularities in clustering/mixing, with heterophilic mixing at walkable scales outperforming raw amenity diversity as a predictor of rental-value change.

Limitations

Cross-sectional global analysis with a single longitudinal case study; causality between amenity mixing and rental values is suggestive rather than established; "amenity type" categories may vary in granularity across different city data sources.

Practical Implications

TOD and zoning planners can use the identified universal walkable-scale mixing patterns as design targets, with more confidence that they'll generalize across city contexts rather than being specific to a handful of well-studied cities.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, TOD and zoning policy teams, real-estate economists, walkability researchers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond city-specific agglomeration-economics case studies to a unified, ~800-city nonparametric framework revealing cross-context universality.

Paper 09 · arXiv (2606.02287)

CityTrajBench: A Unified Benchmark for City-Scale Vehicle Trajectory Generation

Shibo Zhu, Xiaodan Shi, Dayin Chen, Yuntian Chen, Haoran Zhang, Tianhao Wu, Jinyue Yan
CityTrajBench standardizes data, preprocessing, and evaluation across statistical, VAE, GAN, diffusion, and flow-matching trajectory-generation models, finding no single model family dominates across all realism and efficiency criteria.
Why It Matters

Synthetic trajectory generation underpins transportation simulation and privacy-preserving mobility research; without a shared benchmark, reported performance gains across papers may reflect inconsistent evaluation protocols rather than real model improvements.

Key Insights
  • Standardizes data ingestion, normalization, feature construction, model adaptation, and multi-level evaluation across trajectory-generation methods.
  • Supports statistical, VAE-based, GAN-based, diffusion-based, and flow-matching-based generators under one protocol.
  • DiffTraj is strongest on trajectory-level geometric fidelity; DiffRNTraj is competitive on structure-sensitive global realism.
  • TrajFlow offers the best overall balance of realism, conditional consistency, and efficiency.
  • A simple Markov baseline remains competitive on coarse-grained trip and local-movement statistics, undercutting claims that complex generative models are always necessary.
Methodology

Unified benchmark protocol standardizing preprocessing and evaluation, applied to heterogeneous generative model families across three real-world urban vehicle-trajectory datasets, measuring realism, fidelity, conditional consistency, and efficiency.

Results

Clear trade-offs across model families with no single dominant model; a simple Markov baseline remains competitive on certain coarse-grained metrics, highlighting that trajectory generation quality is inherently multi-objective.

Limitations

Limited to three real-world datasets; "realism" and "fidelity" metrics, while standardized, still involve some subjective weighting choices; doesn't address privacy-utility trade-offs explicitly.

Practical Implications

Researchers and agencies generating synthetic trajectory data for simulation or privacy-preserving release should select model families based on which specific fidelity dimension matters most for their use case, rather than defaulting to the newest architecture.

Who Should Care

Transportation simulation researchers, mobility-data privacy engineers, urban-planning ML practitioners.

Comparison to Prior Work

Addresses fragmentation in prior trajectory-generation literature, where inconsistent datasets and metrics made it unclear whether reported gains came from genuine model improvements or experimental protocol differences.

Paper 10 · arXiv (2606.18044)

Model-based clustering of compositional trajectories for the analysis of mobility data

Andrea Panarotto, Manuela Cattelan, Ruggero Bellio
A new statistical framework represents individual mobility as compositional trajectories over road-type proportions, explicitly modeling telephonic location uncertainty, then clusters Padova-area residents into interpretable population-level mobility patterns.
Why It Matters

Phone-based location data is noisy and uncertain, but most clustering approaches ignore that uncertainty; this gives planners a statistically principled way to extract trustworthy mobility patterns from imperfect telephonic data rather than over-trusting point-location estimates.

Key Insights
  • Represents mobility as compositional trajectories encoding proportions of compatible road types at each time point, explicitly modeling device-location uncertainty.
  • Develops a state-space framework for compositional time series capturing both measurement error and latent mobility dynamics.
  • Uses mixtures of state-space models for model-based clustering of trajectories with similar evolution.
  • Aggregates individual movements into interpretable population-level mobility patterns.
  • Applied to a real case study of Padova, Italy and its surrounding municipality.
Methodology

Compositional time-series state-space modeling combined with mixture-model-based clustering, applied to telephonic mobility data from the Padova metropolitan area.

Results

Successfully uncovers interpretable, population-level mobility behaviors while explicitly accounting for telephonic measurement uncertainty, providing policy-relevant clustering output.

Limitations

Single-region case study (Padova); compositional road-type representation may lose information relative to richer trajectory representations; computational complexity of mixture state-space models may limit scalability to larger regions.

Practical Implications

Regional planning agencies relying on telephonic mobility data for travel-demand studies can adopt this uncertainty-aware clustering approach instead of treating noisy location pings as precise ground truth.

Who Should Care

Mobility data statisticians, regional transportation planning agencies, telecom-data mobility researchers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Differs from standard trajectory-clustering methods by explicitly modeling measurement uncertainty inherent to telephonic location data rather than treating positions as exact.

Paper 11 · Smart and Resilient Transportation (2026)

Federated learning driven public transit optimization: a scalable approach for urban mobility in Addis Ababa

N. D. Pritom
A federated-learning framework trained across three GTFS-providing agencies in Addis Ababa — without sharing raw data — cuts travel time 25%, operational costs 20%, and city-wide congestion indices 22.4% while boosting ridership 30%.
Why It Matters

Fragmented, privacy-sensitive transit data across multiple agencies is a common barrier to coordinated optimization in rapidly growing cities; this shows federated learning can deliver centralized-style gains without forcing agencies to pool raw data.

Key Insights
  • Trains LSTM models locally on each agency's GTFS data for arrival-time prediction, aggregating updates via a central server without sharing raw data.
  • Combines federated LSTM arrival prediction with reinforcement learning for route optimization.
  • Delivers a 25% reduction in travel time and a 20% reduction in operational costs.
  • Increases ridership by 30%, attributed to improved service reliability.
  • Outperforms traditional centralized approaches, improving congestion-prediction accuracy by 12.5% and cutting city-wide congestion indices by 22.4%.
  • Modular architecture is designed for scalability to other data-fragmented developing-city contexts.
Methodology

Federated learning framework integrating LSTM-based arrival-time prediction and reinforcement-learning-based route optimization, trained across three transit agencies' GTFS data in Addis Ababa without centralizing raw data.

Results

Outperforms centralized baseline approaches across travel time, operational cost, ridership, and congestion-prediction accuracy metrics.

Limitations

Single-city validation (Addis Ababa); federated learning introduces communication-delay and data-variability challenges across agencies that the paper acknowledges but doesn't fully resolve; results are self-reported by a single-author study.

Practical Implications

Rapidly growing cities with multiple, data-siloed transit agencies could adopt federated learning as a privacy-preserving path to coordinated optimization without requiring data-sharing agreements.

Who Should Care

Transit agencies in rapidly urbanizing developing-world cities, smart-city technology vendors, privacy-conscious municipal data offices.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends federated-learning applications, previously concentrated in healthcare and finance, into multi-agency public transit optimization — an underexplored application domain.

Paper 12 · IEEE Conf. on Technologies for Sustainability (2026)

Critical Transit Infrastructure in Smart Cities and Urban Air Quality: A Multi-City Seasonal Comparison of Ridership and PM2.5

Sean Elliott, Sohini Roy
A multi-source monitoring dataset pairing transit ridership with EPA PM2.5 data across NYC, Chicago, Las Vegas, and Phoenix finds mobility-air-quality relationships are not uniform across cities or seasons, and are strongly shaped by baseline city effects.
Why It Matters

Transit advocacy often assumes a clean, universal "more ridership equals less pollution" relationship; this shows the real relationship is city-specific and seasonally variable, complicating simple air-quality arguments for transit investment.

Key Insights
  • Harmonizes heterogeneous ridership feeds (daily and stop-level) into monthly system totals paired with monthly mean PM2.5 across four metro areas.
  • Reports both absolute and per-capita ridership/PM2.5 metrics to enable cross-city comparability.
  • Finds pronounced structural differences in transit scale and intensity across the four cities.
  • Consistent seasonal shifts occur in both ridership and PM2.5, but the relationship between them varies by urban context.
  • Lightweight regression sensitivity analysis shows apparent mobility-PM2.5 relationships are strongly shaped by baseline city effects rather than a universal pattern.
Methodology

Multi-source monitoring dataset integrating agency-reported transit ridership with U.S. EPA Air Quality System PM2.5 data across two seasonal snapshots (March and October 2024) in four US metro areas, analyzed with descriptive regression sensitivity analysis.

Results

No uniform mobility-air-quality relationship emerges across cities or seasons; baseline city effects dominate over any consistent ridership-PM2.5 association.

Limitations

Only four US cities and two seasonal snapshots; descriptive regression methodology cannot establish causal mobility-air-quality links; PM2.5 has many non-transit sources not controlled for.

Practical Implications

Smart-city sustainability dashboards combining ridership and air-quality data should avoid implying a universal causal link and instead present city-specific, season-aware framing.

Who Should Care

Smart-city sustainability analysts, public-health researchers studying transportation exposure, transit agencies making air-quality-based advocacy claims.

Comparison to Prior Work

Pushes back against simplified mobility-emissions narratives in prior smart-city literature by operationalizing a reproducible, multi-city, multi-season comparison framework.

Contrarian Insights

Today's headline finding cuts against incremental transit politics: a single, dramatic metro extension in Rome would only modestly and locally reduce car dependency, suggesting that the popular strategy of building one flagship line at a time may be structurally incapable of dislodging car-centric travel without simultaneous network-level investment.

Many transit and mobility studies in data-scarce regions quietly assume a single GPS data vendor represents "the population" — but today's Mexico study shows Facebook and a multi-app aggregator paint substantially different, inconsistently biased pictures of the same municipalities, meaning vendor choice alone could be driving published findings as much as the underlying mobility patterns themselves.

Transit's celebrated role in fostering social mixing — a frequent equity argument for expansion — loses statistical significance in smaller Swedish cities once geography is properly controlled for, suggesting big-city research on transit's social benefits may not transfer to the mid-size cities that most often cite it as justification.

Actionable Takeaways

Cities pursuing single flagship rail extensions should pair them with network-level multimodal investment; the Car Dependency Index study's Rome simulation suggests isolated lines underdeliver on systemic car-dependency reduction.

Researchers and agencies relying on commercial GPS mobility data in data-scarce regions should explicitly test for vendor-specific coverage bias rather than treating any single data source as ground truth.

Agencies piloting fare-free bus programs can cite the NYC simulation's finding that fare-free buses meaningfully help lower-income riders with only modest revenue trade-offs, useful for budget negotiations.

Mid-size cities should not assume transit investment automatically increases social mixing as it appears to in large metros; targeted interventions at specific low-diversity, transit-accessible venues may work better than blanket expansion.

Smart-city dashboards pairing ridership with air-quality metrics should avoid implying a universal causal relationship; the four-city PM2.5 study shows baseline city effects dominate over any consistent mobility-pollution pattern.

Long-Term Implications

As transit research matures, expect growing scrutiny of the data and models that underpin it — today's papers on GPS mobility-data bias, trajectory-generation benchmarking inconsistency, and uncertainty-aware compositional clustering all signal that "is our data trustworthy" is becoming as central a question as "what does the data show." At the same time, equity is steadily migrating from a post-hoc evaluation criterion into a first-class constraint inside optimization frameworks for fares, car-sharing networks, and shared-mobility pricing, likely setting a template for how FTA and EU transit-funding applications justify capital requests in coming years. The Car Dependency Index findings are a useful corrective for transit advocates: as cities pursue net-zero commitments, single high-visibility rail projects will likely continue to underdeliver on car-dependency reduction unless paired with systemic, network-level transit and land-use change — a lesson that applies as much to U.S. light-rail extensions as to Rome's archaeology-constrained Metro C.
