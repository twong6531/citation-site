Transit Funding: Where the Money Comes From

Public transit in the United States is funded through an unusually complex layering of federal, state, and local sources — none of which alone comes close to covering the full cost of running a system. Understanding how these streams interact explains why agencies face perpetual budget pressure and why service quality varies so dramatically across cities.

Federal Funding Streams

The federal government funds transit primarily through the Federal Transit Administration (FTA), which distributes money through several formula and competitive grant programs. The largest is the Section 5307 Urbanized Area Formula Grant program, which distributed about $5.7 billion in FY2023 to urban transit agencies based on population and service statistics. Agencies can use these funds for capital purchases (buses, railcars, station rehabilitation) but not operating expenses — a statutory restriction that has shaped how agencies structure their budgets.

The Capital Investment Grant (CIG) program, also called 'New Starts,' funds major new rail lines and BRT projects on a competitive basis, with the federal share typically covering 40–60% of project cost. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 added significant new transit funding, including dedicated electric bus procurement grants.

State Funding Contributions

State contributions to transit vary enormously. California provides about $1 billion annually to transit through multiple programs including the State Transportation Improvement Program and cap-and-trade revenue. New York State contributes billions to the MTA through dedicated tax revenues including a payroll mobility tax that generates roughly $2 billion per year.

Many states provide nothing: transit agencies in Texas, Georgia, and Florida operate almost entirely on local and federal funding, with minimal state contribution. This disparity reflects political culture as much as fiscal capacity — states with stronger transit constituencies tend to fund more generously.

Local Funding: Taxes and Fares

Local funding — the layer transit agencies most directly control — comes from fare revenue and local taxes. Fare revenue typically covers 20–40% of operating costs for U.S. transit agencies, a ratio called the farebox recovery ratio. The remainder comes from local taxes: dedicated sales taxes (common in California, Colorado, and Georgia), property taxes, and general fund appropriations.

The sales tax model has become increasingly prevalent since the 1980s because it bypasses the political difficulty of raising property taxes. Los Angeles County voters have approved multiple half-cent sales tax measures that together generate over $2.5 billion annually for Metro.

Structural Tensions in the Funding Model

The federal restriction on using formula funds for operations forces agencies to fund daily bus and rail operations entirely from local and fare revenue — even as federal capital grants enable construction of expensive new infrastructure that generates new operating costs. This dynamic has been called a 'capital bias' in the federal funding model.

The result: agencies often build new lines they can barely afford to operate, while existing service deteriorates from deferred maintenance. The COVID-19 pandemic emergency relief packages ($69 billion total from 2020–2022) temporarily stabilized agency finances, but most of that money expires by 2025–2026, setting up a cliff for agencies that used it to avoid service cuts rather than structural reform.

Key Takeaways
  • Federal Section 5307 grants ($5.7B in FY2023) fund capital but not operating expenses — a structural constraint shaping agency budgets.
  • State contributions range from billions (California, New York) to nearly zero (Texas, Florida), reflecting political priorities more than fiscal capacity.
  • Sales tax measures generate over $2.5B annually for LA Metro and have become the dominant local funding mechanism.
  • Federal restrictions on using capital grants for operations create a 'capital bias' that encourages building new lines over maintaining existing ones.
