What Makes BRT Different From a Regular Bus

Bus rapid transit promises subway-like reliability at a fraction of the cost. When it delivers on that promise, it moves more people faster and more predictably than any regular bus route. When it doesn't, it's just a bus with a fancy name.

The Core Elements of Real BRT

Genuine bus rapid transit combines several elements that together change how the vehicle moves through a city. Dedicated lanes — physically separated from general traffic — prevent buses from getting stuck behind cars at red lights or in congestion. Level boarding, where the bus floor matches the platform height, eliminates the step-up delay. Off-board fare payment, where riders pay before boarding, removes the bottleneck of each person swiping a card at the door.

When all of these elements work together, a BRT bus can maintain speeds of 15–20 mph in urban corridors where regular buses average 7–10 mph. The Bogotá TransMilenio, launched in 2000, demonstrated this on a grand scale: the system now carries over 2 million riders daily at average speeds that rival many rail systems.

Where American BRT Often Falls Short

The Institute for Transportation and Development Policy publishes a BRT Scorecard rating systems worldwide. Of the dozens of U.S. systems marketed as BRT, very few receive a gold or silver rating. The Cleveland HealthLine and the Eugene EmX are among the exceptions; most U.S. 'BRT' systems lack dedicated lanes, accept payment onboard, and run in mixed traffic.

Agencies frequently apply the BRT label to routes that have upgraded bus stops, real-time arrival signs, and new vehicles — improvements that help but don't change the fundamental speed equation. Riders still wait in the same traffic. The label becomes marketing rather than a meaningful service distinction.

Cost and Construction Compared to Rail

A kilometer of BRT infrastructure in a dedicated median costs roughly $2–10 million to build, compared to $50–200 million for light rail and $200–1,000 million for heavy rail subway. This cost gap makes BRT compelling for cities that need to expand coverage quickly or lack the political capital for a rail project.

The operating costs per passenger are also lower for BRT than rail in most corridors below a certain ridership threshold. Above that threshold — typically around 15,000–20,000 passengers per hour per direction — rail's higher capacity becomes more cost-efficient. BRT systems can handle up to 35,000 passengers per hour with platooned vehicles on exclusive lanes, but most operate well below that ceiling.

When BRT Makes Sense

BRT works best in dense urban corridors where the right-of-way already exists (a wide arterial street with a center median), where rail would be prohibitively expensive, or as an interim step toward eventual rail conversion. Los Angeles has converted several BRT corridors to light rail as ridership grew — using BRT to build the ridership case and political support for a larger investment.

In developing cities with limited capital budgets, BRT on dedicated lanes can transform mobility faster than any rail project. Curitiba, Brazil pioneered this approach in the 1970s; the city's tube stations, level boarding, and dedicated lanes remain a reference point for what disciplined BRT design can accomplish.

Key Takeaways
  • True BRT requires dedicated lanes, level boarding, and off-board fare payment — most U.S. 'BRT' systems lack at least one of these.
  • BRT costs $2–10 million per km to build vs. $50–200 million for light rail.
  • Bogotá's TransMilenio carries 2 million riders daily at speeds rivaling rail.
  • BRT capacity maxes out around 35,000 passengers per hour; rail becomes more efficient above 15,000–20,000 riders per direction.
