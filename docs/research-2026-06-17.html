Executive Summary

This collection of papers presents cutting-edge research across diverse aspects of transit planning, operations, and policy. Key themes include the increasing sophistication of AI and machine learning for predictive modeling (e.g., SMT-GraphFormer for trip prediction, polygon-based occupancy forecasting), the transformative potential of Large Language Models for personalized routing (ChatPlanner) and even map-free route generation (TransitLM), and the critical importance of robust data (NetMob26, GTFS analysis). There's a strong emphasis on social equity, with papers addressing transit desert remediation (Risk-Aware Planning) and justice-informed planning for AMoD systems, moving beyond purely economic or efficiency metrics. Finally, policy evaluation (NYC congestion pricing) and fundamental urban network design (spanning tree, amenity networks, car dependency index) are explored with new methodologies.

Paper 01 · Paper of the Day · arXiv 2606.17530

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao
This paper evaluates the impact of NYC's 2025 congestion pricing on system-wide urban mobility, finding increased bus and subway ridership but with uneven spatial patterns using time series foundation models.
Why It Matters

It's the first empirical analysis of a major US congestion pricing program, offering crucial insights into its real-world effects on public transit and urban travel demand, informing future policy decisions.

Key Insights
  • NYC's congestion pricing led to overall increases in bus and subway ridership.
  • Travel demand changes were spatially uneven across boroughs.
  • Time series foundation models are effective for generating robust counterfactuals for policy evaluation.
  • Congestion pricing policies generate complex spillovers across modes and locations.
Methodology

Time series foundation models are used to generate probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, applied to bus, subway, and aggregate trip volume data.

Results

Post-policy bus and subway ridership increased, but uneven spatial patterns emerged across boroughs.

Limitations

The abstract doesn't explicitly state limitations, but common ones for such studies involve data granularity, external confounding factors, and the generalizability of NYC-specific results. The difficulty in constructing credible control groups is acknowledged and addressed, implying it's a known challenge.

Practical Implications

Provides direct evidence for policymakers on the impacts of congestion pricing, helping to anticipate ridership shifts, identify areas needing transit service adjustments, and refine future pricing strategies.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, transit agencies, policymakers, economists, transportation researchers, city officials.

Comparison to Prior Work

Addresses the challenge of credible control groups in policy evaluation, often a limitation in prior impact studies, by using advanced time series foundation models for counterfactual generation.

  • RAG enhances LLM performance in this domain by providing relevant context.
  • Personalized routing moves beyond simple optimizations like shortest path to include qualitative user needs.
  • Integrating LLM outputs into objective functions of routing algorithms is a viable approach.
Methodology

Fine-tuned LLMs are combined with RAG to extract routing parameters and interpret user preferences from natural language, which are then integrated into the objective function of a transit routing algorithm.

Results

The abstract states the framework 'uses' and 'integrates' these components, implying successful conceptualization and likely experimental validation (though not detailed).

Limitations

The abstract does not explicitly state limitations, but potential ones could include computational cost, robustness to highly ambiguous language, real-time performance, and the scope of preferences that can be effectively interpreted.

Practical Implications

Development of next-generation transit planning apps that understand complex user queries (e.g., 'I need a route that avoids stairs and is scenic, even if it takes a bit longer'), improving user experience and accessibility.

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty

Polina Khoroshevskaya, Ashish Kumar Perukari
This paper formulates the remediation of transit deserts as a risk-aware partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) that incorporates Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) constraints to manage financial tail risk under uncertain demand.
Why It Matters

It provides a robust, quantitative framework for strategically planning transit services in underserved areas, explicitly considering financial risks and demand uncertainty, which is critical for equitable and sustainable urban development.

Key Insights
  • Transit desert remediation can be modeled as a risk-aware POMDP.
  • CVaR constraints are effective for managing financial tail risk in transit planning.
  • Latent demand in transit deserts can be estimated using demographic, land-use, and employment data.
  • Planning under demand uncertainty is crucial for effective remediation strategies.
Methodology

Formulates the problem as a POMDP with CVaR constraints, using demographic, land-use, and employment data to set priors over latent demand before service exists.

Results

The abstract details the formulation of the problem and the data used, implying a framework for solving such problems, though specific numerical results are not provided.

Limitations

The abstract does not detail specific limitations, but POMDPs can be computationally intensive for large-scale networks. The accuracy of latent demand priors is critical.

Practical Implications

Helps transit agencies and urban planners make data-driven decisions on where and how to deploy new transit services in underserved areas, minimizing financial risks while maximizing social equity.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, transit agencies, policy makers, equity advocates, operations researchers, data scientists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Advances existing transit planning models by explicitly incorporating both demand uncertainty and financial tail risk using CVaR, which is often overlooked or simplified in traditional deterministic planning.

TransitLM: A Large-Scale Dataset and Benchmark for Map-Free Transit Route Generation

Hanyu Guo, Jiedong Yang, Chao Chen, Longfei Xu, Kaikui Liu
TransitLM is a vast new dataset of 13M+ transit route planning records from Chinese cities, designed to train Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate structurally valid transit routes without relying on traditional map infrastructure.
Why It Matters

This research challenges the fundamental dependency of transit routing on structured map data, potentially enabling more flexible, adaptive, and perhaps even AI-driven route design and recovery in scenarios where map data is incomplete or unavailable.

Key Insights
  • Transit routing can potentially be performed without explicit reliance on structured map infrastructure.
  • Large-scale datasets are crucial for training LLMs for complex generative tasks like route planning.
  • LLMs can learn to produce structurally valid routes directly from examples.
  • The map-free approach could offer new paradigms for transit system design and robustness.
Methodology

Created TransitLM, a dataset of 13M+ transit route planning records from four Chinese cities (120,845 stations, 13,666 lines), used to train LLMs for map-free route generation.

Results

The paper presents the dataset and its purpose for training LLMs to generate routes without map dependency, suggesting a new benchmark for this task.

Limitations

The abstract doesn't explicitly state limitations, but potential challenges include ensuring the LLM-generated routes are optimal, robust to unseen scenarios, and interpretable for human planners. Scalability to complex, real-world constraints (e.g., real-time traffic, disruptions) is also a question.

Practical Implications

Could lead to new tools for rapid prototyping of transit routes, especially in areas with sparse or outdated map data, or for generating alternative routes during disruptions. It also opens doors for AI-assisted transit network design.

Who Should Care

AI researchers, transit planners, urban developers, data scientists, mapping service providers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Directly contrasts with traditional transit routing, which 'traditionally depends on structured map infrastructure,' by proposing a 'map-free' approach leveraging LLMs and a new large-scale dataset.

  • Heterophily refers to the co-agglomeration of different amenity types (e.g., retail near residential).
  • These patterns are vital for informing transit-oriented planning.
Methodology

Utilizes a Bayesian framework to quantify mixing patterns of urban amenities, identifying spatial scales of homophily and heterophily across global cities.

Results

The study identifies universal spatial scales for homophily and heterophily among amenity types across various global cities.

Limitations

The abstract doesn't detail limitations, but potential issues could include data availability and consistency across global cities, the definition and categorization of "amenities," and the causal relationship between amenity patterns and transit usage (rather than just correlation).

Practical Implications

Who Should Care

Paper 12 · arXiv 2605.18778

T-REX: Fast and Dynamic Journey Planning for Continental-Scale Public Transit Networks

Jonas Sauer, Patrick Steil, Sascha Witt
T-REX is a fast and dynamic journey planning algorithm for continental-scale public transit networks that uses multi-level overlay principles and precomputation to efficiently identify long-distance transfers while pruning irrelevant local connections.
Why It Matters

Efficient journey planning across vast, complex transit networks is crucial for passenger satisfaction, operational efficiency, and promoting public transit as a viable alternative for long-distance travel.

Key Insights
  • Multi-level overlay principles are effective for scaling journey planning to continental networks.
  • Precomputing long-distance relevant transfers significantly speeds up query times.
  • Pruning irrelevant local transfers during queries is key to efficiency.
  • The algorithm Pareto-optimizes for arrival time and transfer count.
Methodology

Applies multi-level overlay principles to Trip-Based Public Transit Routing, identifying long-distance-relevant transfers in a precomputation phase to prune local transfers during queries, optimizing arrival time and transfer count.

Results

The method allows for fast and dynamic journey planning for country/continental-scale networks.

Limitations

The abstract doesn't explicitly state limitations, but potential issues could include the complexity of updating precomputed overlays for dynamic network changes, the trade-off between precomputation time and query speed, and handling highly irregular or infrequent long-distance services.

Practical Implications

Enables highly responsive and accurate journey planning for national or international transit systems, improving user experience for travelers, facilitating intermodal connections, and aiding in strategic network design.

Who Should Care

Transit authorities managing large networks, intercity bus/rail operators, journey planner app developers, travel tech companies, logistics experts.

Comparison to Prior Work

Addresses the scalability challenge of journey planning for continental-scale networks, improving upon existing trip-based routing algorithms by leveraging multi-level overlays and intelligent pruning for greater speed and efficiency.

Contrarian Insights

Map-free Route Generation: TransitLM challenges the fundamental assumption that transit routing *requires* structured map infrastructure, suggesting LLMs can learn to generate valid routes directly.

Localized vs. Global Forecasting: The bus occupancy paper demonstrates that treating cities as homogeneous regions for forecasting is inferior; localized polygon-based models are more accurate.

Incentives vs. Equilibrium Regulation: The AMoD incentives paper proposes an "implementation-based" design that bypasses strong behavioral assumptions and bilevel optimization, suggesting a simpler, more robust alternative to traditional regulatory approaches.

Justice over Efficiency: Multiple papers (Justice-informed Planning, Risk-Aware Planning) argue that optimizing solely for revenue or average travel time often penalizes the worst-off, advocating for a shift towards justice-informed metrics.

Actionable Takeaways

Invest in AI/ML Capabilities: Transit agencies should explore integrating advanced ML models (Graph Transformers) for real-time prediction and consider LLM-powered interfaces for personalized passenger services.

Develop High-Resolution Data Strategies: Prioritize collecting and integrating multi-source, high-resolution operational and contextual data to enable sophisticated analysis and predictive modeling.

Prioritize Equity in Planning: Implement planning frameworks that explicitly consider social equity, user-specific constraints, and risk management in underserved areas, rather than solely optimizing for efficiency.

Adopt Advanced Policy Evaluation Tools: Utilize time series foundation models for robust counterfactual analysis when evaluating major policy changes like congestion pricing to understand true impacts.

Re-evaluate Network Design Methodologies: Explore graph-theoretic and optimization-based approaches (like spanning trees) for strategic network redesign, aiming to optimize for relevant metrics beyond simple coverage.

Long-Term Implications

Hyper-Personalized Mobility: AI-driven systems will offer highly personalized, real-time transit options tailored to individual preferences, accessibility needs, and even emotional states. Resilient and Adaptive Transit Networks: Systems will become more dynamic, capable of real-time adaptation to demand fluctuations, disruptions, and policy changes, potentially managed by AI. Equitable Urban Development: Planning frameworks will increasingly embed social justice, leading to cities where accessibility and mobility are not privileges but fundamental rights, with targeted investments in transit deserts. Reduced Car Dependency: Data-driven insights into car dependency will lead to more effective urban planning and policy interventions, shifting populations towards sustainable modes. Data as the New Infrastructure: The ability to collect, integrate, and analyze vast, diverse datasets will be as critical as physical infrastructure in managing and evolving urban mobility.
