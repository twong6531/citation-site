Topic 1: Vermont Avenue bus project moves ahead without bike lanes, for now

June 15 – June 25, 2026

L.A. County Superior Court Judge Kristin Escalante denied a preliminary injunction sought by street-safety advocate Joe Linton, allowing Metro's $400 million Vermont Avenue bus project to proceed without the bike lanes mandated by Measure HLA. The ruling hinges on whether Metro's coordination with the city of L.A. on permitting makes the city legally responsible for triggering the ballot measure's requirements.

Key Facts

  • $400 million total project cost for over 12 miles of dedicated bus lanes between 120th Street and Sunset Boulevard
  • Judge Escalante's June 15 ruling denied the injunction sought by Streets for All's Michael Schneider and advocate Joe Linton
  • Vermont Avenue has among the highest rates of pedestrian deaths and injuries of any L.A. corridor
  • Measure HLA (passed by voters in 2024) requires Mobility Plan upgrades whenever the city repaves at least 1/8 mile of a networked street

Why it matters: The case sets a precedent for whether Metro-led capital projects can sidestep city-mandated street-safety requirements simply by structuring permitting through county rather than city authority — a workaround other agencies may now attempt.

Key Facts

Why it matters: The report lands amid record World Cup ridership and ahead of 2028 Olympics capital planning, giving critics fresh ammunition just as Metro is asking voters and federal partners to fund its next generation of rail expansion.

Emerging Themes

World Cup ridership data is becoming the region's transit Rorschach test

Record shuttle and rail numbers around SoFi Stadium and the new D Line stations are being cited by Metro as proof that big transit investment pays off, even as the Reason Foundation's "Broken Rails" report and LAX's stalled SkyLink make the opposite case about cost and delivery. The same nine days produced both the strongest ridership data in years and the sharpest public criticism of agency execution.

Megaproject delivery credibility keeps eroding at the highest-profile sites

LAX's SkyLink remains empty three years past its target during the airport's biggest event in decades, while CA High-Speed Rail leans on a new private partner to de-risk its own construction pipeline. Both signal that the region's flagship infrastructure projects are increasingly judged on whether they can simply finish, not just on their long-term vision.

Transit-oriented housing policy and street design are converging on the same corridors

SCAG's preliminary SB 79 transit-zone map, the Vermont Avenue Measure HLA ruling, and the LA Times critique of barren D Line plazas are all, at root, arguments about who gets to shape the space around transit stops — housing advocates, street-safety advocates, or transit agencies optimizing purely for vehicle throughput.

Structural fiscal pressure is regional, not agency-specific

San Diego MTS held the line on fares only by absorbing a flagged structural deficit, while OCTA's budget still channels a majority of new dollars to transit but leaves freeway and express-lane spending largely intact. Across the region, agencies are managing the same underlying gap between rising service demand and stagnant traditional funding sources.

Rail safety remains an unresolved cost of expanding service

The fatal Metrolink strike near Baldwin Park lands the same week Pacific Surfliner and Metrolink are both running expanded, reservation-only schedules for World Cup crowds — a reminder that ramping up frequency on shared freight-passenger corridors continues to carry real safety costs that haven't been fully engineered away.
