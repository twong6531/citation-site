Transit vs. Highway: Comparing the Full Cost of Mobility

Politicians often present highway and transit projects as competing investments, comparing construction costs and implying the cheaper option is better. The comparison is misleading: full lifecycle costs — including maintenance, operations, land use, externalities, and opportunity costs — produce a very different picture.

Capital Costs: Apples to Apples

A lane-mile of urban freeway in a major metro costs $30–80 million to build, excluding land acquisition, which can add hundreds of millions more in congested urban areas. A mile of heavy rail subway costs $500 million to $2 billion in U.S. cities — far more per mile. But freeway lane-miles and subway miles serve very different capacities.

A single subway track can move 30,000–60,000 riders per hour at peak. A freeway lane moves 2,000–2,500 vehicles per hour; at average occupancy of 1.1–1.2 people per car, that's roughly 2,200–3,000 people. A ten-lane freeway moves roughly the same number of people as a single subway track — and costs far more to build and maintain.

Operating and Maintenance Costs

Transit agencies publicly report operating costs. Highway operating costs — snow removal, pothole repair, surface resealing, bridge maintenance, traffic management systems — are diffuse across federal, state, and local budgets and rarely receive the same scrutiny.

The American Society of Civil Engineers estimates the U.S. has a $786 billion infrastructure backlog, much of it on roads and bridges. Each year of deferred maintenance adds exponentially to eventual repair costs. Transit assets have similar deferred maintenance problems — New York's subway capital needs run into the tens of billions — but the accounting is more visible because agencies file consolidated financial statements.

Land Use and Induced Demand

New highway capacity induces new driving demand within 5–10 years, largely erasing congestion relief — a phenomenon called induced demand, documented extensively in research including a famous 2011 study in the American Economic Review. This means highway projects often deliver less congestion reduction than projected while accelerating suburban sprawl.

Transit, especially rail, induces transit-supportive land use when combined with zoning policy: higher-density housing, walkable retail, and reduced car ownership near stations. This changes the underlying demand structure rather than just serving existing demand.

Externalities: Carbon, Safety, Health

Driving generates externalities that are paid socially, not by the driver: greenhouse gas emissions, local air pollution, noise, traffic crashes, and sedentary health effects. A 2020 study in Transportation Research estimated the social cost of automobile externalities at roughly $0.30–0.50 per vehicle-mile traveled — costs that are not reflected in gas taxes or tolls.

Transit trips displace car trips and their associated externalities. The American Public Transportation Association estimates that transit use in the U.S. saves approximately 37 million metric tons of CO2 annually. Including externalities in the comparison significantly improves transit's cost-effectiveness relative to highways.

Key Takeaways
  • A single subway track can move 30,000–60,000 riders per hour vs. ~3,000 per lane for a freeway at average occupancy.
  • Induced demand erases freeway congestion relief within 5–10 years, documented repeatedly in transportation research.
  • Social costs of driving externalities — pollution, crashes, emissions — run $0.30–0.50 per vehicle-mile, not counted in direct cost comparisons.
  • U.S. transit use saves an estimated 37 million metric tons of CO2 annually.
