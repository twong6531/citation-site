Data-Driven Transit: How Agencies Redesign Routes With Numbers

For most of transit history, route planning relied on operator knowledge, public meetings, and educated guessing. Today, automatic passenger counters, farecard swipe data, GPS traces, and mobile app signals give agencies an unprecedented picture of how people actually use the system — and where the gaps are.

The Data Sources Available Today

Modern transit agencies collect multiple streams of operational data. Automatic vehicle location (AVL) systems track every vehicle in real time, recording departure and arrival times at every stop. Automatic passenger counters (APC) on most new vehicles log boardings and alightings at each stop using infrared sensors.

Farecard systems — whether magnetic stripe, contactless, or mobile — record transaction times and locations. Trip-chaining algorithms can reconstruct origin-destination pairs for riders who use the same card on connecting routes. Some agencies partner with mobile data firms to access anonymized smartphone location data that captures trips on routes without farecard requirements, such as buses in smaller cities.

Route Performance Analysis

With stop-level boarding data, planners can identify which portions of a route carry the most riders, where long dwell times occur, and where passengers cluster at peak hours versus midday. A route may average 100 boardings per trip, but if 60 of those board within the first 4 stops and the remaining 12 stops average fewer than 4 boardings each, there's a strong case for shortening the route and deploying the saved hours on a higher-demand corridor.

Origin-destination matrices reveal whether riders are making through trips (boarding at one end, riding to the other) or local trips (short-hop riders who could be served by a different route). A through-trip-heavy route may be a candidate for express service; a local-trip-heavy route may need frequency improvements more than coverage extension.

Network Redesign Using Data

Houston Metro's 2015 network redesign became the most-cited example of data-driven transit restructuring. Planners analyzed origin-destination data to identify the travel markets — pairs of neighborhoods generating the most trips — and designed a new network to connect those pairs with frequent, direct service. Routes that had accumulated over decades of incremental additions were replaced by a rationalized grid.

The results were measurable: within two years, the number of riders with access to frequent service (10-minute headways) increased by 50%, and total ridership on the redesigned network grew 20%. Columbus, Indianapolis, and Austin have since completed similar redesigns using comparable data-driven methodologies.

Limits of the Data Approach

Data describes where people go, not where they want to go but can't. A neighborhood with poor transit generates no transit trips by definition — its absence from origin-destination matrices doesn't mean demand doesn't exist. Planners must supplement ridership data with demographic analysis, employment center mapping, and sometimes field observation to identify latent demand.

Data quality issues are also real: farecard data underrepresents cash riders, APC sensors malfunction in bad weather, and AVL systems can have GPS gaps in tunnels or urban canyons. Agencies increasingly use multiple data sources cross-validated against each other to catch anomalies.

Key Takeaways
  • Modern agencies combine AVL, APC, farecard, and mobile location data to build stop-level ridership pictures.
  • Houston's data-driven 2015 redesign increased access to frequent service by 50% and grew ridership 20%.
  • Origin-destination matrices from farecard data can reveal whether routes carry through-trip or local-trip demand.
  • Data captures where people go but not latent demand in underserved areas — demographic overlay is essential.
