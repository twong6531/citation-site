Executive Summary

Today's cluster is anchored by the first wave of credible empirical evidence on what actually happens when a major U.S. city reprices driving: a study of New York's cordon-based congestion pricing program, live since January 2025, uses time-series foundation models to build calibrated counterfactual demand forecasts and finds real but spatially uneven gains in bus and subway ridership. A companion NYC paper builds a socioeconomic-aware agent-based simulation of fare policy itself, finding fare-free buses deliver outsized equity benefits relative to distance-based pricing. A second strand pushes beyond U.S. borders to ask whether transit access changes life outcomes, not just travel patterns: a quasi-natural experiment across five Indian states finds free bus schemes for women causally increase workforce participation, with the largest effects in the most mobility-restricted, patriarchal districts; a companion study across 64 Indian and Mexican cities shows residents of deprived neighborhoods systematically use mobility to "break the bubble" into better-resourced areas, beyond what simple gravity models predict; and a Helsinki/Madrid/Budapest comparison finds that radial transit lines into peripheral neighborhoods boost social-mixing potential without necessarily closing the amenity-access gap that the 15-minute-city framework cares about. A third strand is purely infrastructural: a new continental-scale journey-planning algorithm (T-REX) answers transit queries across all of Europe in under 10 milliseconds, a GTFS-mining method extracts stable recurring schedules from messy real-world feeds, and a Manhattan case study shows justice-informed routing for autonomous mobility-on-demand can approach full social-equity outcomes simply by pairing it with free public transit.

Emerging Themes

Congestion pricing has moved from theory to live policy laboratory: NYC's first-in-nation cordon program is now generating real before/after ridership data, while a parallel simulation paper stress-tests alternative fare structures against the same city's socioeconomic geography.

Transit access is being measured by life outcomes, not just trip counts: free-bus and mobility studies in India and Mexico tie transit directly to women's workforce participation and to residents' ability to physically escape neighborhood-level deprivation.

Core transit engineering keeps quietly scaling up: continental-scale journey planning, stable GTFS schedule extraction, and justice-aware AMoD/transit network optimization all push the operational substrate further without much public attention.

Paper 01 · Paper of the Day · arXiv (2606.17530)

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
Using time-series foundation models to build calibrated counterfactual forecasts, the study finds New York's first-in-the-nation cordon congestion-pricing program produced real bus and subway ridership gains — but distributed unevenly across the city.
Why It Matters

Congestion pricing has been debated almost entirely on the basis of pre-deployment models; this is among the first papers to evaluate the actual, deployed NYC program with a methodologically serious counterfactual, giving other cities considering similar tolls real before/after evidence instead of simulation alone.

Key Insights
  • NYC's January 2025 cordon-based congestion pricing program is evaluated using actual post-deployment bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data.
  • Time-series foundation models generate probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, addressing the lack of a clean control group.
  • Post-policy bus and subway ridership both show measurable gains relative to the counterfactual.
  • Travel demand changes are spatially uneven, with some areas and corridors absorbing much larger shifts than others.
  • The spillover pattern suggests congestion pricing's mode-shift effects cannot be modeled as a single city-wide average.
Methodology

Time-series foundation models trained to produce probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, applied to real bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data before and after NYC's January 2025 congestion pricing launch.

Results

Statistically meaningful gains in transit ridership following congestion pricing, with substantial spatial heterogeneity in how much travel demand shifted across different parts of the city and network.

Limitations

Single-city, single-policy natural experiment with no true randomized control; counterfactual quality depends on the foundation model's calibration; effects measured so far reflect only the early post-launch period and may evolve.

Practical Implications

Cities evaluating their own congestion-pricing proposals (London-style expansions, Chicago, and others) can point to NYC as the first large-scale, methodologically credible U.S. case showing real transit ridership gains, while planning for uneven spatial impacts rather than assuming uniform city-wide benefit.

Who Should Care

Congestion-pricing policy designers, transit agency planning teams, city DOTs evaluating road-pricing proposals, transportation economists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond pre-deployment simulation studies of congestion pricing (London, Stockholm, Singapore precedents) by using a deployed U.S. program and a foundation-model-based counterfactual approach built specifically to handle policy spillovers that defeat traditional control-group designs.

Paper 02 · arXiv (2604.14758)

Ticket to ride: Impact of free public transport on women's workforce participation in India

Udayan Rathore, Ashish Singh
A triple-difference quasi-natural experiment across five Indian states finds free bus schemes for women causally increase paid workforce participation, with the largest effects in the most mobility-restricted, patriarchal districts.
Why It Matters

Free-transit debates usually center on ridership and revenue; this shows a free-fare policy targeted at one demographic group can causally move a structural labor-market outcome, reframing transit subsidy as a labor-participation lever, not just a mobility one.

Key Insights
  • Uses two rounds of India's representative Time Use Survey and a triple-difference estimation strategy, complemented by an event-study design.
  • Free bus schemes for women significantly improved women's paid work participation and duration of employment.
  • Results are confirmed not to be a continuation of prior employment trends already underway before the scheme.
  • Effects concentrate among early-adopter states (Punjab, Tamil Nadu) with historically different baseline workforce participation.
  • Effects are disproportionately larger for women in more patriarchal districts with higher pre-existing mobility restrictions.
  • The mechanism is non-financial: the scheme works by easing mobility constraints rather than purely reducing fare cost.
Methodology

Quasi-natural experiment exploiting staggered introduction of free bus schemes for women across five Indian states, analyzed via triple-difference estimation on Time Use Survey data and validated with an event-study framework.

Results

Statistically robust increases in women's paid work participation and employment duration, concentrated among early-adopter states and strongest in districts with the highest baseline mobility restrictions on women.

Limitations

Limited to five Indian states with specific scheme designs; generalizability to other cultural and labor-market contexts outside South Asia is untested; mechanism is inferred rather than directly observed.

Practical Implications

Policymakers designing gender-targeted transit subsidies elsewhere in the Global South can use this as causal evidence that fare-free transit removes a binding non-financial constraint on women's labor supply, particularly where mobility restrictions are culturally entrenched.

Who Should Care

Gender and labor economists, transit equity policymakers, development economics researchers, South Asian transportation ministries.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior fare-elasticity and ridership-focused free-transit research by directly linking a transit subsidy to a downstream labor-market outcome with a causal identification strategy, rather than stopping at mode-share or ridership effects.

Paper 03 · arXiv (2605.18778)

T-REX: Fast and Dynamic Journey Planning for Continental-Scale Public Transit Networks

Jonas Sauer, Patrick Steil, Sascha Witt
A new multi-level overlay algorithm answers Pareto-optimal journey-planning queries across the entire European transit network in under 10 milliseconds — 20× faster than prior trip-based routing and 80× faster than unindexed approaches.
Why It Matters

Cross-border, continental-scale trip planners (the kind needed for a true pan-European Google Maps-style transit app) have historically required either huge precomputed indexes or slow queries; T-REX makes interactive, real-time continental journey planning practical with a moderate memory footprint.

Key Insights
  • Applies multi-level overlay principles to Trip-Based Public Transit Routing (TB) for country- and continent-scale networks.
  • Identifies only the transfers relevant to long-distance travel during a short precomputation phase, pruning irrelevant local transfers at query time.
  • Pareto-optimizes arrival time and number of trips used, matching the optimality guarantees of prior state-of-the-art algorithms.
  • Answers queries on a full European network in under 10ms — a 20× speedup over TB and 80× over algorithms without preprocessing.
  • Precomputation takes only two minutes with moderate memory use, and real-time schedule updates can be incorporated within seconds.
Methodology

Multi-level network partitioning combined with a redesigned trip-based query algorithm focused on transfer relevance rather than trip relevance, benchmarked against prior overlay-based and unindexed routing algorithms on a full-scale European transit network.

Results

Sub-10-millisecond query times at continental scale with two-minute precomputation and moderate memory footprint, while preserving Pareto-optimality on arrival time and trip count.

Limitations

Benchmarked primarily on the European network; performance characteristics on networks with very different topology (e.g., sparse North American intercity rail) are not directly demonstrated; real-time update latency under heavy simultaneous disruption is not stress-tested.

Practical Implications

Transit app developers and multi-country routing platforms can adopt T-REX to deliver interactive, real-time cross-border itinerary search without the memory and latency trade-offs of prior continental-scale algorithms.

Who Should Care

Transit routing engine developers, multimodal trip-planner companies, national rail and cross-border transit authorities, algorithms researchers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Directly outperforms Trip-Based Public Transit Routing (TB) and prior overlay-based continental algorithms on both query latency and memory efficiency, while preserving the same optimality guarantees.

Paper 04 · arXiv (2606.21897)

Simulating Public Transit Fare Policies in NYC: An Efficient, Socioeconomic-Aware Framework

Parker Wischhover, Hossein Amiri, Kiara Ha, Jooyoung Yoo, Lina Li, Andreas Züfle, Joon-Seok Kim
A scalable agent-based simulation of NYC fare policy finds that fare-free bus programs substantially benefit lower-income riders by increasing bus use and cutting fare burden, with modest trade-offs in revenue.
Why It Matters

Fare policy debates are usually driven by revenue projections alone; this gives agencies a socioeconomic-aware simulation tool to weigh equity gains against revenue trade-offs before committing politically and financially to a fare change — complementing today's real-world congestion-pricing evidence with a forward-looking fare-design tool.

Key Insights
  • Combines a synthetic population, agent-based simulation, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling.
  • Pricing changes modestly affect total ridership but significantly alter modal composition.
  • Impacts of fare changes are heterogeneous across income groups, not uniform city-wide.
  • Fare-free bus policy substantially increases bus usage and reduces fare burden for lower-income riders.
  • A sampling-based approach reduces computational cost while preserving city-scale aggregate accuracy.
Methodology

Agent-based simulation with a synthetic NYC population, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling, evaluated under multiple fare scenarios including fare-free buses and distance-based pricing.

Results

Fare-free bus policy delivers the largest equity benefit among scenarios tested, with modest revenue trade-offs; distance-based pricing and fare increases reshape mode share more than total ridership.

Limitations

Synthetic population and mode-choice assumptions may not capture all behavioral nuance; calibrated specifically to NYC, which has unusually dense, multimodal transit options not present in most US cities.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies considering fare-free bus pilots can use this framework's sampling-based simulation approach to model city-scale equity and revenue trade-offs before deployment.

Who Should Care

Transit agency fare-policy teams, city budget offices, transportation equity advocates.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior fare-elasticity studies, which typically estimate aggregate ridership effects, by explicitly modeling socioeconomic heterogeneity in mode-choice response.

Paper 05 · arXiv (2603.29782)

Urban mobility enables deprivation bubble breaking in Indian and Mexican cities

Yuan Liao, Federico Delussu, Sílvia de Sojo, Laura Alessandretti, Antonio Desiderio
Combining mobile-phone mobility networks with satellite-derived deprivation indices across 64 cities in India and Mexico, the study finds residents of deprived areas systematically travel to better-off areas far more than simple gravity models predict.
Why It Matters

Most deprivation measurement is residence-based and static; this shows daily mobility itself is a meaningful, measurable channel by which residents partially escape neighborhood-level disadvantage, with direct implications for how transit access should be weighed in deprivation indices.

Key Insights
  • Quantifies "bubble breaking" — travel to less-deprived areas — using mobile phone mobility networks and satellite-derived deprivation indices across 64 Indian and Mexican cities.
  • Residents of deprived areas exhibit a compensatory mobility pattern exceeding gravity-model expectations based on population and road networks.
  • The "residual" bubble breaking (unexplained by gravity models) is tied to built-environment tension: high local amenity diversity reduces the need to travel, while high amenity density and positive neighborhood spillovers are associated with more cross-boundary movement.
  • Residual bubble breaking reveals a dimension of spatial inequality invisible to residence-based deprivation measures alone.
Methodology

Mobile phone mobility network analysis combined with satellite-derived deprivation indices and gravity-model baselines, applied across 64 cities in India and Mexico.

Results

Systematic, gravity-model-exceeding travel from deprived to less-deprived areas, with the residual component explained partly by local amenity diversity and density/spillover effects in the built environment.

Limitations

Limited to India and Mexico; mobile-phone mobility data carries its own representativeness caveats; satellite-derived deprivation indices are a proxy rather than a direct socioeconomic measurement.

Practical Implications

Urban planners and development agencies assessing neighborhood deprivation should incorporate mobility-based "bubble breaking" capacity, not just residence-based indicators, when prioritizing transit investment in underserved areas.

Who Should Care

Urban deprivation researchers, development economists, transit equity planners in the Global South, mobility data scientists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond static, residence-based deprivation indicators used in prior urban poverty research by quantifying a dynamic, mobility-based escape mechanism with an explicit gravity-model counterfactual.

Paper 06 · arXiv (2604.00699)

Public transport in the 15-minute city

Zsófia Zádor, Gergő Pintér, Máté Mizsák, Bence Kovács, Imre Felde, Balázs Lengyel
Comparing Helsinki, Madrid, and Budapest, the study finds multimodal public transport substantially extends 15-minute-city amenity access and social-spatial integration, but radial high-speed lines into peripheral neighborhoods boost social mixing without actually closing the amenity-access gap there.
Why It Matters

The 15-minute city framework is usually framed around walking and cycling; this is one of the first frameworks to formally incorporate public transport, and its peripheral-neighborhood finding is a direct, practical warning against assuming any new transit line automatically delivers 15-minute-city benefits.

Key Insights
  • Proposes a framework incorporating public transport into the 15-minute city model using openly available data.
  • Multimodal mobility substantially increases amenity access and socio-spatial integration within a 15-minute reach across all three cities studied.
  • Urban peripheries benefit significantly in social-mixing potential from radial or high-speed transit lines.
  • Those same radial/high-speed lines alone do not improve peripheral neighborhoods' actual access to amenities.
  • The findings argue for optimizing polycentric transport networks rather than simply extending high-speed radial lines outward.
Methodology

Comparative framework incorporating multimodal public transport into 15-minute-city accessibility modeling, applied to open data across Helsinki, Madrid, and Budapest.

Results

Multimodal transport meaningfully extends amenity access and social integration city-wide, but radial/high-speed lines into peripheries improve social mixing potential without proportionally improving those areas' amenity access.

Limitations

Limited to three European cities with relatively dense, mature transit systems; "amenity access" and "social mixing potential" are modeled constructs rather than directly observed behavior; polycentric network optimization is recommended but not concretely designed.

Practical Implications

Cities pursuing 15-minute-city policy should evaluate whether planned transit investment targets actual amenity access in peripheral areas, rather than assuming any new high-speed radial line will deliver 15-minute-city-style benefits.

Who Should Care

15-minute-city and TOD policy planners, urban accessibility researchers, European transit network designers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends 15-minute-city literature, which has focused primarily on walking/cycling access, by formally integrating multimodal public transport and exposing a gap between social-mixing benefits and amenity-access benefits that walking-focused frameworks miss.

Paper 07 · arXiv (2605.25617)

Justice-informed Planning of Intermodal Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems under Operational Constraints

Giacomo Ganassoli, Francesco Mazzeo, Cecilia Pasquale, Silvia Siri, Mauro Salazar
A Manhattan case study finds that monetary budgets sharply limit how just autonomous mobility-on-demand systems can be when run as profit-seeking transportation network companies — but pairing AMoD with free public transit gets nearly all the way to a fully equitable outcome.
Why It Matters

Robotaxi deployment is usually evaluated on efficiency and revenue; this is among the first papers to operationally optimize for justice constraints directly and show, with a real case study, that policy choices (like free transit) matter more than AMoD fleet design alone for achieving equitable outcomes.

Key Insights
  • Characterizes intermodal AMoD-plus-transit operations via mesoscopic network flow models that explicitly account for user budget and safety constraints and infrastructural capacity.
  • Optimizes system operations under both utilitarian-efficiency and justice-informed objectives.
  • Monetary budget constraints on users significantly limit the social-justice potential achievable when AMoD operates as a standard transportation network company.
  • Granting free public transit alongside AMoD achieves sufficiency levels very close to a fully free intermodal system.
  • Justice-informed operations can be achieved without compromising standard efficiency metrics when paired with the right social policy.
Methodology

Mesoscopic network flow optimization models for intermodal AMoD-plus-transit systems, comparing utilitarian and justice-informed objectives under budget, safety, and infrastructure capacity constraints, validated with a real-world Manhattan case study.

Results

Budget constraints are the binding limiter on social justice for profit-seeking AMoD; combining AMoD with free public transit achieves justice outcomes nearly matching a fully free system without sacrificing efficiency.

Limitations

Single-city case study (Manhattan); relies on mesoscopic, not microscopic, behavioral modeling; assumes AMoD and transit operators cooperate with the optimization rather than pursuing independent commercial incentives.

Practical Implications

Cities regulating robotaxi deployment can use this as evidence that pairing AMoD permits with free or heavily subsidized transit access is a more effective equity lever than regulating AMoD pricing or service-area mandates alone.

Who Should Care

City transportation regulators, AMoD/robotaxi policy teams, transit equity researchers, operations research practitioners in shared mobility.

Comparison to Prior Work

Bridges transport-justice research, which has focused on assessing injustices, with transport-planning optimization, which has focused on utilitarian efficiency, by building operational models that explicitly prescribe justice-informed solutions.

Paper 08 · arXiv (2606.23931)

Welfarist Control Design — How to fulfill the societal mandate in multi-agent control?

Sophie Hall, Kai Zhang, Ilia Shilov, Heinrich H. Nax, Saverio Bolognani
A position paper argues that automated systems allocating scarce shared resources — including public transit, road space, and grid capacity — are shaped mostly by industry convention rather than principled ethical design, and proposes feedback-control paradigms to fix that.
Why It Matters

As transit and mobility allocation becomes more automated (dynamic pricing, AMoD dispatch, signal control), this argues the real gap is institutional and ethical, not technical — a useful counterweight to the purely algorithmic optimization papers also in today's batch.

Key Insights
  • Frames public transit, road space, water rights, energy access, and grid capacity as instances of the same underlying problem: automated allocation of scarce shared resources among agents.
  • Argues current design choices for such systems are driven largely by industry norms and conventions rather than conscientious ethical design.
  • Surveys three control-design paradigms — online feedback optimization, control of Markov decision processes, and model predictive control — as tools for principled allocation.
  • Proposes a pipeline: aggregate individual agents' preferences into design objectives, then ensure and certify those specifications are fulfilled.
  • Argues the feedback nature of control systems enables more equitable resource allocation than has been achieved to date.
Methodology

Conceptual/position paper surveying and synthesizing three control-design paradigms (feedback optimization, MDP control, model predictive control) as applied to societal resource-allocation problems, without new empirical experiments.

Results

No empirical results; the contribution is a conceptual framework and design pipeline for incorporating societal preference aggregation and certification into automated control systems.

Limitations

Purely conceptual with no empirical validation or case study; preference-aggregation step faces well-known social-choice-theory difficulties not deeply addressed; "certification" of fulfilled specifications is asserted as feasible without a worked example.

Practical Implications

Agencies designing automated transit-incentive or dynamic-pricing systems (see Paper 11 in recent digests) should treat preference-aggregation and ethical-design choices as first-class engineering requirements, not implementation details to be decided ad hoc by whichever vendor builds the system.

Who Should Care

Control engineers designing automated transit/mobility allocation systems, transportation policy ethicists, smart-infrastructure regulators.

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinguishes itself from the largely technical optimization literature (including several papers in recent digests) by foregrounding the institutional and ethical gap in automated resource-allocation design rather than proposing a new algorithm.

Formal definition of DayTypes based on complete Route Pattern trip sets, paired with an H3 spatial-key-based stable extraction algorithm and a schedule-similarity comparison framework, applied to real-world GTFS feeds.

Results

Produces a stable, reusable recurring-schedule object (DayTypes) from raw GTFS data, addressing a structural gap that previously left scheduling, assignment, and mismatch models without an explicit recurrent supply representation.

Limitations

Who Should Care

Contrarian Insights

The 15-minute-city paper finds that radial or high-speed transit lines into peripheral neighborhoods boost "social mixing potential" without actually improving those neighborhoods' access to amenities — a finding that complicates the common assumption that any new transit line to an underserved area closes the access gap it was built to close.

The Welfarist Control Design position paper argues that the real bottleneck in equitable automated transit allocation isn't algorithmic sophistication — it's that design choices are driven by "industry norms and conventions" rather than principled ethical design, a pointed critique of the same optimization-heavy literature (including several papers in this and recent digests) that assumes better algorithms alone solve equity.

Actionable Takeaways

Cities evaluating congestion-pricing proposals can now cite NYC's deployed program as real-world evidence of transit ridership gains, but should plan for spatially uneven demand shifts rather than assuming uniform city-wide benefit.

Policymakers designing gender-targeted transit subsidies should note that free-fare schemes work primarily by easing non-financial mobility constraints, with the largest workforce-participation gains concentrated in the most mobility-restricted populations.

15-minute-city planners should audit whether proposed radial or high-speed transit lines into peripheral neighborhoods will actually improve amenity access there, rather than assuming any new transit investment automatically delivers 15-minute-city benefits.

Agencies regulating AMoD/robotaxi deployment can treat pairing free or subsidized public transit with AMoD permits as a more effective equity lever than regulating AMoD pricing or service-area mandates in isolation.

Multi-country routing platforms and transit app developers facing continental-scale latency problems should evaluate T-REX-style multi-level overlay routing instead of unindexed or simple trip-based approaches.

Long-Term Implications

Today's papers mark a turning point from simulation-only congestion-pricing research to real deployed-policy evaluation, and the early NYC evidence — real ridership gains, but spatially uneven — will likely become the reference case other U.S. and global cities cite (for or against) as more congestion-pricing proposals move from theory to legislation. At the same time, transit research is increasingly measuring success by downstream life outcomes rather than trip counts alone: India's free-bus-for-women evidence and the India/Mexico deprivation-bubble-breaking study both suggest that future transit-equity funding cases will need to demonstrate labor-market and opportunity-access effects, not just ridership or mode-share, to remain persuasive to budget offices. Underneath both threads, the purely technical substrate — continental-scale routing, stable GTFS schedule extraction, justice-aware AMoD optimization — keeps maturing quietly, while today's Welfarist Control Design paper is a pointed reminder that none of this technical progress automatically produces equitable outcomes unless ethical and institutional design choices are made deliberately rather than inherited from industry convention.
