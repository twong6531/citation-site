Emergency Evacuation: Getting People Out of Tunnels and Stations

A fire in a subway tunnel, a power failure trapping passengers underground, a flooding station — transit emergency evacuations are rare but require extensive planning because the consequences of a disorganized response are catastrophic. The 1987 King's Cross fire that killed 31 people in London's Underground rewrote evacuation protocols worldwide.

King's Cross and Its Legacy

The 1987 King's Cross Underground fire started in a wooden escalator and killed 31 people. The subsequent investigation by Desmond Fennel produced a landmark report that identified systemic failures: staff who did not consider fire part of their responsibility, inadequate communication systems, and no evacuation procedures practiced by frontline workers.

The Fennel report drove evacuation planning reforms not just in London but internationally. It established that transit frontline workers — not just managers — must understand and be trained in evacuation procedures, that communication must be redundant, and that regular drills are non-negotiable. Almost every major transit system revised its emergency procedures in the years following King's Cross.

Evacuation Challenges Underground

Subway evacuations face challenges that surface transit does not. Tunnels have limited escape routes, may fill with smoke faster than people can move, and lack natural navigation cues like daylight. Passengers unfamiliar with a station may not know where emergency exits are. People with mobility disabilities face disproportionate difficulty on stairs when elevators are unavailable.

Evacuation research recommends emergency lighting at floor level (which remains visible below smoke), consistent stairway locations and signage across a system, and pre-posted evacuation maps at every platform. New York's subway requires evacuation route maps at all platforms; London's Underground has color-coded escape paths at each station.

Communication During Emergencies

Effective communication is the most critical element of transit emergency response. Passengers who hear clear instructions — 'there is a fire in the station, please proceed to the nearest exit' — respond better than passengers who hear confusing or absent announcements. Studies of evacuation behavior consistently find that people wait for official guidance before acting, sometimes fatally.

Modern transit systems invest in distributed speaker systems that can direct specific messages to specific locations, public address systems with voice clarity standards, and visual displays that show emergency routing. New York's subway has deployed a cellular-enabled emergency communication network allowing trains and stations to maintain contact even in deep tunnels.

Drills, Training, and Continuous Improvement

Regular evacuation drills are mandatory for transit agencies receiving federal funds, but the quality of drills varies widely. Paper-based table-top exercises identify planning gaps; full-scale drills with actual train movements and passenger volunteers identify execution problems.

BART, London Underground, and Tokyo Metro conduct annual full-scale evacuation exercises involving hundreds of participants. Post-drill reports are used to revise procedures, relocate equipment, and address training gaps. Agencies that treat drills as compliance exercises rather than genuine learning opportunities consistently perform worse in real emergencies than those that invest in realistic scenario training.

Key Takeaways
  • The 1987 King's Cross fire killed 31 people and rewrote evacuation planning standards worldwide for transit systems.
  • Floor-level emergency lighting, consistent stairway signage, and evacuation maps at every platform are evidence-based evacuation design requirements.
  • Passengers consistently wait for official guidance before evacuating — clear, early communication is the most critical emergency response factor.
  • BART, London Underground, and Tokyo Metro conduct annual full-scale evacuation exercises; table-top exercises alone are insufficient.
