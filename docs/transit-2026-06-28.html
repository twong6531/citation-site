HIGH LA Metro LADOT

Topic 1: Vermont Avenue bus project stays on track without bike lanes

June 15 – June 25, 2026

L.A. County Superior Court Judge Kristin Escalante denied a preliminary injunction sought by street-safety advocate Joe Linton, letting Metro's $400 million Vermont Avenue bus project proceed without the bike lanes that Measure HLA arguably requires. Escalante found that Metro's coordination with the city of L.A. on permitting does not make the city the legal "owner" of the project, sidestepping HLA's trigger for street-safety upgrades. The ruling remains the key legal fact heading into the project's next construction phase.

Key Facts

  • $400 million project adding dedicated bus lanes along more than 12 miles of Vermont Avenue between 120th Street and Sunset Boulevard
  • Judge Escalante's June 15 ruling denied Joe Linton and Streets for All's Michael Schneider's request for an injunction
  • Vermont Avenue has among the highest rates of pedestrian deaths and injuries of any L.A. corridor
  • Linton's underlying lawsuit argues the city failed to apply Measure HLA's Mobility Plan upgrades when it last resurfaced Vermont's service roads

Why it matters: The ruling sets an early precedent for whether Metro-led capital projects can avoid city-mandated street-safety requirements by routing permitting through the county rather than the city — a workaround other regional megaprojects could lean on next.

Topic 3: LAX's $3.34B people mover still hasn't carried a single World Cup passenger

June 24 – June 28, 2026

As World Cup travelers stream into LAX, the long-delayed Automated People Mover remains stuck in testing rather than carrying passengers — three years past its original 2023 opening target. Disputes between Los Angeles World Airports and contractor LINXS over responsibility for the delays continue, with the contractor citing airport-side "blockers" and LAWA citing extensive safety-testing requirements. Travel advisories are urging World Cup visitors to budget significant extra time for ground transportation at LAX.

Key Facts

  • The elevated automated train is a $3.34 billion project, now three years behind its original 2023 opening
  • Built by contractor LAX Integrated Express Solutions (LINXS) for LAWA
  • LINXS projects passenger service won't begin until at least early October 2026
  • World Cup arrivals are being shifted onto shuttles and traffic-choked roadways instead

Why it matters: LAX's people mover is the connective piece meant to link the airport to Metro's rail network — its absence during the World Cup is a costly preview of risk for the 2028 Olympics, when far larger crowds will need exactly this link.

MEDIUM CA High-Speed Rail

Topic 4: LA-to-Anaheim high-speed rail segment advances through environmental review

December 5, 2025 – February 3, 2026 (ongoing impact)

The California High-Speed Rail Authority's Draft EIR/EIS for the 30-mile Los Angeles-to-Anaheim section — the southernmost link of Phase 1 — closed public comment in February after a roughly two-month review window. The Authority's preferred alternative shares passenger track along the existing freight/Metrolink/Amtrak corridor through Vernon, Commerce, Bell, Montebello, Pico Rivera, Norwalk, La Mirada, Buena Park, Fullerton, and Anaheim, with a light maintenance facility sited at 26th Street. The segment's progress comes as statewide system cost estimates have separately climbed as high as $231 billion.

Key Facts

  • 30-mile LA-to-Anaheim segment connects Union Station to the Anaheim Regional Transportation Intermodal Center
  • Draft EIR/EIS comment period ran December 5, 2025 – February 3, 2026
  • Preferred alternative: Shared Passenger Track Alternative A with a light maintenance facility at 26th Street
  • Statewide system cost estimates have been reported as high as $231 billion

Why it matters: LA-to-Anaheim is the segment that would finally tie California's bullet train into the existing SoCal commuter-rail network at Union Station — its environmental clearance timeline is now one of the clearest signals of whether HSR construction will ever reach the LA basin.

Key Facts

  • Agency faces a $15 million budget shortfall tied to mechanical and supply-chain issues
  • Could lose up to $10 million in funding from LA Metro and OCTA, its two largest funders
  • Under consideration: elimination of weekend service and a possible fare increase as early as October

Why it matters: Metrolink set an all-time weekend ridership record in March 2025 after its "Reimagined" service expansion — now, just over a year later, it's contemplating the opposite move, a classic "transit death spiral" risk where service cuts and ridership losses reinforce each other.

Topic 7: LA City Council delays SB 79 transit-housing density mandate for up to 88% of eligible sites

May 14 – June 3, 2026

The L.A. City Council voted June 3 to approve a "Phased Implementation Ordinance" and a "Low-Rise Ordinance" that together delay SB 79's denser-housing-near-transit mandate citywide until likely 2030 for the large majority of eligible parcels, while upzoning some sites now in exchange. SB 79, signed by Gov. Newsom in October 2025, upzones land within a half-mile of major rail and bus-rapid-transit stops and takes statewide effect July 1, 2026 — but L.A. is using built-in carve-outs to soften its local impact.

Key Facts

  • SB 79 signed into law October 10, 2025; takes statewide effect July 1, 2026
  • City Council approved delay ordinances on June 3, 2026, after Planning Commission approval May 14
  • Up to 88% of eligible sites citywide could see implementation delayed until 2030
  • Remaining ineligible-for-delay hubs are concentrated in the San Fernando Valley and Westside

Why it matters: SB 79 was designed to force denser housing near exactly the kind of transit investment Metro is touting in its D Line ridership numbers — L.A.'s delay tactics show how much friction remains between state housing mandates and local land-use control even on the state's flagship transit corridors.

Emerging Themes

LA Metro and Metrolink are telling opposite stories with the same World Cup

Metro is posting its best rail ridership numbers in six years and running an unprecedented 15-location World Cup shuttle network, while its regional rail partner Metrolink is locking in indefinite 20% service cuts and weighing the elimination of weekend service entirely. The divergence shows how unevenly the region's transit fortunes are distributed even within a single funding ecosystem — and how much Metrolink's crisis could undercut Metro's World Cup wins if riders can't make connecting trips.

Megaproject delivery credibility keeps eroding at the highest-profile sites

LAX's people mover remains empty three years past target during the airport's biggest event in decades, while California High-Speed Rail's LA-to-Anaheim segment is still working through environmental review as systemwide costs are reported near $231 billion. Both are increasingly judged on whether they can simply finish, not on their original promise.

Transit-oriented housing policy is colliding with the same corridors Metro is investing in

LA's move to delay SB 79's transit-housing density mandate for up to 88% of eligible sites lands just as Metro touts a 62% year-over-year ridership jump on the D Line extension that SB 79 was designed to densify around. The city is simultaneously expanding rail capacity and slow-walking the housing policy meant to capitalize on it.

Shared-corridor capacity is the region's hidden bottleneck

Pacific Surfliner's newly restored pre-pandemic frequency, Metrolink's service cuts, and CA High-Speed Rail's proposed shared passenger track through the same LA-to-Anaheim corridor all compete for the same physical rail right-of-way — meaning a disruption or funding shortfall on any one service has ripple effects across all three.

Institution-building is a quiet but consequential thread

Metro's transit police recruitment drew nearly 2,500 applicants in weeks — a sign the agency's in-house Department of Public Safety, launched amid safety concerns, may scale faster than comparable transit police forces elsewhere, even as larger capital and funding fights dominate headlines.
