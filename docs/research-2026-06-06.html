Transit Research Digest

Transit Equity & Automation

June 6, 2026 · 10 Papers · arXiv, iScience, International Economic Review

Transit research in 2025-2026 is converging on three urgent imperatives: decarbonizing bus fleets pragmatically rather than ideologically, deploying autonomous and on-demand services that genuinely serve underserved communities, and using AI to scale previously labor-intensive planning tasks like walkability assessment and ridership forecasting. A striking theme across multiple papers is that optimization without equity constraints produces worse social outcomes — from SAV network design to transit investment type. The field is maturing from proof-of-concept simulation to real-world pilot analysis, with Atlanta's MARTA Reach and Pioneer Valley Transit Authority providing empirical anchors for theoretical models.

Long-Term Implications

The transit sector is approaching a bifurcation point: agencies that integrate equity analysis, renewable energy co-planning, and AI-assisted operations will diverge sharply in performance and social impact from those treating each challenge in isolation. The papers collectively suggest that the 2030s will be defined not by whether cities adopt electric buses or autonomous vehicles, but by whether they have the institutional capacity to deploy these technologies in ways that reduce rather than entrench inequality.

Critical Transit Infrastructure in Smart Cities and Urban Air Quality: A Multi-City Seasonal Comparison of Ridership and PM2.5

Sean Elliott, Sohini Roy · 2026-01-16

Transit ridership correlates with improved air quality, but the relationship varies significantly by city and season — there is no one-size-fits-all urban air quality benefit from transit.

Why It Matters

As cities invest in transit to meet climate targets, this paper shows that PM2.5 reductions from ridership are highly context-dependent, challenging simplistic policy assumptions.

Key Insights

  • City-specific baseline characteristics dominate the transit-air quality relationship more than ridership levels alone
  • Seasonal variation in transit use produces asymmetric PM2.5 effects across the four studied cities
  • New York shows the strongest correlation between transit scale and air quality improvement
  • Las Vegas and Phoenix exhibit weaker relationships, likely due to sprawl and lower baseline transit share
  • Integrated monitoring datasets combining transit and EPA data are a novel contribution to the field

Methodology

Integrated EPA air-quality monitoring (PM2.5) with agency-reported transit ridership for NYC, Chicago, Las Vegas, and Phoenix across two seasonal periods in 2024

Results

Pronounced structural differences in transit-air quality relationships across cities; seasonal effects differ by urban form and transit density

Limitations

Only four cities studied; 2024 data may not capture long-term trends; correlation does not establish causation

Practical Implications

Transit agencies should not assume ridership growth automatically improves local air quality; city-specific modeling is essential for credible climate co-benefit claims

Who Should Care

City planners, transit agencies, EPA policymakers, climate equity advocates

The Impact of Shared Autonomous Vehicles in Microtransit Systems: A Case Study in Atlanta

Jason Lu, Tejas Santanam, Hongzhao Guan, Connor Riley, Pascal Van Hentenryck · 2026-01-28

Autonomous shuttle integration into Atlanta's MARTA Reach microtransit program outperforms conventional on-demand services in service quality through dynamic demand-responsive repositioning.

Why It Matters

Demonstrates a real-world deployment path for autonomous vehicles that augments—rather than replaces—public transit, with measurable service quality gains.

Key Insights

  • Model predictive control repositioning reduces idle vehicle time and improves coverage of high-demand zones
  • SAV-enhanced microtransit shows superior user satisfaction scores vs. conventional dispatching
  • Integration with fixed-route MARTA lines is key to system efficiency
  • Dynamic repositioning is more impactful than simply adding autonomous vehicles
  • Atlanta's low-density suburban zones benefit most from the flexible shuttles

Methodology

Enhanced dispatching optimization model + model predictive control simulation using MARTA Reach pilot program data from Atlanta

Results

Autonomous shuttles outperform conventional on-demand services in service quality and user satisfaction metrics

Limitations

Single city case study; simulation-based results need real-world AV deployment validation; cost model not fully explored

Practical Implications

Transit agencies running microtransit pilots should prioritize dynamic repositioning algorithms before SAV procurement to maximize ROI

Who Should Care

Transit operators, mobility-as-a-service providers, urban planners in low-density suburban areas

Charge Schedule Optimization and Infrastructure Planning for Solar-Integrated Electric Bus Transit Systems

Madhusudan Baldua, Rito Brata Nath, Vivek Vasudeva, Tarun Rambha · 2025-04-29

Solar-integrated charging optimization can cut electric bus operating costs by up to 32% by jointly sizing battery storage and solar panels alongside dynamic charging schedules.

Why It Matters

Provides a practical computational framework that transit agencies can use to dramatically reduce the cost of electric bus transition while increasing renewable energy use.

Key Insights

  • Joint optimization of solar, battery storage, and charging schedules outperforms solving each independently
  • Benders decomposition enables computational tractability for real-scale transit networks
  • Cost reductions of 16.48% (Durham, ON) and 32.00% (Canberra) demonstrate cross-geography validity
  • Seasonal solar variability is a key constraint that naive charging schedules ignore
  • Dynamic electricity pricing integration is critical for maximizing savings

Methodology

Two-stage stochastic programming with Benders decomposition; validated on Durham (Ontario) and Canberra transit networks

Results

16.48% and 32.00% cost reductions respectively; model scales to real transit network size

Limitations

Assumes stable solar resource estimates; grid reliability and policy incentives not modeled; real-world installation logistics excluded

Practical Implications

Transit agencies planning electric bus fleets should co-plan solar and battery storage from the outset rather than retrofitting

Who Should Care

Transit fleet managers, energy planners, sustainability officers at transit agencies

Harnessing hybrid buses in the near term leads to faster transit decarbonization

Mahsa Arabi, Tolu Oke, Erin Baker, Jimi Oke · 2025-09-13

Under realistic budget and infrastructure constraints, a mixed electric-hybrid fleet achieves 6% more emissions reduction over 18 years than an all-electric strategy that stretches agency resources.

Why It Matters

Challenges the all-or-nothing narrative around electric bus mandates, showing pragmatic hybrid strategies can accelerate actual decarbonization for under-resourced agencies.

Key Insights

  • Hybrid buses enable faster retirement of old diesel fleets when charging infrastructure is limited
  • Under high-budget scenarios, mixed fleets achieve up to 45% lifecycle emissions reduction
  • All-electric mandates without infrastructure funding may backfire by delaying diesel retirement
  • Pioneer Valley Transit Authority used as empirical anchor for model validation
  • Budget constraints are the binding factor, not technology performance

Methodology

Optimization framework across 10 fleet transition scenarios; integrated energy and financial modeling for Pioneer Valley Transit Authority, MA

Results

Mixed electric-hybrid strategy yields 6% additional emissions reduction vs. all-electric; 45% reduction achievable under high-budget scenarios

Limitations

Single agency studied; future battery and hydrogen costs not modeled; political feasibility of hybrid approach not assessed

Practical Implications

Transit agencies with limited capital budgets should advocate for hybrid transition allowances in zero-emission mandates rather than fighting for exemptions

Who Should Care

Transit agency CEOs, state DOTs, federal transit policymakers, clean energy advocates

Can a Large Language Model Assess Urban Design Quality? Evaluating Walkability Metrics Across Expertise Levels

Chenyi Cai, Kosuke Kuriyama, Youlong Gu, Filip Biljecki, Pieter Herthogs · 2025-04-28

GPT-4 Vision can assess street-level walkability from imagery, but requires expert-structured prompts to avoid systematic score inflation.

Why It Matters

Scalable AI-based walkability assessment could transform city-wide pedestrian infrastructure auditing from expensive field surveys to rapid automated analysis.

Key Insights

  • MLLMs without expert knowledge prompting consistently over-rate walkability
  • Expert-knowledge integration significantly improves consistency and reduces variance in AI assessments
  • Street view imagery provides sufficient visual signal for pedestrian safety and attractiveness scoring
  • Interpretation errors persist even with improved prompting, limiting fully automated deployment
  • The approach scales to city-wide assessments impossible with manual surveys

Methodology

Structured expert knowledge prompting of ChatGPT-4 Vision using street view imagery; assessed pedestrian safety and attractiveness metrics

Results

Expert-prompted MLLMs show higher consistency; systematic upward bias identified in baseline model; models still make interpretation errors

Limitations

Only GPT-4 tested; geographic diversity of training data may cause bias; subjective walkability dimensions not fully captured

Practical Implications

Cities can use LLM-based walkability audits as a low-cost screening tool, but expert validation remains necessary for infrastructure investment decisions

Who Should Care

Urban planners, walkability researchers, city governments seeking scalable street audit tools

Forecasting MBTA Transit Dynamics: A Performance Benchmarking of Statistical and Machine Learning Models

Sai Siddharth Nalamalpu, Kaining Yuan, Aiden Zhou, Eugene Pinsky · 2025-12-02

For Boston MBTA forecasting, temporal features like day-of-week and season improve ridership prediction more than weather data, which often induces overfitting.

Why It Matters

Provides actionable model selection guidance for transit agencies building ridership and delay forecasting systems, with counterintuitive findings about weather variables.

Key Insights

  • Day-of-week and season are more predictive than weather for MBTA ridership
  • Weather features can reduce model performance through overfitting on temporal correlations
  • Self-exciting point processes offer a novel approach to delay prediction
  • 10+ model comparison provides a robust benchmarking baseline for the field
  • Seasonal features capture ridership patterns that weather proxies miss

Methodology

Benchmarking of 10-11 statistical and ML models including ARIMA, gradient boosting, neural networks; RMSE evaluation metric; novel self-exciting point process for delays

Results

Temporal features dominate predictive performance; weather reduces accuracy in most models; novel delay model outperforms baselines

Limitations

Single transit system (MBTA); findings may not generalize to sunbelt cities where weather effects are stronger; post-COVID ridership patterns may differ

Practical Implications

Transit data science teams should prioritize clean temporal feature engineering over complex weather data pipelines for ridership forecasting

Who Should Care

Transit data scientists, operations planning teams, real-time passenger information system developers

VR-digital twin integration enables intuitive participatory walkability assessment, allowing residents to evaluate pedestrian infrastructure before it is built.

Equity Impacts of Public Transit Network Redesign with Shared Autonomous Mobility Services

Max T.M. Ng, Meredith Raymer, Hani S. Mahmassani, Omer Verbas, Taner Cokyasar · 2025-01-03

Integrating shared autonomous mobility services into Chicago's transit network reduces access gaps for underserved communities, but only when equity is explicitly prioritized in network design.

Why It Matters

Shows that autonomous mobility won't automatically improve transit equity — intentional equity-focused network design is required to capture the benefits.

Key Insights

  • Cost-minimization network redesign may worsen equity even with SAMS integration
  • Explicit equity constraints in optimization produce meaningfully better outcomes for underserved areas
  • Suburban transit gaps see larger relative improvements than urban core gaps
  • Chicago case study provides a replicable equity-analysis framework for other major cities
  • SAMS as a first/last-mile solution requires policy mandates to reach equity goals

Methodology

Network optimization comparing cost-minimization vs. equity-prioritization approaches using Chicago transit data; SAMS integration modeled for both scenarios

Results

SAMS bridges suburban transit access gaps; equity gains visible only under equity-prioritized design; cost-focused design may increase disparities

Limitations

Paper was withdrawn for restructuring; Chicago-specific findings; cost of equity-prioritized design not fully analyzed

Practical Implications

Transit agencies adopting SAMS should require explicit equity metrics in procurement and network design RFPs, not just cost efficiency targets

Who Should Care

Transit equity advocates, metropolitan planning organizations, federal transit administrators, civil rights organizations

Revitalizing Public Transit in Low Ridership Areas: An Exploration of On-Demand Multimodal Transit Systems

Jiawei Lu, Connor Riley, Krishna Murthy Gurumurthy, Pascal Van Hentenryck · 2023-08-02

Replacing fixed low-ridership bus routes with on-demand multimodal shuttles in Austin, Texas improves service coverage and operational efficiency simultaneously.

Why It Matters

Provides a simulation-validated framework for the major strategic question facing smaller transit agencies: when to replace fixed routes with flexible demand-responsive service.

Key Insights

  • Demand-responsive shuttles outperform fixed low-ridership routes on both coverage and cost metrics
  • Multimodal integration (shuttle to fixed-route connections) is essential for network coherence
  • Austin's dispersed suburban areas are representative of hundreds of US metro areas
  • Simulation enables risk-free scenario testing before costly service restructuring
  • On-demand transit can serve areas where traditional fixed routes are economically unsustainable

Methodology

Agent-based simulation of on-demand multimodal transit in Austin, TX; compared against fixed-route baseline scenarios

Results

On-demand multimodal system outperforms fixed-route service in low-ridership areas on coverage, cost, and service quality

Limitations

Simulation-based; 2023 data; no real-world deployment validation; Austin demographics may not generalize

Practical Implications

Small and medium transit agencies should pilot on-demand service replacement on their lowest-performing fixed routes before full network redesign

Who Should Care

Small and regional transit agencies, state DOTs, rural mobility planners

International Economic Review (2025) High Confidence

Public Transit Access and Income Segregation

Prottoy Akbar, et al. · 2025-08-01

Transit improvements that maximize ridership reduce income segregation for slower transit modes, but faster transit improvements can paradoxically increase income segregation by enabling high-income suburbanization.

Why It Matters

Fundamentally challenges the assumption that all transit investment reduces inequality — the type and speed of transit matters enormously for social outcomes.

Key Insights

  • Slow transit improvements (local bus) reduce income segregation by improving mobility for low-income residents
  • Fast transit improvements (express rail) can increase segregation by enabling high-income residents to live farther from employment centers
  • Transit investment without housing policy can exacerbate gentrification and displacement
  • The ridership-maximization objective of most transit agencies may conflict with equity goals
  • Spatial economics framework reveals mechanisms hidden from ridership-only analyses

Methodology

Spatial economics model applied to transit access and income distribution data; examines fast vs. slow transit investment scenarios

Results

Slow transit reduces segregation; fast transit increases it; ridership-maximizing investments have ambiguous equity effects

Limitations

Modeling assumptions may not capture all behavioral responses; policy counterfactuals are theoretical

Practical Implications

Transit equity programs should specify which income groups benefit from investments and distinguish between local and express service expansions

Who Should Care

Metropolitan planning organizations, housing advocates, transit equity researchers, federal grant evaluators
