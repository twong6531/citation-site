Executive Summary

Today's transit research reveals a field in transition: empirical evidence from NYC's landmark congestion pricing program confirms that bold pricing interventions can drive transit ridership upward while reducing overall vehicle demand. Simultaneously, AI-driven planning tools are enabling more equitable service deployment in transit deserts, and new high-fidelity datasets from Brazil and California are expanding the methodological frontier. A recurring theme is the tension between aggregate mobility improvements and spatially uneven distributional effects—particularly for elderly, low-income, and peripheral urban populations.

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao — 2026

Emerging Themes
Pricing as a Transit Lever
Congestion pricing is now empirically validated as a dual-action tool that simultaneously reduces car travel and grows transit ridership. NYC's 2025 program is the first large-scale natural experiment in the US.
AI Planning for Equity
Machine learning and causal AI frameworks are being deployed to plan transit service in data-scarce, underserved areas where traditional demand signals don't yet exist. Risk-aware sequential optimization outperforms static planning.
Representational Bias in Data
GPS datasets and large mobility corpora systematically underrepresent elderly, low-income, and rural populations, distorting planning models in ways that compound existing inequities.
Contrarian Insights
⚡ Counterintuitive findings from today's papers
  • Larger AI models do not improve transit planning under data scarcity. A Qwen3-4B LLM performed no better than a simple first-order Markov chain for elderly trajectory generation when training data was limited.
  • NYC's congestion pricing transit gains extend beyond Manhattan's Congestion Relief Zone—suggesting that pricing policy has city-wide network effects that traditional zone-based analysis systematically misses.
  • Transit desert remediation performs best at moderate budget levels (+9.9pp at baseline), not maximum funding—risk-aware adaptive deployment outperforms full-budget static optimization.

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing
HIGH
Li, Zhuang, Li, Shen, Cao, Zheng, Wang, Zhao · arXiv econ.GN · 2606.17530 · 2026
NYC's first-in-nation cordon congestion pricing program significantly increased bus and subway ridership while modestly reducing overall travel demand, with spatially heterogeneous effects and transit gains extending beyond Manhattan's Congestion Relief Zone.
Key Insights
  • Bus and subway ridership increased significantly vs. no-policy counterfactual
  • Overall travel demand decreased modestly post-pricing
  • Transit gains extend beyond Manhattan's Congestion Relief Zone into outer boroughs
  • Socio-demographic analysis reveals uneven neighborhood-level adaptation
  • Time series foundation models enable credible counterfactual estimation without clean control groups
Methodology
Time series foundation models for probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasting applied to bus, subway, and aggregate trip volume data from NYC's January 2025 congestion pricing implementation.
Practical Implications
Provides a replicable evaluation framework for other cities; highlights need for equity monitoring by neighborhood; supports the case for congestion pricing as a transit-positive policy.

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty
HIGH
Khoroshevskaya, Perukari · arXiv cs.CY · 2606.08371 · 2026
A POMDP framework with CVaR financial constraints for deploying transit service in underserved areas achieves 53.6% desert remediation across 25 cities, outperforming static optimization by 5pp—with best gains at moderate, not maximum, budget levels.
Key Insights
  • Remediates median 53.6% of transit-desert tracts over 5 years across 25 US cities
  • Outperforms static optimization by 5.0pp on average; gains in 16 of 25 cities
  • Gains are LARGEST at moderate budget levels (+9.9pp at baseline)
  • Robust to 50% prior demand miscalibration
  • Population density and existing transit density are strongest cost predictors (R²=0.41)

HIGH
Wang, He, Zhou · arXiv cs.CY · 2606.31207 · 2026
Elderly riders have 19.4% smaller activity spaces and 56% lower mobility entropy—but models trained on majority-population data systematically misrepresent these patterns, and larger LLMs provide no benefit under demographic data scarcity.
Key Insights
  • Elderly activity spaces: 958m vs 1,189m for young riders (19.4% smaller)
  • Mobility entropy: 1.82 vs 4.15 (56% lower)
  • Majority-population models overestimate elderly step length by 4.5%
  • Qwen3-4B LLM performs no better than first-order Markov chain under data scarcity

Actionable Takeaways
01
Plan for spillover demand in peripheral neighborhoods
City planners considering congestion pricing should pre-position service capacity in peripheral neighborhoods to capture spillover demand that extends beyond the cordon zone. NYC's experience shows transit gains are a city-wide phenomenon.
02
Audit mobility datasets for demographic representation before deploying AI tools
Transit agencies should audit their mobility modeling datasets for demographic underrepresentation before deploying AI-driven planning tools. More capable AI models do not compensate for biased training data.
03
Use risk-aware (CVaR) deployment strategies for transit deserts
Transit desert planners should use risk-aware sequential deployment strategies rather than static optimization. A 5pp improvement over 5 years is achievable with POMDP + CVaR constraints—performance peaks at moderate, not maximum, budget levels.
Long-Term Implications

The convergence of behavioral pricing levers (congestion pricing), AI-assisted planning (transit deserts, e-scooter hubs), and high-resolution simulation datasets (SF-LIFE, NetMob26) is creating the technical infrastructure for a new era of data-driven urban mobility policy. The critical challenge remains: ensuring that AI-optimized systems do not inadvertently reproduce or deepen existing spatial and demographic inequities. The next frontier is integrating disaggregated demographic modeling into core transit planning pipelines—not as an afterthought, but as a foundational design requirement.
