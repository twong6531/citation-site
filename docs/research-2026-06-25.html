Executive Summary

Today's cluster is anchored by the first wave of credible empirical evidence on what happens when a major U.S. city actually reprices driving at scale: a study of New York's cordon-based congestion pricing program, live since January 2025, uses time-series foundation models to build calibrated counterfactual demand forecasts and finds that bus and subway ridership gains extend well beyond the priced Manhattan core, even as the steepest drop in overall travel demand stays concentrated inside the cordon. A companion NYC paper builds a socioeconomic-aware agent-based simulation of fare policy itself, finding fare-free buses deliver outsized equity benefits for lower-income riders relative to distance-based pricing, at only modest revenue cost. A second strand pushes transit-equity planning from after-the-fact evaluation into forward-looking decision-making under uncertainty: a risk-aware POMDP planner for transit-desert remediation across 25 cities beats static demographic scoring by 5 points on average even when its demand priors are badly miscalibrated, while a Manhattan case study of justice-informed autonomous mobility-on-demand finds that pairing AMoD with free public transit gets nearly all the way to a fully equitable system without sacrificing efficiency. A third strand is purely infrastructural and operational: a distributed SDN/edge-computing architecture pushes latency down for autonomous rail-pod (Pods4Rail) control, a new open-source pipeline (GROSS) finally brings reproducible nation-scale rail simulation to SUMO, a graph transformer beats stop-level tabular models at trip-level boarding/alighting prediction in Trondheim, and an LLM-based routing assistant (ChatPlanner) shows natural-language rider preferences can be extracted reliably enough to reshape an actual routing objective function.

Emerging Themes

Congestion pricing and fare policy are moving from theory to a live policy laboratory: NYC's deployed cordon program is now generating real before/after ridership data with spatial spillover effects, while a parallel simulation paper stress-tests fare-free and distance-based alternatives against the same city's socioeconomic geography.

Transit equity research is shifting from measuring outcomes after the fact to planning under uncertainty before the fact: risk-aware transit-desert remediation and justice-informed AMoD-plus-transit optimization both build decision frameworks that work even when demand or operator behavior can't be observed in advance.

Core transit engineering keeps quietly scaling: distributed edge-computing control for autonomous rail pods, open-source nation-scale rail simulation, trip-level sequential prediction, and LLM-driven preference-aware routing all push the operational substrate forward with little public attention.

Paper 01 · Paper of the Day · arXiv (2606.17530)

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
Using time-series foundation models to build calibrated counterfactual demand forecasts, the study finds New York's cordon congestion-pricing program produced real bus and subway ridership gains that extend well beyond the priced zone itself, while overall travel demand fell mainly inside it.
Why It Matters

Congestion pricing has been argued almost entirely from pre-deployment models; this is one of the first papers to evaluate the actual, live NYC program with a methodologically serious counterfactual, giving other cities considering similar tolls real before/after evidence instead of simulation alone.

Key Insights
  • NYC's January 2025 cordon-based congestion pricing program is evaluated using real post-deployment bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data rather than a pre-deployment model.
  • Time-series foundation models generate probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, addressing the lack of a clean control group for a city-wide policy.
  • Post-policy bus and subway ridership both show statistically meaningful gains relative to the modeled counterfactual.
  • Reductions in overall travel demand concentrate inside the Congestion Relief Zone, while transit ridership gains extend beyond Manhattan's core.
  • Socio-demographic analysis shows uneven adaptation across neighborhoods, with spatial equity implications for who benefits and who bears the cost.
Methodology

Time-series foundation models trained to produce probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, applied to real bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data before and after NYC's January 2025 congestion-pricing launch, with socio-demographic stratification of the results.

Results

Statistically meaningful, spatially extensive gains in bus and subway ridership following congestion pricing, paired with a more geographically concentrated drop in overall travel demand inside the priced zone, and clear socio-demographic unevenness in how neighborhoods adapted.

Limitations

Single-city, single-policy natural experiment with no true randomized control; counterfactual quality depends entirely on the foundation model's calibration; effects measured so far reflect only the early post-launch period and may evolve as behavior settles.

Practical Implications

Cities evaluating their own congestion-pricing proposals can point to NYC as a methodologically credible U.S. case showing real transit ridership gains beyond the cordon, while planning for uneven neighborhood-level adaptation rather than assuming uniform city-wide benefit.

Who Should Care

Congestion-pricing policy designers, transit agency planning teams, city DOTs evaluating road-pricing proposals, transportation economists.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond pre-deployment simulation studies of congestion pricing (London, Stockholm, Singapore precedents) by using a deployed U.S. program and a foundation-model-based counterfactual approach built specifically to handle the policy spillovers that defeat traditional control-group designs.

Paper 02 · arXiv (2606.21897)

Simulating Public Transit Fare Policies in NYC: An Efficient, Socioeconomic-Aware Framework

Parker Wischhover, Hossein Amiri, Kiara Ha, Jooyoung Yoo, Lina Li, Andreas Züfle, Joon-Seok Kim
A scalable agent-based simulation of NYC fare policy finds that fare-free bus programs substantially benefit lower-income riders by increasing bus use and cutting fare burden, with only modest revenue trade-offs.
Why It Matters

Fare-policy debates are usually driven by revenue projections alone; this gives agencies a socioeconomic-aware simulation tool to weigh equity gains against revenue trade-offs before committing politically and financially to a fare change, directly complementing today's real-world congestion-pricing evidence with a forward-looking fare-design tool.

Key Insights
  • Combines a synthetic population, agent-based simulation, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling.
  • Pricing changes modestly affect total ridership but significantly alter modal composition across the network.
  • Impacts of fare changes are heterogeneous across income groups rather than uniform city-wide.
  • Fare-free bus policy substantially increases bus usage and reduces fare burden for lower-income riders, with trade-offs in revenue.
  • A sampling-based approach reduces computational cost while preserving city-scale aggregate accuracy, making the framework practical for routine policy testing.
Methodology

Agent-based simulation built on a synthetic NYC population, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling, evaluated under multiple fare scenarios including distance-based pricing, fare increases, and fare-free buses.

Results

Fare-free bus policy delivers the largest equity benefit among scenarios tested, raising bus usage and cutting fare burden for lower-income riders, with modest revenue trade-offs; distance-based pricing and flat fare increases reshape mode share more than total ridership.

Limitations

Synthetic population and mode-choice assumptions may not capture all behavioral nuance; calibrated specifically to NYC, which has unusually dense, multimodal transit options not present in most U.S. cities.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies can use this kind of socioeconomic-aware simulation to pre-test fare changes for equity and revenue impact before politically committing to them, rather than relying on city-wide ridership-elasticity assumptions alone.

Who Should Care

Transit agency fare-policy planners, urban economists, equity researchers, city budget offices.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior fare-elasticity modeling by explicitly stratifying impacts by income group and integrating multimodal mode choice, rather than treating ridership as a single city-wide aggregate.

Paper 03 · arXiv (2606.21390)

Distance-based subsidy rate design to incentivize ride-hail access to advanced air mobility hubs

Zhenglei Ji, Hai Yang, Joseph Y. J. Chow
A NYC airport-access case study finds that advanced air mobility (AAM) operators would need to subsidize ride-hailing trips to vertiports whenever air-taxi operating costs exceed $12 per mile, with ridership and profit potential varying sharply across candidate Manhattan vertiport sites.
Why It Matters

Air-taxi networks cannot succeed without seamless first/last-mile ground access; this gives AAM operators and regulators a concrete subsidy-design tool and a cost threshold for when ground-access subsidies become financially necessary, rather than treating multimodal integration as an afterthought.

Key Insights
  • AAM success depends on effective integration with ground transport, particularly first- and last-mile ride-hail access to vertiports.
  • Proposes a distance-based subsidy rate design incorporating both AAM operator profitability and travelers' route choices.
  • Uses real-world high-volume for-hire vehicle (HVFHV) data from NYC taxi zones to model realistic spatial demand distributions.
  • AAM operators need to subsidize ride-hailing operators on vertiport-access trips once air-taxi operating costs exceed $12 per mile.
  • Ridership and profit contributions differ sharply across candidate vertiports in Manhattan, reflecting spatial demand heterogeneity.
  • Identifies which NYC taxi zones generate the highest passenger demand to all three major airports under lower air-taxi fare scenarios.
Methodology

Distance-based subsidy rate optimization model integrating AAM operator profitability and traveler value-of-time-based route choice, calibrated with real NYC high-volume for-hire vehicle (HVFHV) airport-access trip data.

Results

A clear cost threshold ($12/mile) above which AAM operators must subsidize ride-hail access trips, plus spatially heterogeneous ridership and profit outcomes across candidate Manhattan vertiport sites.

Limitations

Single-city (NYC) case study built around airport access specifically; assumes ride-hail and AAM operator behavior responds rationally to subsidy incentives; does not model competing ground-transit modes like subway or commuter rail access to vertiports.

Practical Implications

AAM operators and city regulators designing vertiport siting and subsidy policy can use the $12/mile threshold and site-specific demand findings to prioritize which vertiports need ground-access subsidies versus which are self-sustaining.

Who Should Care

Advanced air mobility operators, ride-hailing platforms, vertiport siting planners, urban air mobility regulators.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends prior AAM feasibility studies, which largely treated ground access as a fixed cost, by jointly optimizing subsidy design with operator profitability and rider route choice using real spatial demand data.

Paper 04 · arXiv (2606.15315)

ChatPlanner: A Large Language Model Framework for Personalized Public Transit Routing

Tingting Yang, Chenhao Xue, Jun Chen
A fine-tuned LLM combined with retrieval-augmented generation extracts nuanced rider preferences from natural-language queries and feeds them directly into a transit routing algorithm's objective function, surfacing valuable route alternatives that existing planners overlook.
Why It Matters

Most transit trip planners only optimize for time or transfers; this shows natural-language preference extraction ("avoid stairs," "fewer transfers even if slower," "well-lit stops at night") can be reliably converted into routing parameters, opening a path to genuinely personalized public-transit navigation at scale.

Key Insights
  • Employs fine-tuned LLMs with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to extract routing parameters and interpret nuanced user preferences from natural-language queries.
  • Preferences are integrated directly into the objective function of a public transit routing algorithm rather than handled as a post-hoc filter.
  • Designs preference-aware datasets incorporating eight personas and five contexts to establish scoring standards for both fine-tuning and RAG.
  • Three experiments validate solution feasibility, accuracy of routing-parameter extraction, and quality/completeness of the resulting route sets.
  • Fine-tuning enforces output structure and learns general preference patterns, while RAG resolves imprecise or conversational phrasing and calibrates continuous scores.
  • The combined fine-tuning plus RAG approach achieves the highest accuracy in both routing-information extraction and preference interpretation.
Methodology

Fine-tuned LLM augmented with Retrieval-Augmented Generation, trained on preference-aware datasets spanning eight rider personas and five contexts, with routing parameters fed into a public transit routing algorithm's objective function and validated through three separate experiments.

Results

ChatPlanner reliably generates feasible routing solutions, accurately extracts routing parameters and preferences from natural language, and surfaces route alternatives across different dimensions that existing route planners overlook.

Limitations

Validation relies on selected case studies and persona/context datasets designed by the authors rather than live deployment with real riders; generalization to dialects, languages, or edge-case preferences outside the eight personas is untested.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and trip-planner app developers can integrate LLM-based preference extraction to offer genuinely personalized routing (accessibility, safety, comfort) without building bespoke preference-elicitation UI for every rider need.

Who Should Care

Transit app developers, accessibility advocates, transit agencies building next-generation trip planners, conversational AI researchers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Goes beyond prior rule-based or slot-filling preference systems by using RAG to handle imprecise conversational language and by integrating preferences directly into the routing objective rather than as a post-processing filter.

Paper 05 · arXiv (2606.08371)

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty

Polina Khoroshevskaya, Ashish Kumar Perukari
A POMDP-based planner that updates latent demand beliefs as new service is deployed remediates a median of 53.6% of transit-desert tracts across 25 cities over five years, beating static optimization by 5 percentage points on average and holding up even when prior demand estimates are badly miscalibrated.
Why It Matters

Transit deserts are hard to plan for precisely because ridership can't be observed before service exists; this gives agencies a principled way to sequence transit-desert investment under that exact chicken-and-egg uncertainty, rather than relying on static demographic scoring alone.

Key Insights
  • Transit deserts affect tens of millions of residents across the Americas, and the standard ridership demand signal is unavailable before service exists.
  • Formulates risk-aware transit-desert remediation as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) with Conditional Value-at-Risk constraints for financial tail risk.
  • Uses demographic, land-use, and employment data to set a prior over latent demand, updated as new service deployments generate ridership observations.
  • A myopic belief-aware planner remediates a median of 53.6% of transit-desert tracts after five years across 25 cities, evaluated with a unified financial model covering operating cost, capex, fare revenue, and net subsidy.
  • Improves on static optimization by 5.0 percentage points on average, with gains in 16 of 25 cities, and the largest gains (+9.9 points) occur at moderate budgets.
  • Performance persists under 50% prior-demand miscalibration; population density and existing transit density are the strongest predictors of per-tract remediation cost (R²=0.41).
Methodology

Partially observable Markov decision process with Conditional Value-at-Risk financial constraints, using demographic/land-use/employment priors over latent demand updated via Bayesian belief revision as new service deployments produce ridership observations, evaluated across 25 cities with a unified financial model.

Results

A median 53.6% remediation rate of transit-desert tracts after five years, a 5.0-percentage-point average improvement over static optimization (up to +9.9 points at moderate budgets), and robustness to 50% prior-demand miscalibration.

Limitations

Myopic (not fully look-ahead) belief-aware planning may leave further performance on the table relative to a non-myopic POMDP solver; financial model is unified across cities and may not capture local capital-cost or labor-cost variation precisely.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies sequencing limited capital budgets for underserved areas can adopt belief-updating, risk-aware planning instead of one-shot static demographic scoring, particularly under tight or moderate budget constraints where the gains are largest.

Who Should Care

Transit agency capital planning teams, transit-equity researchers, city transportation departments, federal transit funding program designers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond static demographic-score-based transit-desert prioritization by explicitly modeling demand as latent and uncertain, and by sequencing investment as a belief-updating decision process rather than a one-time allocation.

Paper 06 · arXiv (2606.03282)

GROSS: German Rail Open-Source SUMO Scenario

Juri Penell, Damian Dailisan
A new open pipeline combines OpenStreetMap rail infrastructure with GTFS schedules to build nation-scale, simulation-ready rail scenarios for SUMO, cutting teleportation artifacts by 1.7–76.8× and enabling a full Germany-wide scenario with 35,925 trips.
Why It Matters

Open rail simulation has lagged far behind road-traffic simulation, leaving researchers without reproducible tools to study delay propagation and disruption at country scale; GROSS closes that gap and lowers the barrier for anyone to build their own national rail digital twin.

Key Insights
  • Open SUMO scenarios and toolchains remain road-traffic centric, leaving rail underrepresented despite its sensitivity to network-wide disruptions.
  • Combines OpenStreetMap railway infrastructure with GTFS schedules to generate nation-scale rail scenarios for SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility).
  • Existing geometry-only stop-to-track matching creates routing anomalies and unstable simulations dominated by teleportation artifacts; GROSS instead uses topology-aware stop mapping via a hierarchical station model.
  • Adds station-level routing with validation and targeted repair to catch and fix mismatches automatically.
  • Reduces average teleportations per vehicle by a factor of 1.7–76.8× across multiple German regions and shortens delays compared to the vanilla SUMO pipeline.
  • Enables end-to-end generation of a Germany-wide scenario with 35,925 trips, compared against operator-reported delay statistics.
Methodology

Open pipeline combining OpenStreetMap railway infrastructure extraction with GTFS schedule ingestion, topology-aware stop-to-track mapping via a hierarchical station model, and station-level routing with validation and targeted repair, benchmarked against the vanilla SUMO rail pipeline and operator-reported delay data.

Results

A 1.7–76.8× reduction in teleportation artifacts per vehicle, shorter simulated delays than the vanilla pipeline, and successful generation of a full Germany-wide 35,925-trip rail scenario.

Limitations

Remaining long delays in the simulation highlight limitations in available timetable metadata and rail dispatch modeling; validated primarily on the German rail network, so transferability to networks with different signaling and dispatch conventions is untested.

Practical Implications

Researchers and agencies studying rail delay propagation, disruption response, or timetable robustness at country scale can adopt GROSS instead of building bespoke, geometry-only rail scenario pipelines from scratch.

Who Should Care

Rail operations researchers, national rail infrastructure managers, SUMO/simulation tool developers, disruption-management planners.

Comparison to Prior Work

Directly improves on geometry-only stop-to-track matching used in prior open rail SUMO conversions, which the authors show produces routing anomalies and unstable, teleportation-dominated simulations.

Paper 07 · arXiv (2606.00572)

Spatiotemporal Multi-Task Graph Transformer for Trip-Level Transit Prediction

Oluwaleke Yusuf, Adil Rasheed, Frank Lindseth
SMT-GraphFormer reframes trip-level transit prediction as sequence-to-sequence modeling across a bus line's stop sequence, beating stop-level tabular benchmarks on Trondheim transit data and gaining +0.24 R² on alighting prediction by treating boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell time as jointly dependent tasks.
Why It Matters

Transit planners need boarding/alighting forecasts at the trip level, not just at isolated stops, to manage crowding, scheduling, and digital-twin scenario analysis; this shows that explicitly modeling a trip as an evolving sequence along the line captures dynamics that stop-by-stop tabular models miss.

Key Insights
  • Frames trip-level transit prediction as sequence-to-sequence modeling given a line's stop sequence and trip-level context.
  • Predicts successive boarding and alighting counts along the trip, with delay and dwell time treated as encoder-side surrogate tasks.
  • Uses graph embeddings for multi-relational stop similarity, a context encoder for weather and temporal information, and a multi-gate mixture-of-experts decoder for task-specific boarding/alighting representations.
  • Evaluated on public bus transit data from Trondheim, Norway, outperforming stop-level tabular benchmarks.
  • Ablation studies show the sequential formulation yields a +0.24 R² gain on alighting prediction and consistent improvements on boarding, delay, and dwell time.
  • Provides a horizon-agnostic basis for scenario analysis in digital-twin environments supporting transit planners and operators.
Methodology

Spatiotemporal multi-task graph transformer combining multi-relational stop-similarity graph embeddings, a weather/temporal context encoder, and a multi-gate mixture-of-experts decoder, trained as sequence-to-sequence prediction over a line's stop sequence and evaluated against stop-level tabular benchmarks on real Trondheim bus data.

Results

Outperforms stop-level tabular benchmarks across boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell-time prediction, with the largest gain (+0.24 R²) on alighting prediction, confirming the value of trip-level sequential modeling.

Limitations

Evaluated on a single city's (Trondheim) bus network; performance on rail or multimodal systems with different boarding/alighting dynamics is untested; relies on weather and temporal context features that may not be available in all transit data environments.

Practical Implications

Transit operators building digital-twin or crowding-management tools can adopt trip-level sequential models instead of stop-level tabular forecasting to get materially better alighting and boarding predictions for scenario analysis.

Who Should Care

Transit operations planners, digital-twin developers, transportation data scientists, bus and rail scheduling teams.

Comparison to Prior Work

Outperforms prior stop-level tabular forecasting approaches by explicitly modeling within-trip evolution and inter-target dependencies (boarding, alighting, delay, dwell) rather than treating each stop and target independently.

Justice-informed Planning of Intermodal Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems under Operational Constraints

Giacomo Ganassoli, Francesco Mazzeo, Cecilia Pasquale, Silvia Siri, Mauro Salazar
A Manhattan case study finds that monetary budgets sharply limit how much social justice an autonomous mobility-on-demand (AMoD) system can deliver as a for-profit transportation network company, but pairing AMoD with free public transit gets nearly all the way to a fully free, justice-optimal system without sacrificing standard efficiency metrics.
Why It Matters

Most AMoD/robotaxi planning research optimizes for revenue or average travel time, which tends to penalize the worst-off riders; this is one of the first papers to build operational network-flow models that explicitly account for rider budget and safety constraints and to show a concrete, low-cost policy lever (free transit) that gets most of the equity benefit.

Key Insights
  • Most transport-planning research optimizes revenue or utilitarian metrics like average travel time, which can penalize the worst-off riders for the sake of efficiency or profit.
  • Transport-justice research has focused on assessing injustices without prescribing operational solutions; this paper bridges that gap with optimization models for justice-informed operational planning.
  • Characterizes an intermodal AMoD system — where self-driving robotaxis provide on-demand mobility jointly with public transit and active modes — via mesoscopic network flow models.
  • Optimizes system operations through both utilitarian-efficiency and justice-informed objectives, accounting for rider budget and safety limitations and infrastructural capacity constraints.
  • Shows in a real-world Manhattan case study that monetary budgets significantly limit the social-justice potential of AMoD systems if deployed as for-profit transportation network companies.
  • Granting free public transit alongside AMoD achieves sufficiency levels very close to a fully free intermodal AMoD system, with justice-informed operations achievable without compromising standard efficiency metrics.
Methodology

Mesoscopic network-flow optimization models for an intermodal AMoD-plus-transit system, incorporating rider budget and safety constraints and infrastructural capacity limits, comparing utilitarian-efficiency and justice-informed objectives in a real-world Manhattan case study.

Results

Monetary budget constraints sharply cap the social-justice potential of for-profit AMoD deployment, but combining AMoD with free public transit nearly matches a fully free intermodal system's justice outcomes while preserving standard efficiency metrics.

Limitations

Single-city (Manhattan) case study with its own dense transit substitute network; mesoscopic network-flow modeling abstracts away some individual-level routing and matching dynamics that a fully microscopic AMoD simulation would capture.

Practical Implications

Cities regulating AMoD/robotaxi permits can treat pairing free or heavily subsidized public transit with AMoD deployment as a more effective and tractable equity lever than trying to directly regulate AMoD pricing or service-area mandates.

Who Should Care

AMoD/robotaxi regulators, transport-justice researchers, city transportation planning departments, public transit agencies negotiating with AMoD operators.

Comparison to Prior Work

Bridges transport-justice research (which historically assessed injustice without prescribing fixes) with operational AMoD planning research (which historically optimized efficiency without justice constraints), producing the first concrete operational justice-informed planning models for intermodal AMoD-plus-transit systems.

Implementation-Based Incentive Design for Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand and Transit Systems

Xinling Li, Runyu Zhang, Gioele Zardini
Instead of asking which municipal policy induces the best AMoD-transit equilibrium, this paper computes the minimum payment that makes deviating from a socially desirable operating profile unattractive for both AMoD and transit operators, revealing in a NYC case study how the dominant source of incentive misalignment shifts with congestion.
Why It Matters

Equilibrium-based regulation of selfish AMoD and transit operators requires strong, often unrealistic behavioral assumptions and creates equilibrium-selection headaches; this k-implementation-theory approach sidesteps that by directly computing tight, implementable payments, giving regulators a more tractable lever than trying to predict operator behavior.

Key Insights
  • Achieving a socially desirable operating point is hard when AMoD and public transit operators pursue selfish objectives alongside endogenous passenger choices.
  • Existing equilibrium-based regulation models search over municipal policies to predict induced operator equilibrium, creating strong behavioral assumptions and difficult bilevel optimization problems.
  • Proposes an implementation-based alternative: given a target operating profile, compute the minimum realized transfer that makes unilateral deviation unattractive for each operator, using k-implementation theory.
  • The payment decomposes into two unilateral deviation gains, one for the AMoD operator and one for public transit, requiring three computed objects: the social target, the AMoD best response, and the PT best response.
  • Derives tailored formulations for each: an entropy-regularized mixed-integer convex formulation for the social target, an exact reformulation plus convex relaxation and sequential convex approximation for the AMoD oracle, and a mixed-integer convex relaxation with characterized exactness conditions for the PT oracle.
  • A NYC case study computes tight implementation-payment bounds and reveals how the dominant source of incentive misalignment shifts as congestion changes.
Methodology

k-implementation theory applied to a network-flow model of intermodal AMoD-plus-transit operations, with tailored mixed-integer convex formulations, convex relaxations, and sequential convex approximations for computing the social target and each operator's best response, validated in a NYC case study.

Results

Computes tight, implementable minimum-transfer payment bounds that keep both AMoD and transit operators at a socially desirable operating point, and identifies how the dominant driver of incentive misalignment shifts as congestion levels change in the NYC case study.

Limitations

Relies on mixed-integer convex relaxations and approximations rather than exact global optimization for the AMoD oracle, which may leave an optimality gap; single-city case study limits generalizability of the specific congestion-driven misalignment findings.

Practical Implications

City regulators designing AMoD permitting or revenue-sharing frameworks can use implementation-based payment design as a more computationally tractable alternative to equilibrium-prediction-based regulation, particularly in congested networks where misalignment drivers shift.

Who Should Care

AMoD/transit regulatory economists, city transportation departments negotiating operator agreements, mechanism-design and network-optimization researchers.

Comparison to Prior Work

Departs from prior equilibrium-based AMoD-transit regulation models, which require predicting how operators respond to a policy, by instead directly computing the minimum payment needed to implement a desired outcome — avoiding equilibrium-selection and strong behavioral-assumption problems.

Distributed SDN-Based Communication Architecture for the Pods4Rail System

Dingyang Liu, Dereje Mechal Molla, Léo Mendiboure, Marion Berbineau
A distributed Software-Defined Networking and Multi-Access Edge Computing architecture for the Pods4Rail automated guideway pod system achieves lower controller communication and flow-setup latency than prior reported results, using hierarchical regional and edge controllers to keep pods running through local failover without central dependency.
Why It Matters

Autonomous rail-pod (PRT-style) networks live or die on communication latency at switch interlockings, where a pod must hand off control between trackside zones in real time; this shows a practical SDN/MEC architecture can meet that requirement while staying resilient to central-controller failure, a prerequisite for scaling automated guideway transit beyond pilot deployments.

Key Insights
  • Future multimodal transportation systems need reliable, low-latency communication infrastructure to coordinate autonomous vehicles and moving infrastructure across rail and road networks.
  • Traditional centralized control architectures struggle with latency, scalability, and adaptability in highly dynamic pod-network environments.
  • Proposes a distributed architecture integrating Software-Defined Networking (SDN) and Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) for a flexible, programmable, low-latency network.
  • Uses hierarchical control with regional and edge controllers to support low-latency interface management and edge autonomy.
  • Controller communication and flow-setup latency between edge SDN controllers and pods are measured lower than reported in prior literature.
  • Combines regional policy coordination with edge-level autonomy, enabling local failover and adaptive interface management without central dependency.
Methodology

Distributed SDN/MEC network architecture with hierarchical regional and edge controllers, evaluated against representative operational workflows and control-logic scenarios for the Pods4Rail automated guideway pod system, benchmarked on controller communication and flow-setup latency.

Results

Lower controller communication and flow-setup latency between edge SDN controllers and pods than reported in prior literature, with demonstrated local failover and adaptive interface management independent of central control.

Limitations

Evaluated through representative operational workflows and scenarios rather than a full nationwide or multi-vendor deployment; latency gains are benchmarked against prior literature values rather than a live head-to-head deployment comparison.

Practical Implications

Operators deploying automated guideway transit (PRT-style pod) systems can adopt hierarchical SDN/MEC communication architectures to meet real-time switch-interlocking latency requirements while gaining resilience to central-controller outages.

Who Should Care

Automated guideway transit system integrators, rail communications engineers, SDN/MEC network architects, transit infrastructure regulators.

Comparison to Prior Work

Improves on traditional centralized rail-pod control architectures by distributing intelligence to regional and edge controllers, directly addressing the latency and central-dependency limitations of prior centralized designs.

MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

Contrarian Insights

The NYC congestion-pricing paper complicates the simple "tolls push people onto transit" story: while transit ridership gains are spatially extensive, the steepest reduction in overall travel demand is concentrated inside the priced zone itself, meaning the policy's biggest effect may be suppressing total trips in the core rather than purely converting drivers into transit riders.

The justice-informed AMoD paper finds that pairing AMoD with free public transit gets a system almost all the way to fully equitable outcomes -- which implies that the marginal equity value of further regulating AMoD pricing or service areas, the focus of most current robotaxi policy debates, may be small compared to simply funding free transit alongside it.

The transit-desert remediation paper shows static, demographics-only prioritization -- the default approach in most agency capital planning -- underperforms a belief-updating planner by 5 points on average and by nearly 10 points at moderate budgets, suggesting that a large share of current transit-desert investment decisions are leaving measurable remediation on the table simply by not updating priorities as ridership data comes in.

Actionable Takeaways

Cities evaluating congestion-pricing proposals can cite NYC's deployed program as real-world evidence of transit ridership gains beyond the cordon, but should model the demand-reduction effect and the ridership-gain effect separately rather than assuming they are the same phenomenon in different neighborhoods.

Transit agencies weighing fare reform should treat fare-free bus programs as the highest-equity option per dollar of revenue given up, based on agent-based simulation evidence, before defaulting to distance-based pricing schemes that primarily reshape mode share rather than improve equity.

Agencies planning transit-desert investment should adopt belief-updating, risk-aware sequencing instead of static demographic scoring, particularly at moderate budget levels where the planning-method gap is largest.

City regulators drafting AMoD/robotaxi policy should evaluate pairing free or heavily subsidized public transit with AMoD permits as a primary equity lever, rather than concentrating regulatory effort on AMoD pricing caps or service-area mandates alone.

Operators deploying automated guideway pod systems facing switch-interlocking latency requirements should evaluate hierarchical SDN/MEC architectures, which demonstrate lower controller latency and resilience to central-controller failure than traditional centralized control.

Long-Term Implications

Today's papers mark a turning point from simulation-only congestion-pricing and equity research toward real deployed-policy evaluation and operational decision frameworks. NYC's early evidence -- real ridership gains that extend beyond the priced zone, paired with concentrated demand suppression inside it -- will likely become the reference case other U.S. and global cities cite as more congestion-pricing proposals move from theory to legislation, while the fare-policy simulation work gives those same cities a forward-looking tool to pre-test alternatives before committing politically. At the same time, transit-equity research is increasingly building uncertainty directly into the planning process rather than evaluating equity only after infrastructure exists: risk-aware transit-desert remediation and justice-informed AMoD-plus-transit optimization both suggest the next generation of equity-focused transit policy will be judged on how well it performs under demand and behavioral uncertainty, not just on its theoretical equity properties. Underneath both threads, the purely technical substrate -- distributed edge-computing rail-pod control, open nation-scale rail simulation, trip-level sequential prediction, and natural-language-aware routing -- keeps maturing quietly, and will increasingly be the infrastructure layer that determines whether these equity-aware policy designs can actually be operationalized at city scale.
