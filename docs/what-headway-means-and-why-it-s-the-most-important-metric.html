Headway: The Single Number That Defines Your Transit Experience

Ask a transit planner what matters most about a bus or train line and most will say headway — the time between consecutive vehicles. A route running every 10 minutes feels fundamentally different from one running every 45, even if both take the same amount of time to complete the trip.

Defining Headway Precisely

Headway is the scheduled time interval between the departure of one vehicle and the next on the same route at the same stop. A 15-minute headway means a bus leaves every 15 minutes; a 5-minute headway means trains arrive every 5 minutes. It is distinct from frequency (which is just the inverse: trips per hour) but the terms are often used interchangeably.

Headway determines how long a spontaneous rider waits. On a 10-minute headway route, a passenger arriving at a random time waits on average 5 minutes — half the headway. On a 60-minute rural bus, the expected wait is 30 minutes. This simple math explains why planners treat headway as the primary lever of service quality.

The Threshold Where Behavior Changes

Research by transit consultant Jarrett Walker and studies in cities from Zurich to Houston consistently find a threshold around 10–15 minutes. Below that interval, riders perceive the service as 'turn up and go' — they don't consult a schedule, they just walk to the stop. Above 15 minutes, riders start planning their trips around departure times, increasing the cognitive burden and making the service less convenient.

This threshold has real ridership consequences. When Houston Metro redesigned its network in 2015, it restructured routes to run frequently on a smaller grid rather than infrequently on many routes. Within a year, ridership on the redesigned corridors grew 20%, even before any infrastructure changes. The headway improvement alone drove the gains.

Headway vs. Speed and Reliability

Riders care about total travel time, which includes the wait. A fast bus running every 45 minutes may deliver worse real-world travel times than a slow bus running every 10, because the average wait for the fast bus is 22.5 minutes versus 5 minutes for the frequent one.

Bus bunching — two vehicles arriving close together after one falls behind schedule — effectively doubles the headway experienced by riders at intermediate stops. When a scheduled 10-minute headway collapses into one bus arriving after 18 minutes and another 2 minutes later, riders at the rear of the route wait almost as long as if the service ran every 20 minutes. This is why headway reliability matters as much as the scheduled interval.

How Agencies Set and Improve Headways

Headway decisions involve ridership demand, available vehicles, operator hours, and budget. Agencies typically publish standards: New York City Transit aims for 10-minute or better headways on major routes during peak hours; San Francisco SFMTA targets 12 minutes on core routes. Off-peak headways often stretch to 20 or 30 minutes due to lower demand and fleet constraints.

Improving headway without adding cost usually means consolidating routes. If four infrequent routes share a common corridor, merging them into two well-designed routes can double frequency on each. This is the core insight behind network redesigns in Houston, Columbus, and Austin: by accepting that not every neighborhood gets a direct route, agencies can give more riders access to frequent service.

Key Takeaways
  • Headway is the interval between vehicles; average wait time equals half the headway.
  • Service running every 10 minutes or less is perceived as 'turn up and go,' dramatically increasing usability.
  • Houston's 2015 network redesign increased ridership 20% on redesigned corridors through headway improvements alone.
  • Bus bunching can effectively double experienced headway even when scheduled headways are short.
