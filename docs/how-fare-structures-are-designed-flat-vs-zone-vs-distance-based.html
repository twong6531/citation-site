Fare Structures: How Transit Systems Decide What to Charge

How much should a bus ride cost? The answer varies dramatically by city: a single ride on New York City's subway costs the same whether you travel one stop or 25 miles, while London charges different fares depending on how many zones you cross. The choice of fare structure shapes who rides, how far they travel, and how much revenue the agency collects.

Flat Fares: Simple and Equitable

A flat fare charges every rider the same amount regardless of distance. New York City's $2.90 base fare applies whether you ride one station or the full length of the A train from Far Rockaway to 207th Street — over 31 miles. Chicago, Los Angeles, and most U.S. bus systems use flat fares.

Flat fares favor long-distance riders and penalize short-trip riders who might otherwise walk. They are easy to understand, fast to process (no distance calculation needed), and reduce the revenue impact of fare evasion since there's nothing to under-pay relative to trip length. Equity advocates often prefer flat fares because they don't penalize low-income commuters who live far from employment centers.

Zone-Based and Distance-Based Pricing

Zone-based fares divide a service area into concentric rings. A trip within one zone costs less than a trip crossing multiple zones. BART in the San Francisco Bay Area uses distance-based fares that range from $2.15 for a short trip to over $7.00 for the longest journeys. London's Oyster card zones 1–9 produce fares ranging from £2.50 for a single-zone trip to over £7.00 for the longest journeys.

Distance pricing recovers costs more accurately — longer trips consume more vehicle hours — but they create a price barrier for suburban commuters who already bear higher housing and transportation costs. Research on BART's fare structure found that outer-zone riders paid proportionally more of their income on fares than inner-city riders despite earning less on average.

Transfer Policies and Their Effects

How a system handles transfers between routes or modes fundamentally changes the perceived fare. New York City's MetroCard historically allowed free transfers between subway and bus within two hours, effectively subsidizing multimodal trips. When the city moved to OMNY contactless payment, the transfer policy was preserved — a decision that saved the average bus-to-subway commuter hundreds of dollars annually.

Some European systems charge a flat fare for unlimited transfers within a time window — 90 minutes in Zurich, 75 minutes in many German cities. This approach treats the transit system as a unified network rather than a collection of separate routes, simplifying pricing and encouraging network use.

Discounts, Passes, and Pricing Equity

Most agencies layer discount programs on top of their base fare structures: reduced fares for seniors, people with disabilities, students, and low-income riders. Chicago's Ventra card offers half-price rides to qualifying low-income adults. San Francisco's Lifeline Pass charges $45/month for unlimited rides for qualifying residents compared to the standard $100+ monthly pass.

Monthly and annual passes reward high-frequency riders with bulk discounts but require upfront payment that can be a barrier for low-income riders paid weekly. Some agencies have moved to income-based fare programs that calculate an affordable rate as a percentage of income — a model pioneered by Seattle's ORCA LIFT program, which charges $1 per trip to qualifying low-income riders.

Key Takeaways
  • Flat fares favor long-distance riders and are simpler to administer; distance fares recover costs more accurately.
  • BART charges $2.15–$7+ per trip by distance; New York charges $2.90 flat for any subway journey.
  • Transfer policies dramatically affect real-world fare burden for riders making multimodal trips.
  • Income-based fare programs like Seattle's ORCA LIFT charge qualifying riders $1/trip regardless of distance.
