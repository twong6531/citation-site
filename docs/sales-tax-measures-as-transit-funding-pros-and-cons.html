Sales Tax Transit Measures: How Voters Become Funders

Since Los Angeles County passed Proposition A in 1980, sales tax ballot measures have become the primary vehicle for expanding transit funding in the United States. More than 30 major transit systems now rely at least partly on dedicated sales taxes — a model with real advantages and real equity problems.

Why Sales Taxes Became the Dominant Model

Sales tax measures appeal to transit advocates and politicians for several reasons. They bypass the property tax, which faces intense political resistance in most American jurisdictions. They generate revenue proportional to economic activity rather than assessed property values. They can be proposed as time-limited measures with specific project commitments — 'vote yes to fund the Green Line extension' — giving voters something concrete to approve.

The political track record is strong. Measures tied to specific capital projects with clear benefits typically pass more easily than general operating levies. Los Angeles has passed four transit sales taxes since 1980, cumulatively generating billions annually and funding the construction of over 100 miles of rail.

Revenue Stability and Limitations

Sales tax revenue is volatile: it rises in economic booms and falls sharply in recessions. The 2008–2009 recession cut sales tax receipts for transit agencies in California, Georgia, and Colorado by 15–25%, forcing service cuts precisely when ridership demand typically holds steady or increases. The COVID-19 recession produced even sharper drops.

Most measures also restrict how revenue can be spent. A measure that earmarked funding for rail construction cannot be redirected to bus operations during a budget crisis, even if the agency desperately needs operating funds. This inflexibility has forced agencies to cut service elsewhere while the restricted capital funds accumulate.

Equity Concerns: Who Pays and Who Benefits

Sales taxes are regressive: lower-income households spend a higher percentage of their income on taxable goods than higher-income households, so they pay a larger proportional share of the tax. If the resulting transit investment primarily serves suburban commuter rail or park-and-ride facilities used by higher-income riders, the tax structure effectively transfers money from poor to rich.

Several California researchers have documented this pattern in LA Metro's sales tax investments, finding that early sales tax measures funded rail projects primarily serving wealthier westside corridors while bus service — used by lower-income riders — was held flat or cut. The Bus Riders Union's successful 1996 consent decree against Metro forced a reallocation toward bus service.

Alternatives and Reform Proposals

Some advocates argue for payroll taxes (which fund New York's MTA through the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation Mobility Tax) or property value capture as more stable and progressive alternatives. Payroll taxes vary with employment levels rather than consumer spending, providing somewhat more stability. Property value capture taxes residents of areas that directly benefit from transit investment.

No funding model is perfect. The most resilient systems combine multiple revenue streams — federal grants, fares, sales tax, payroll tax — to reduce dependence on any single source that might collapse in a downturn.

Key Takeaways
  • Sales tax measures have funded over 100 miles of LA Metro rail since 1980 and are now relied on by 30+ major transit systems.
  • Sales tax revenue falls 15–25% in recessions, forcing service cuts precisely when transit demand stays steady.
  • Sales taxes are regressive — lower-income households pay a higher percentage of their income into the tax.
  • New York's payroll mobility tax and property value capture offer more stable, progressive alternatives.
