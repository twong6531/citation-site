INTRO — ESTABLISHING STAKES (0:30–1:30)

In February 2026, a Brussels Commercial Court ordered Ryanair to stop showing fake seat scarcity warnings, promoting non-existent discounts, and hiding baggage charges during booking. [Source: Brussels Commercial Court, Feb 2026] The airline had been using what researchers call dark patterns — interface designs that work against the user's interests. What no one covered: transit agencies have been running the same playbook.

97% of popular websites use at least one dark pattern [Source: EU/BEUC Report, 2022]

SECTION 1 — DARK PATTERNS: A SHORT HISTORY (1:30–3:00)

The term "dark pattern" was coined by UX designer Harry Brignull on July 28, 2010. [Source: Wikipedia/Dark_pattern] His taxonomy identified types like "roach motel," "drip pricing," and "confirmshaming." By 2019, a Princeton study of 11,000 shopping sites found 1,818 distinct instances. The FTC began enforcement in 2021. Epic Games was fined $245 million in March 2023. [Source: FTC, March 2023] None of that enforcement touched public transit.

SECTION 2 — FIVE PATTERNS TRANSIT AGENCIES USE (3:00–5:00)

Pattern 1: The Login Wall. San Francisco Muni's official app requires account creation before showing live departures. Your email and travel history are data. Third-party ticketing partnerships require identifiable users. You are the product.

Pattern 2: The Fractured App Ecosystem. The MTA operates separate apps for the subway, LIRR, and Metro-North. Three apps. One agency. No unified planner. Consolidation would threaten vendor contracts.

Pattern 3: The Buried Fare Calculator. LA Metro's fare calculator requires you to choose your fare category before it shows you any price. Uncertainty keeps riders from discovering reduced-fare eligibility. That saves the agency money.

Pattern 4: Real-Time Data Paywalled by Default. Several agencies publish GTFS feeds but throttle the API or require paid developer keys. Third-party apps like Transit and Moovit often have better real-time data than the official agency app, because they've paid for priority access. [Source: Wikipedia/Transit_app]

Pattern 5: Buried Accessibility Features. Step-free route planning, elevator outage alerts, and audio-navigation features are typically buried in settings menus or missing from official apps entirely.

SECTION 3 — WHO'S DOING IT RIGHT (5:00–7:00)

In September 2019, Berlin's BVG launched Jelbi — described at launch as the world's first large-scale Mobility-as-a-Service platform. [Source: Wikipedia/Berliner_Verkehrsbetriebe, 2019] One app. U-Bahn, tram, bus, bike-share, scooter, carsharing. Live arrivals, no login required for basic journey planning. BVG serves 400 million passengers a year. Amsterdam's GVB operates similarly. The contrast isn't a technology gap — it's an incentive gap.

A Rider-First App Charter would require:

  • Live departures accessible without login
  • Unified journey planning across all modes under one agency
  • Reduced-fare eligibility surfaced proactively, not buried
  • Real-time elevator/escalator status on default screen, not settings
  • GTFS-RT feeds public and unthrottled for third-party use
