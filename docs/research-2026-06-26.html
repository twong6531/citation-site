Executive Summary

Today's batch of transit research splits cleanly into two currents: a wave of papers using causal inference and reinforcement learning to put hard numbers on long-debated operational questions (plainclothes vs. uniformed fare inspection, dynamic multimodal pricing, NYC congestion pricing's real ridership effect), and a wave testing whether AI — LLMs, agentic systems, learned routing models — can replace or augment classical transit engineering tools, from line planning to fare-evasion detection to map-free route generation. A throughline across both currents is a willingness to challenge default assumptions: the 'exact' line-planning model loses to a compact approximation at scale, the intuitively-deterrent uniformed inspector turns out to be the less effective one, and average trip experience turns out not to be what riders remember — the worst moment and the ending dominate. Several papers (the electric-bus aggregator study, the AMoD incentive-design paper) flag that introducing intelligent automation into transit operations creates new principal-agent risk: the same optimization capability that helps an agency can also be configured to extract value from it.

Emerging Themes

Causal and counterfactual rigor is replacing intuition in transit policy evaluation — fare inspection visibility, congestion pricing's ridership effect, and fare-free bus equity impacts are now being settled with causal ML and foundation-model counterfactuals rather than theory or small pilots.

AI agents are moving from route recommendation into operational control — reinforcement learning agents now set prices and incentives, and agentic supervisory layers manage electric bus fleets and V2G trading in real time, raising new governance and value-extraction questions.

Classical OR transit models are being stress-tested at real-world scale and several are failing — the canonical line-planning network and a few traditional resilience-scoring approaches don't hold up against compact approximations and multi-dimensional KPI frameworks once tested on real national networks and disruption case studies.

Transit equity is increasingly measured with socioeconomic disaggregation built in from the start — fare simulations, congestion pricing evaluations, and transit-desert remediation models all explicitly track distributional effects rather than reporting aggregate ridership/efficiency numbers alone.

Paper 01 · Paper of the Day · arXiv (2606.24386)

Line Planning at Scale: Models, Methods, and Insights

Rolf van Lieshout, Bart van Rossum
Across 972 test cases on real Dutch and Swiss rail networks, the textbook-standard exact transfer model for line planning loses to a simpler, compact approximation as soon as networks get large.
Why It Matters

This challenges decades of operations-research convention: the 'change-and-go network' is taught as the accurate way to model line planning, but the paper shows it's often unsolvable at real-world scale, while a less precise but compact model reliably wins.

Key Insights
  • The change-and-go network (CGN) models passenger transfers exactly but its size explodes combinatorially with network size.
  • Three more compact alternative models approximate transfers differently, trading some accuracy for tractability.
  • Tested on 972 instances derived from the Dutch and Swiss national rail networks — a large, realistic benchmark.
  • The CGN is competitive only on small/easy instances and frequently fails to find any feasible solution at scale.
  • A compact 'direct connection' model finds the best solution on over 83% of instances despite its theoretical inaccuracy.
Methodology

Comparative computational study of four line-planning formulations (the canonical CGN plus three compact approximations), each paired with custom solution methods, benchmarked across 972 instances built from Dutch and Swiss railway network data.

Results

The compact direct-connection model outperforms the theoretically more accurate CGN on the large majority of instances, and the CGN often fails entirely on large networks.

Limitations

Benchmarks are drawn from two specific European rail networks; transferability to bus-heavy or grid-based networks (vs. national rail) is untested.

Practical Implications

National and regional rail planners should reconsider defaulting to 'exact' transfer-modeling tools for large-network line planning and instead adopt validated compact approximations that actually return solutions.

Who Should Care

Rail network planning agencies, OR practitioners building line-planning software, and academics teaching transit network design.

Comparison to Prior Work

Directly contradicts the implicit consensus in the line-planning literature that the change-and-go network is the gold-standard formulation, by showing its practical failure rate at scale.

Paper 02 · arXiv (2606.27196)

Optimizing Accessibility and Maximum Travel Times in Transit Networks: The Hub Covering Location Problem

Carmen-Ana Domínguez-Bravo, Elena Fernández, Armin Lüer-Villagra
A new hub-location optimization model balances how far riders must travel to reach a transit access point against how fast the trunk network gets them where they're going.
Why It Matters

Most transit-network design tools optimize coverage or speed separately; this model formalizes the trade-off between first/last-mile access time and trunk-line speed in a single mixed-integer program, giving planners a tunable lever instead of a fixed assumption.

Key Insights
  • Trips are split into a short 'access' segment (walk/bike/drive to a hub) and a longer 'trunk' segment, each with its own time cap.
  • Only riders within a defined coverage radius are assumed to use the system at all — coverage is binary, not probabilistic.
  • Two exact MILP formulations are proposed alongside a matheuristic for when exact solving is too slow.
  • Tightening the trunk travel-time limit forces hub consolidation; loosening the access limit spreads hubs outward.
  • Computational experiments show the matheuristic recovers near-optimal solutions far faster than the exact formulations on larger instances.
Methodology

Two mixed-integer linear programming formulations for hub-covering location, solved exactly on small/medium instances and via a custom matheuristic on larger ones; sensitivity analysis sweeps the access and trunk time-limit parameters.

Results

The matheuristic matches or closely approaches optimal MILP solutions at a fraction of the runtime, and parameter sweeps reveal clear, interpretable structural shifts in hub placement as coverage and trunk-time limits change.

Limitations

The model assumes static, deterministic travel times and a fixed coverage radius rather than a continuous probability of use; it is also tested on synthetic/benchmark instances rather than a live transit agency's real demand data.

Practical Implications

Agencies designing or restructuring hub-and-spoke networks (e.g., regional rail-plus-feeder-bus systems) get a quantitative way to set access-time standards before committing to trunk infrastructure, rather than guessing at walk-shed assumptions.

Who Should Care

Transit network planners, OR/optimization researchers, and agencies considering hub-and-spoke or timed-transfer redesigns.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends classical hub-covering location models (long studied in logistics/airline networks) into the transit-access context by explicitly separating and bounding access vs. trunk travel time, rather than treating coverage as a single radius.

Paper 03 · arXiv (2606.27077)

Urban Context and Travel Experience Events: An Exploratory Comparison of Two German Cities

Marie Güntert, Esther Bosch
Real-time, in-trip surveys of 91 riders in Hamburg and rural Tuttlingen show that the single biggest driver of a good transit experience differs sharply by urban context — capacity in the small town, punctuality and information in the big city.
Why It Matters

It's a rare direct, same-method comparison of urban vs. rural transit experience, undercutting the assumption that a single 'customer experience' framework applies everywhere.

Key Insights
  • In rural Tuttlingen, sufficient vehicle capacity had the strongest positive effect on trip experience — crowding read as scarcity, not just discomfort.
  • In Hamburg, punctuality failures and negative real-time information events hit experience hardest.
  • Personal well-being independently affected rated experience in the rural sample, beyond service factors.
  • Multi-level regression on real-time, geo-tagged event data captured in-the-moment reactions rather than recalled summaries.
  • Sample sizes are small (21 rural, 70 urban participants), limiting statistical power for subgroup claims.
Methodology

Mobile-app-based experience sampling: 91 total participants logged everyday trips with real-time situational ratings; multi-level regression models linked specific events (punctuality, capacity, information) to on-trip experience scores.

Results

Capacity offer dominates in the rural city; punctuality and information events dominate in the urban one — the ranking of what matters most flips between contexts.

Limitations

Small, non-random samples (especially the 21-person rural group); two German cities only, limiting generalizability to other transit cultures or fare/service models.

Practical Implications

Agencies should not import a one-size-fits-all customer-experience scorecard from a peer city; rural/low-frequency systems may get more rider-satisfaction return from added capacity than from on-time-performance investments, and vice versa in dense urban systems.

Who Should Care

Transit customer-experience teams, rural/regional operators, and researchers studying context-dependent service quality.

Comparison to Prior Work

Most travel-satisfaction research aggregates post-trip recall surveys across mixed contexts; this study's in-the-moment, geo-located event tracking and explicit urban-rural contrast is a methodological step beyond that norm.

Paper 04 · arXiv (2606.26400)

When Agents Meet Electric Bus Fleet Operations: Pricing Behavior, Trade-offs, and Policy Implications in an Aggregator Framework

Jônatas Augusto Manzolli, Ali Eslami, Luis Miranda-Moreno, Jiangbo Yu
An 'agentic' AI layer that supervises electric bus scheduling, charging, and vehicle-to-grid trading can keep fleets feasible under disruptions — but if tuned to maximize profit, it siphons financial value away from the public transit operator.
Why It Matters

This is a concrete, quantified warning about a fast-emerging pattern: LLM/agent-based 'aggregator' middleware sitting between public infrastructure and private optimization incentives, with measurable distributional consequences.

Key Insights
  • The framework couples a physics-constrained optimization core (routes, batteries, chargers, V2G) with supervisory agents that detect disturbances and trigger re-optimization.
  • Day-ahead and real-time coordination modes were tested under service delays, route-energy deviations, and electricity price shocks.
  • Profit-oriented tariff configurations extract value from the public transport operator even while keeping schedules technically feasible.
  • Operation-based (rather than profit-based) coordination modes preserve more value for the public operator.
  • The same agentic capability is dual-use: efficient under one incentive design, extractive under another.
Methodology

Case study of a realistic electric bus depot combining a mixed-integer optimization scheduling core with supervisory disturbance-detection and tariff-adaptation agents, evaluated across day-ahead/real-time and profit-based/operation-based coordination scenarios.

Results

Agentic aggregation maintains feasible schedules and improves charging/V2G flexibility utilization, but profit-based pricing configurations transfer value from the public operator to the aggregator even under otherwise 'successful' operation.

Limitations

Single depot case study; results depend on the specific tariff and disturbance scenarios modeled, and real-world aggregator contracts may differ from the profit/operation-based dichotomy tested.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies adopting third-party AI fleet-management or V2G aggregation services should demand auditable tariff-setting and explicit value-sharing terms before signing on, not just feasibility guarantees.

Who Should Care

Electric bus fleet operators, transit agency procurement and finance staff, V2G/grid-services vendors, and policymakers drafting agentic-AI procurement rules.

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior electric bus scheduling work optimizes for feasibility or cost minimization alone; this paper is among the first to model the agentic supervisory layer itself as a strategic actor with its own incentive-driven behavior.

Paper 05 · arXiv (2606.24181)

Visible or Covert? The Causal Effect of Inspector Visibility on Fare Evasion Detection: A Causal Machine Learning and Policy Learning Approach

Hannes Wallimann, Cédric Brütsch, Martin Huber
Analyzing 21,727 real fare-inspection records from Switzerland's largest regional bus operator, plainclothes inspectors catch about 26% more fare evaders per hour than uniformed ones, and an optimal policy would deploy plainclothes inspection almost everywhere.
Why It Matters

Fare enforcement strategy is usually set by intuition or visibility-as-deterrence theory; this is a rigorously causal, large-sample answer that contradicts the common assumption that visible uniformed presence is the better deterrent/detection strategy.

Key Insights
  • Causal machine learning isolates the effect of inspector visibility (uniformed vs. plainclothes) from confounding factors like line, time, and location.
  • The average treatment effect is -0.173 detected evaders per hour for uniformed vs. plainclothes — a 26% relative reduction in detection efficiency for uniformed inspection.
  • Policy-tree-based optimal targeting recommends plainclothes inspection in 83.3% of contexts.
  • Uniformed inspection is only preferred on lines with below-median share of foreign residents and above-median population — a narrow, specific carve-out.
  • No evidence of broad heterogeneity: the plainclothes advantage is robust and pervasive across the network, not concentrated in a few corridors.
Methodology

Causal machine learning (treatment effect estimation) on 21,727 real inspection records from PostAuto, Switzerland's largest regional bus operator, followed by policy learning via optimal policy trees to derive context-specific deployment recommendations.

Results

Plainclothes inspections detect significantly more fare evaders per hour than uniformed inspections, and an optimal policy would assign plainclothes inspection to the vast majority of route/time contexts.

Limitations

Single operator and country (Switzerland); detection efficiency is not the same as overall deterrence — uniformed presence may still reduce evasion attempts even if it catches fewer per hour, an effect this design cannot fully separate.

Practical Implications

Agencies weighing fare-enforcement staffing models have direct, causal evidence to shift inspection mix toward plainclothes deployment for detection efficiency, while being mindful that visible deterrence and detection are different goals.

Who Should Care

Transit fare-enforcement program managers, revenue protection teams, and policymakers concerned about equity in enforcement targeting.

Comparison to Prior Work

Most fare-evasion literature relies on observational correlations or survey-based deterrence theory; this is a large-sample causal-ML study with an explicit optimal-policy recommendation, a more rigorous standard of evidence.

Paper 06 · arXiv (2606.23257)

Dynamic multi-agent deep reinforcement learning-based pricing and incentivization approach in multimodal transportation networks

Khadidja Kadem, Mostafa Ameli, Carlos Lima Azevedo, Mahdi Zargayouna, Latifa Oukhellou
A two-agent reinforcement learning system, one representing a public authority and one a shared-mobility provider, learns dynamic pricing that cuts commuter costs ~20% and emissions ~10% while nearly doubling public transit profit during the morning peak.
Why It Matters

It's a concrete simulation showing that coordinated (not just competing) dynamic pricing between public transit and shared-mobility providers can hit multiple policy goals — equity, emissions, revenue — simultaneously rather than trading them off.

Key Insights
  • Two RL agents represent conflicting real stakeholders: a public authority optimizing equity/emissions/efficiency, and a private SMS provider optimizing revenue.
  • Public-transport incentives are allocated dynamically in space and time rather than as flat subsidies.
  • Combining public incentives with dynamic SMS fares lets both objectives improve rather than directly trading off.
  • Tested specifically over a three-hour morning peak window with evolving congestion and demand.
  • Public transport profit nearly doubles under the dynamic incentive scheme relative to baseline.
Methodology

Multi-agent deep reinforcement learning simulation with two interacting RL agents (public authority and SMS provider) operating over a multimodal transportation network during a simulated morning peak period.

Results

Commuter costs fall ~20%, emissions fall ~10%, public transit profit nearly doubles, and benefit distribution becomes more equitable, compared to static pricing baselines.

Limitations

Simulation-only results over a single peak window and network configuration; real-world deployment would require live demand-responsive infrastructure and stakeholder buy-in that the model doesn't test.

Practical Implications

Provides a quantified, simulation-backed case for cities to pilot coordinated dynamic incentive programs between transit agencies and shared-mobility operators rather than regulating each independently.

Who Should Care

Mobility-as-a-service policymakers, transit agency revenue and partnerships teams, and shared-mobility operators evaluating subsidy/incentive partnerships.

Comparison to Prior Work

Builds on single-agent dynamic pricing and static subsidy literature by modeling the bidirectional, adaptive interaction between public and private actors directly, rather than assuming one side is fixed.

Paper 07 · arXiv (2606.21897)

Simulating Public Transit Fare Policies in NYC: An Efficient, Socioeconomic-Aware Framework

Parker Wischhover, Hossein Amiri, Kiara Ha, Jooyoung Yoo, Lina Li, Andreas Züfle, Joon-Seok Kim
A city-scale agent-based simulation of NYC fare policy finds that fare-free bus programs meaningfully help low-income riders' wallets and ridership while costing little in total ridership decline elsewhere — but at a real revenue trade-off.
Why It Matters

It gives transit agencies a sampling-based, computationally cheap simulation tool to test fare-equity policy scenarios (fare-free bus, distance-based pricing, fare hikes) before committing politically and financially.

Key Insights
  • Synthetic population plus agent-based simulation plus fare-sensitive mode choice modeling produces socioeconomically disaggregated impact estimates, not just aggregate ridership numbers.
  • Pricing changes shift modal composition more than they shift total ridership.
  • Fare-free bus policy disproportionately benefits lower-income riders via increased bus usage and reduced fare burden.
  • A new sampling-based approach cuts computational cost for city-scale simulation while preserving aggregate accuracy.
  • Equity gains under fare-free bus policy come with explicit revenue trade-offs, not a free lunch.
Methodology

Scalable agent-based simulation combining a synthetic population of NYC residents, multimodal travel-time estimation, and fare-sensitive mode-choice modeling, evaluated against multiple fare policy scenarios with a sampling-based scalability technique.

Results

Fare-free bus policies substantially increase low-income bus ridership and reduce fare burden, at a cost to operator revenue; distance-based pricing and fare hikes mainly reshuffle mode share rather than reduce total trips.

Limitations

Calibrated to NYC's specific socioeconomic geography and existing multimodal network; mode-choice model assumptions (fare sensitivity parameters) drive results and may not transfer cleanly to other metro areas.

Practical Implications

Gives fare-policy decision-makers a defensible, equity-disaggregated quantitative basis for fare-free or distance-based pilot proposals instead of relying on small pilot studies or advocacy estimates alone.

Who Should Care

Transit agency fare-policy planners, city transportation equity offices, and researchers building transportation digital twins.

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond prior aggregate-ridership fare-elasticity studies by explicitly disaggregating impacts by socioeconomic group at city scale, while keeping the simulation computationally tractable via sampling.

Paper 08 · arXiv (2606.17530)

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
Using time-series foundation models to build a probabilistic counterfactual for NYC's first cordon congestion-pricing program, researchers find bus and subway ridership rose significantly relative to the no-policy baseline, but the travel-demand benefits are spatially uneven across neighborhoods.
Why It Matters

It's a real, large-scale, real-world natural experiment evaluation — not a simulation — of America's first congestion-pricing program, using a novel uncertainty-aware counterfactual method that gets around the classic 'no clean control group' problem for city-wide policy evaluation.

Key Insights
  • Time-series foundation models generate calibrated, probabilistic counterfactual demand forecasts where no clean control group exists.
  • Post-policy bus and subway ridership rose significantly above the no-policy-expected baseline.
  • Overall (all-mode) travel demand fell only modestly.
  • Reductions in overall travel demand concentrate inside the Congestion Relief Zone, but transit ridership gains extend beyond Manhattan's core.
  • Socio-demographic analysis shows uneven adaptation across neighborhoods — equity implications, not just an aggregate win.
Methodology

Time-series foundation models applied to real bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data from NYC's January 2025 cordon congestion-pricing rollout, generating probabilistic counterfactual (no-policy) demand forecasts for causal comparison.

Results

Transit ridership gains are real and statistically significant and geographically broader than the zone itself, while travel-demand reductions are concentrated and demand adaptation is socio-demographically uneven.

Limitations

Counterfactual forecasting, however well-calibrated, is still a model-based substitute for a true control group; early post-policy data (months, not years) may not capture longer-run adaptation.

Practical Implications

Other US cities considering congestion pricing (and the methodology itself) get real-world validation that transit ridership gains materialize quickly and broadly, while flagging that equity monitoring of who benefits is necessary alongside aggregate success metrics.

Who Should Care

City and state DOT policymakers evaluating congestion pricing, MTA and other large transit agencies, and transportation economists studying real-world policy evaluation methods.

Comparison to Prior Work

Earlier congestion-pricing evaluations (London, Stockholm, Singapore) relied on simpler before/after or comparison-city designs; this paper's foundation-model counterfactual approach is a methodological advance suited to settings with no clean control group.

Paper 09 · arXiv (2606.15315)

ChatPlanner: A Large Language Model Framework for Personalized Public Transit Routing

Tingting Yang, Chenhao Xue, Jun Chen
ChatPlanner combines fine-tuned LLMs with retrieval-augmented generation to translate conversational preferences ('I hate transfers and want a scenic route') into transit routing parameters, surfacing route options that standard planners miss.
Why It Matters

Existing transit routers (Google Maps, Citymapper) optimize generic objectives like fastest/fewest-transfers; this shows a viable architecture for genuinely personalized routing driven by natural language rather than checkbox filters.

Key Insights
  • Eight rider personas and five contextual scenarios were designed to build preference-aware training and evaluation datasets.
  • Fine-tuning enforces structured output and learns general preference patterns; RAG resolves imprecise/conversational phrasing and calibrates continuous preference scores.
  • The combination of fine-tuning and RAG outperforms either approach alone on routing-parameter extraction accuracy.
  • Case studies show ChatPlanner surfaces valuable route alternatives that existing route planners overlook entirely.
  • Three separate experiments validated feasibility, extraction accuracy, and solution-set quality/completeness.
Methodology

Fine-tuned LLM combined with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to extract routing parameters from natural-language queries, integrated into a transit routing algorithm's objective function; evaluated via persona/context-based test datasets across three experiments.

Results

ChatPlanner reliably produces feasible routes, accurately extracts preferences from conversational input, and generates route alternatives that existing planners miss, with fine-tuning plus RAG outperforming either alone.

Limitations

Evaluation relies on constructed personas/contexts rather than live user studies; no reported testing against real-world transit API latency or live disruption data.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and trip-planning app developers have an architectural blueprint for adding natural-language personalization on top of existing routing engines without rebuilding the optimization core.

Who Should Care

Transit app/trip-planner developers, agencies investing in rider-facing digital tools, and accessibility advocates interested in preference-driven routing (e.g., for mobility-limited riders).

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior personalized-routing work typically hand-codes preference weights or uses limited structured surveys; this is among the first to use LLM+RAG to interpret open-ended natural language preferences directly into a routing objective.

Paper 10 · arXiv (2606.08849)

A Resilience-as-a-Service assessment framework for coordinated disruption response in interdependent urban transit systems

Sara Jaber, S. M. Hassan Mahdavi, Neila Bhouri, Mostafa Ameli
Tested on Paris's RER B line, a multi-dimensional KPI framework shows that coordinated multimodal disruption response beats any single-mode fallback on overall resilience, cost, and equity — while flagging the often-ignored 'helper line' service degradation that coordination causes elsewhere.
Why It Matters

Most resilience studies score disruption response with one number; this is a structured, multi-KPI accounting that explicitly captures the hidden cost of cross-line bus/rail substitution — vehicles pulled from helper lines degrading service there.

Key Insights
  • Resilience is evaluated across eight complementary dimensions: vulnerability, adaptability, robustness, resilience loss, responsiveness, cost, emissions, and equity — not collapsed into one score.
  • The framework explicitly models secondary service degradation on 'helper lines' when vehicles are withdrawn to support a disrupted corridor — a cost most resilience studies omit.
  • Combines an optimization model for the response strategy with agent-based simulation for behavioral/passenger-level evaluation.
  • Coordinated multimodal response achieves the most balanced resilience profile: high service continuity, lower total disruption cost than single-mode alternatives, improved equity, competitive emissions.
  • Sensitivity analysis identifies the specific disruption severity/duration conditions where coordination's added value is largest.
Methodology

KPI-driven, time-indexed framework combining an optimization model for disruption-response strategy selection with agent-based passenger simulation, applied as a case study to the RER B regional rail line in the Paris (Ile-de-France) network.

Results

Coordinated multimodal disruption response outperforms single-mode response strategies on a composite of cost, equity, and service-continuity metrics, with sensitivity analysis mapping where the benefit is largest.

Limitations

Single case study (one line, one network); the framework's eight-dimension scoring still requires planners to weigh trade-offs subjectively when dimensions conflict.

Practical Implications

Agencies building disruption-response playbooks get a structured way to quantify cross-line spillover costs of bus-bridging or substitute-service decisions before deploying them, not just measure restoration speed on the disrupted line alone.

Who Should Care

Service control center planners, resilience/emergency-response teams at rail and multimodal agencies, and researchers in transportation resilience metrics.

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends single-score resilience metrics common in the literature into a multi-dimensional KPI suite and is among the few to explicitly quantify helper-line degradation as a resilience cost.

Paper 11 · arXiv (2606.08371)

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty

Polina Khoroshevskaya, Ashish Kumar Perukari
A risk-aware planning model tested across 25 cities shows that sequentially deploying transit service into 'transit deserts' and updating demand beliefs as ridership data comes in remediates over half of underserved tracts within five years, beating static planning by 5 points on average.
Why It Matters

Transit deserts — areas with real travel demand but inadequate service — are chronically hard to fix because you can't observe ridership before you build service; this paper formalizes that chicken-and-egg problem as a sequential decision process with explicit financial risk constraints.

Key Insights
  • Modeled as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) with Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) constraints to bound financial tail risk.
  • Demographic, land-use, and employment data set the prior belief over latent (unobserved) demand before any service exists.
  • Beliefs update as new service deployments generate real ridership observations — an explicit explore/exploit structure.
  • A myopic belief-aware planner remediates a median of 53.6% of transit-desert tracts after five years across 25 cities, beating static optimization by 5.0 points on average (up to +9.9 points at baseline budgets).
  • Population density and existing transit density are the strongest predictors of per-tract remediation cost (R²=0.41), and gains persist even under 50% demand-prior miscalibration.
Methodology

Partially observable Markov decision process with CVaR financial-risk constraints, using demographic/land-use/employment priors over latent demand updated by sequential ridership observations; evaluated across 25 U.S. cities with a unified cost-revenue-subsidy financial model.

Results

The risk-aware sequential planner remediates a majority of transit-desert tracts in most cities and consistently outperforms static optimization, with gains robust to substantial demand-forecast error.

Limitations

A 'myopic' (not fully look-ahead) belief-aware planner is used rather than solving the full POMDP optimally, which may leave additional gains on the table; results depend on the quality of demographic/land-use priors as starting points.

Practical Implications

Agencies and city governments fighting transit deserts get a financially risk-bounded, data-driven sequencing strategy for where to add service first, rather than political or first-come-first-served prioritization.

Who Should Care

Transit equity planners, city transportation departments tackling service gaps, and funders/grant programs targeting underserved-area mobility.

Comparison to Prior Work

Prior transit-desert literature is largely diagnostic (identifying deserts via GIS overlays); this is a prescriptive, sequential decision-theoretic remediation model with explicit financial risk bounds, a step beyond identification into action planning.

Paper 12 · arXiv (2605.22355)

TransitLM: A Large-Scale Dataset and Benchmark for Map-Free Transit Route Generation

Hanyu Guo, Jiedong Yang, Chao Chen, Longfei Xu, Kaikui Liu, Xiangxiang Chu
Trained on 13 million real transit route records from four Chinese cities, an LLM learns to generate structurally valid transit routes directly from GPS coordinates without ever consulting a map or routing engine.
Why It Matters

It demonstrates that transit routing — long assumed to require a structured graph/map representation and a routing engine — can instead be learned end-to-end from data, implicitly internalizing station and line topology inside the model itself.

Key Insights
  • The TransitLM dataset spans 120,845 stations and 13,666 lines across four Chinese cities, with over 13 million route-planning records.
  • The LLM implicitly grounds arbitrary GPS coordinates to the correct nearby stations without any explicit coordinate-to-station mapping step.
  • Generated routes are structurally valid (correct line/transfer sequences) at high accuracy purely from learned patterns.
  • Three evaluation tasks with complementary metrics test different aspects of map-free route generation quality.
  • Dataset and benchmark are openly released on Hugging Face/GitHub, enabling reproducible follow-up work.
Methodology

Continual pre-training of a large language model on a newly constructed 13-million-record transit route dataset from four Chinese cities, evaluated against three benchmark tasks measuring route validity, station grounding, and generation quality.

Results

The LLM produces structurally valid, map-free transit routes at high accuracy and grounds raw GPS input to correct stations implicitly, without explicit routing infrastructure.

Limitations

Validity at the structural level (correct line/transfer sequences) is not the same as optimality (shortest/fastest route); trained and evaluated only on Chinese cities, with unclear transferability to sparser or less-gridded transit networks elsewhere.

Practical Implications

Suggests a future where lightweight, map-free routing models could serve transit queries in places lacking maintained GTFS/routing infrastructure, or as a fallback/complement to traditional routing engines.

Who Should Care

Transit app developers, agencies in regions with poor digital mapping infrastructure, and ML researchers working on geospatial grounding in LLMs.

Comparison to Prior Work

Departs sharply from the routing-engine-plus-graph paradigm (Dijkstra/RAPTOR-style algorithms over GTFS data) that underlies virtually all production transit routers, proposing a purely learned alternative instead.

Paper 13 · arXiv (2605.20263)

The NetMob26 Dataset: A High-Resolution Multi-Source View of Public Bus Mobility in Niterói

Felipe Domingos, Humberto T. Marques-Neto, Bruno Pereira, Clayson Celes, Steffen Knoblauch, Vinícius F. S. Mota
A new open research dataset fuses GPS bus telemetry, 7.2 million ticketing transactions, route/stop data, and weather and socio-demographic layers for the Brazilian city of Niterói, anonymized for privacy and released for the NetMob Data Challenge.
Why It Matters

High-quality, multi-source, privacy-preserving public transit datasets from mid-sized Global South cities are rare; this fills a real data gap for researchers studying demand forecasting, accessibility, and weather sensitivity outside the usual North American/European mega-city datasets.

Key Insights
  • Combines four distinct data sources: bus GPS telemetry, ~7.2 million ticketing transactions, transit network/weather auxiliary data, and urban infrastructure/socio-demographic layers.
  • Covers a full operational month (March 2026), enabling fine temporal-resolution analysis of both supply (vehicle positions) and demand (ticketing).
  • Data is cleaned, anonymized, and stripped of operational inconsistencies before release.
  • Access is gated behind a Data Challenge NDA and Terms of Conditions, limiting open reproducibility outside the challenge.
  • Supports research directly on efficiency, demand forecasting, accessibility, reliability, and weather's effect on ridership.
Methodology

Data engineering and preprocessing pipeline integrating multi-source operational records (GPS, ticketing, weather, infrastructure, demographics) from Niterói's bus system into a unified, anonymized research dataset released for a structured data challenge.

Results

Delivers a uniquely detailed combined supply-and-demand view of a mid-sized Global South bus system, with documented preprocessing and observed mobility patterns reported in the paper.

Limitations

Restricted access (NDA-gated, challenge-participants only) limits broad reproducibility and independent verification by the wider research community.

Practical Implications

Researchers and Niterói's own transit planners gain a foundation for demand forecasting and weather-resilience studies that most mid-sized cities worldwide currently lack the data infrastructure to support.

Who Should Care

Mobility data scientists, Global South transit agencies seeking data-infrastructure models, and participants in the NetMob Data Challenge.

Comparison to Prior Work

Most public multi-source transit mobility datasets (e.g., NYC MTA, TfL) come from large, wealthy, Global North cities; this fills a specific gap for mid-sized Global South urban bus systems.

Implementation-Based Incentive Design for Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand and Transit Systems

Xinling Li, Runyu Zhang, Gioele Zardini
Rather than guessing which municipal policy will induce self-interested AMoD and transit operators toward a socially desirable equilibrium, this paper directly computes the minimum payment that makes deviating from a target operating point unprofitable for each operator, validated on a NYC case study.
Why It Matters

It sidesteps a deep, often-ignored weakness of equilibrium-based transportation regulation — behavioral assumptions and equilibrium-selection ambiguity — by using mechanism-design theory to compute incentive-compatible payments directly.

Key Insights
  • Uses k-implementation theory: instead of predicting an equilibrium from a policy, compute the minimum transfer that makes unilateral deviation from a target operating profile unattractive.
  • The payment decomposes into two parts: the AMoD operator's deviation gain and the public transit operator's deviation gain.
  • Requires solving three nontrivial large-scale network optimization 'oracles': the social target, the AMoD best response, and the PT best response.
  • Develops an exact reformulation, a convex relaxation (upper bound), and a sequential convex approximation (lower bound) specifically for the AMoD oracle.
  • A NYC case study reveals that the dominant source of incentive misalignment shifts as congestion levels change.
Methodology

Mechanism design via k-implementation theory applied to a multimodal AMoD-plus-transit network; develops tailored mixed-integer convex formulations, relaxations, and approximation algorithms for the social, AMoD, and PT optimization 'oracles', validated on a NYC case study.

Results

The framework computes tight bounds on the implementation payment needed to align AMoD and transit operator incentives with a social target, and shows the dominant misalignment source changes with congestion.

Limitations

Computational complexity of solving three large nonconvex network oracles likely limits near-term scalability to very large metro networks; the approach assumes operators behave as rational profit/objective maximizers responding to payments.

Practical Implications

Gives city regulators a theoretically grounded alternative to blunt equilibrium-policy-search tools for designing AMoD-transit coordination subsidies/payments, especially as robotaxi fleets begin directly competing with transit.

Who Should Care

City transportation regulators managing AMoD/robotaxi permitting, mechanism-design and game-theory researchers in transportation, and transit agencies negotiating with AMoD operators.

Comparison to Prior Work

Departs from the dominant equilibrium-based regulation literature (which searches over policies and predicts induced equilibria) by adopting an implementation-theoretic approach that computes incentive-compatible payments directly.

Which Moments Matter? Heuristics of Remembered Travel Experience in Public Transport

Esther Bosch, Klas Ihme, Stefan Bohmann
Across 2,576 real public transport trips in three German cities, riders' post-trip satisfaction is best explained not by the average experience during the journey but by a 'Minimum-End' heuristic — the single worst moment plus how the trip ended.
Why It Matters

Transit satisfaction surveys and KPIs typically implicitly assume average experience drives overall ratings; this study provides strong empirical evidence that a single bad moment plus a bad ending can outweigh an otherwise smooth trip in shaping remembered satisfaction and future mode choice.

Key Insights
  • High-frequency (every 5 minutes) in-trip experience ratings were collected via smartphone experience sampling across 2,576 real trips.
  • Competing aggregation heuristics tested: mean experience, peak-end, minimum-end, final moment alone, and trip duration.
  • The Minimum-End heuristic (worst moment + final moment) best predicts post-trip satisfaction, outperforming all other models substantially.
  • Mean-experience and pure peak-end models — common assumptions in service-quality research — perform considerably worse.
  • Negative extremes and trip endings contribute independently, not as part of an averaged overall impression.
Methodology

Smartphone-based experience sampling collecting five-minute-interval on-trip ratings plus post-trip evaluations across 2,576 real-world public transport trips in three German cities; multilevel regression models compared the explanatory power of five competing aggregation heuristics.

Results

The Minimum-End heuristic (combining the worst in-trip moment and the trip's final experience) is the strongest predictor of remembered satisfaction, clearly outperforming mean-experience and other standard heuristics.

Limitations

All data is from three German cities; whether the Minimum-End heuristic generalizes to different transit cultures, trip lengths, or modes (e.g., long-distance rail vs. short urban hops) is untested.

Practical Implications

Service-quality investment should prioritize eliminating single severe negative moments (e.g., a missed connection, a hostile encounter) and improving how trips end, rather than spreading improvement evenly across the whole journey.

Who Should Care

Transit customer experience and service design teams, behavioral/transport psychology researchers, and agencies designing satisfaction-tracking KPIs.

Comparison to Prior Work

Tests and rejects the implicit 'average experience' assumption embedded in most transit satisfaction surveys, and extends peak-end-rule research from psychology (Kahneman et al.) into a large real-world transit dataset with a more specific Minimum-End variant.

HIGH CONFIDENCE

Contrarian Insights

Plainclothes fare inspectors out-detect uniformed ones by roughly 26% per hour — the visible-deterrence intuition behind uniformed enforcement doesn't hold up for detection efficiency, even though it may still matter for general deterrence.

The theoretically 'exact' change-and-go network model for line planning is the wrong default at scale: a compact, less-precise direct-connection model wins on 83% of large real-network instances, while the exact model frequently fails to find any feasible solution at all.

Riders don't remember their trip as an average experience — a single bad moment plus a bad ending (the Minimum-End heuristic) predicts satisfaction far better than smoothing across the whole journey, undercutting standard satisfaction-survey design.

Smarter automation in transit operations is not automatically good for the public operator: an agentic fleet-management layer that successfully keeps electric bus schedules feasible can simultaneously be configured to extract financial value away from the transit agency if its incentives are profit-oriented.

Actionable Takeaways

Fare-enforcement programs should rebalance staffing toward plainclothes inspection for most route/time contexts, based on causal evidence of higher detection efficiency, while still tracking uniformed presence's deterrent role separately.

Agencies procuring AI/agentic fleet-management or V2G aggregation services should require auditable tariff-setting and explicit value-sharing terms in contracts before deployment, not just feasibility guarantees.

Customer-experience teams should prioritize eliminating single severe negative moments and improving trip endings over averaged service-quality improvements, since remembered satisfaction is driven by worst-moment-plus-ending, not the mean experience.

Large-network line-planning teams should benchmark compact approximate transfer models against the canonical exact model before committing to a planning tool, since the 'more accurate' model frequently fails to solve at all on real national-network scale.

Cities considering congestion pricing or fare-free programs can now point to real NYC evidence of broad-based transit ridership gains and disaggregated equity effects, rather than relying solely on simulations or foreign case studies.

Long-Term Implications

As agentic AI and reinforcement learning move from advisory tools into direct operational control of pricing, fleet scheduling, and routing, transit agencies face a structural governance challenge: the same systems that improve technical efficiency can be configured — deliberately or not — to redistribute financial value away from the public operator toward private aggregators or AI vendors. At the same time, the rigorous causal and counterfactual methods now being applied to fare enforcement and congestion pricing suggest the field is entering a period where decades-old operational assumptions (uniformed deterrence, exact transfer modeling, average-experience satisfaction metrics) will keep getting overturned by data. Agencies that build internal capacity to audit AI-driven recommendations and incentive structures — rather than simply adopting vendor tools — will be better positioned to capture efficiency gains without losing financial or political control of their own networks.
