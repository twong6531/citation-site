LONG-FORM YOUTUBE (5–8 MINUTES)

HOOK (0:00–0:30)

If you've ever wondered why American cities have such bad public transit compared to Tokyo, Seoul, or Paris — the answer isn't that those cities are culturally different or more urban. The answer is five specific policy decisions made by the US federal government between 1950 and 1990. We built one system at the expense of the other. On purpose. With federal money.

INTRO — THE FIVE DECISIONS (0:30–1:30)

The Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956 created the Interstate Highway System and the Highway Trust Fund. The federal government covered 90% of construction costs. States covered 10%. Transit was explicitly excluded. Every gas tax dollar collected went to roads. [Source: Federal Aid Highway Act, 1956] This wasn't a neutral policy. It was a massive subsidy for car-based development, funded by all taxpayers — including those who would never own a car.

DECISION 1: THE HIGHWAY ACT OF 1956

The Interstate Highway System cost an estimated $634 billion in today's dollars. The federal match was 90 cents on every dollar — the highest federal participation rate in any infrastructure program in US history. Compare: the first significant federal transit funding — the Urban Mass Transportation Act of 1964 — offered $375 million total. [Source: Urban Mass Transportation Act, 1964]

DECISION 2: THE DESTRUCTION OF STREETCAR NETWORKS (1950s–1960s)

Between 1936 and 1950, National City Lines — a company backed by General Motors, Firestone, Standard Oil, and Mack Trucks — acquired 28 electric streetcar systems in 16 cities and converted them to bus lines. A federal court convicted the company of conspiracy in 1949. The fine: $5,000. [Source: General Motors streetcar conspiracy, Wikipedia] By the late 1960s, American cities that had once had extensive streetcar networks — Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago — were left with remnants or nothing.

DECISION 3: HIGHWAY ROUTING THROUGH URBAN NEIGHBORHOODS

The Interstate system was routed through the most "expendable" urban land — which in practice meant Black and low-income neighborhoods. Communities that depended on transit were physically divided by elevated highways. The residents who lost the most weren't the ones who could afford cars. They were the ones who needed transit most.

DECISION 4: TRANSIT EXCLUDED FROM HIGHWAY TRUST FUND UNTIL 1982

For 26 years after the Highway Trust Fund was created, not a single dollar of gas tax revenue could be spent on transit. The Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982 finally allowed a small share of gas tax revenue — one cent per gallon — to go to transit. [Source: Surface Transportation Assistance Act, 1982] By then, transit systems in most American cities had spent two decades in decline.

DECISION 5: LOW-DENSITY ZONING LOCKED IN CAR DEPENDENCY

Federal housing policy — FHA mortgage guarantees, suburban development incentives — combined with local zoning codes to produce sprawl that transit cannot efficiently serve. A bus line needs 7–15 residents per acre to be cost-effective. Most American suburbs have 2–4. No amount of transit investment can efficiently serve land use patterns that were deliberately designed around the car.

WHERE WE STAND NOW

US transit carries about 10 billion passenger trips a year. Japan's rail systems alone carry over 25 billion. China has built more high-speed rail since 2008 than the US has built of any rail transit in its entire history. The IIJA (Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act) represents a $89 billion investment in transit over 5 years — the largest in US history. [Source: APTA] APTA estimates the total transit infrastructure funding gap at roughly $90 billion. We're starting to close it. But we're fighting 70 years of structural underinvestment, and the zoning patterns that make transit hard to serve are still in place in most American cities.

OUTRO

Understanding how we got here matters because fixing it requires more than money. It requires changing the zoning codes, the land use patterns, and the political coalitions that have protected car-dependent development for three generations. The IIJA is a start. What comes after it — the IIJA reauthorization fight happening right now — will determine whether we actually close the gap or just slow its growth. If you want to follow that fight, subscribe.
