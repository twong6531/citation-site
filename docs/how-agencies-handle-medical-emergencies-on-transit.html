Medical Emergencies on Transit: From Incident to Care

A rider collapses on a subway platform. Another has a cardiac arrest on a bus. Transit agencies respond to tens of thousands of medical incidents annually — and their response protocols, equipment, and coordination with emergency services determine who lives and who doesn't.
Article 62 of 100June 12, 2026Transit Encyclopedia

Scale and Types of Medical Incidents

New York City Transit reports approximately 25,000 medical emergencies annually on the subway system. The most common incidents are fainting and hypoglycemia; the most dangerous are cardiac arrest, stroke, and drug overdose. BART handles over 3,000 medical incidents per year; Chicago CTA responds to roughly 8,000.

The mix of incident types has shifted over the past decade. Drug overdoses — particularly opioid overdoses — now represent a significant share of transit medical emergencies in cities with active opioid crises. New York City Transit reported over 1,000 suspected opioid overdoses on the subway system in 2022, up from a handful annually a decade earlier.

First Response: Staff Training and Equipment

Transit agency first response begins with station agents, operators, and cleaning staff — the workers who first encounter a medical emergency. Most agencies train frontline workers in CPR and basic first aid and have deployed automated external defibrillators (AEDs) at stations.

The Washington Metro has over 1,200 AEDs installed throughout its system and trains all station and operations staff in their use. BART reports several cardiac arrest survivors annually attributable to bystander AED use at stations. AED placement studies recommend devices within 3 minutes of any point in a station, covering the critical window for defibrillation to be effective in ventricular fibrillation.

Coordination With EMS and Fire

Transit agencies do not have EMTs or paramedics on staff — they rely on city fire departments and EMS for medical response. The coordination challenge is access: getting a stretcher and paramedic crew onto a subway platform or into a tunnel requires clear paths, elevator access, and accurate incident location information.

Several cities have pre-positioned emergency response stations near high-volume subway stations. In New York, FDNY has engine companies within a few blocks of most Manhattan subway stations. Chicago's fire department pre-stages medical units at O'Hare and other major stations during peak hours. These arrangements reduce response times by several minutes — critical for cardiac events where survival probability falls 7–10% per minute without defibrillation.

Naloxone and Overdose Response

The opioid overdose epidemic has pushed agencies to stock naloxone (Narcan) and train staff to administer it. New York City Transit began equipping station agents with naloxone in 2021; BART followed in 2022. Philadelphia's SEPTA has naloxone available at all stations in the system.

Naloxone administration by transit staff before EMS arrival can mean the difference between survival and death for opioid overdoses, where respiratory depression causes death within minutes without intervention. The policy shift required both equipment procurement and significant training investment to ensure staff are confident and legally protected in administering the medication.

Key Takeaways
  • NYC Transit handles ~25,000 medical emergencies annually; opioid overdoses reached over 1,000 on the subway in 2022.
  • Washington Metro has 1,200+ AEDs installed system-wide; BART reports several cardiac arrest survivors annually from bystander AED use.
  • Survival probability in cardiac arrest falls 7–10% per minute without defibrillation — proximity of pre-staged EMS units is critical.
  • NYC Transit began equipping station agents with naloxone in 2021; Philadelphia's SEPTA stocks it at all stations.
