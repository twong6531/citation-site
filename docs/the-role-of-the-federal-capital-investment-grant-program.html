New Starts: The Federal Program That Builds Transit America's Rail Lines

The federal Capital Investment Grant program — known as New Starts — has funded more than 100 major transit projects since its creation in the 1970s, from the original BART extensions to new BRT lines in small cities. It is the primary federal mechanism for building new transit infrastructure, and it works in ways that profoundly shape what gets built.

Program Structure and History

The Capital Investment Grant program was formalized in the Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1978 and has been modified in every major federal transportation reauthorization since. It operates through three tiers: New Starts (projects over $400 million), Small Starts ($75M–$400M), and Core Capacity (investments in existing heavily used corridors).

The program is discretionary — funding is not guaranteed to any project — and operates on a project-by-project basis. The FTA evaluates each project independently and Congress ultimately appropriates the specific funding. A Full Funding Grant Agreement (FFGA) is the contract between the federal government and the sponsor, specifying the maximum federal contribution and project milestones.

The Evaluation Process

New Starts projects progress through a defined process: initial alternatives analysis, project development, engineering, and final design, before receiving an FFGA. The FTA uses a standardized rating system evaluating cost-effectiveness (cost per hour of travel time saved), land use, local financial commitment, and project scope.

Cost-effectiveness is calculated using a defined formula: capital and operating cost divided by projected annual ridership, compared to a 'baseline' alternative (typically an enhanced bus option). A project with a cost-effectiveness rating above $25 per hour of travel time saved is rated 'medium' or higher, generally eligible for funding. This formula has been criticized for favoring projects in areas with high existing ridership over projects in underserved areas with high potential ridership.

What the Program Has Built

New Starts and its predecessors have funded portions of Denver's light rail system, Phoenix's Valley Metro Rail, San Jose's BART extension, Charlotte's LYNX Blue Line, Seattle's Link light rail, Houston's METRORail, and dozens of BRT systems. The program has distributed over $3 billion annually in recent years.

Not every funded project has been successful. Some projects — including several BRT lines that received funding but were implemented with limited stop access and conventional buses — have performed significantly below projections. The program's evaluation criteria don't consistently predict ridership outcomes.

Criticisms and Reform Proposals

The New Starts process is widely criticized for being slow — the average time from project initiation to construction is 7–10 years. Critics blame complex environmental review requirements (NEPA), the multi-stage FTA evaluation process, and local political obstacles that add years of planning before federal review even begins.

Reform proposals include establishing a 'programmatic' environmental review process that covers common project types in advance, streamlining the evaluation criteria to include equity factors more explicitly, and expanding funding for operating costs of new lines (currently excluded). The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 increased CIG authorization to $23 billion over five years, the largest single investment in the program's history.

Key Takeaways
  • New Starts has funded over 100 major transit projects; the program distributes $3B+ annually.
  • Cost-effectiveness is calculated per hour of travel time saved; projects above $25/hour typically qualify for funding.
  • The average time from project initiation to construction is 7–10 years under current New Starts procedures.
  • The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 authorized $23 billion for CIG over five years — the largest in program history.
