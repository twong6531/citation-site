TOPIC 5: LA's Speed Camera Program Becomes California's Largest at 125 Sites

June 16, 2026

Los Angeles has hired Verra Mobility to design, build, operate, and maintain automated speed cameras at 125 locations, the largest such program in California. Sites were chosen using crash and speeding data under a 2023 state pilot law that also covers San Jose, Oakland, Glendale, Long Beach, and San Francisco; cameras capture only still images and license plates, with no facial recognition.

Key Facts

  • 125 camera locations citywide — California's largest program
  • Operated with Verra Mobility; all sites expected live by end of 2026
  • San Francisco's comparable program cut speeding 72% within six months at 15 sites
  • From 2017–2021, unsafe speed caused 16% of LA's fatal/severe crashes, including 21% of bicyclist deaths

Why it matters: Speed cameras disproportionately protect pedestrians and cyclists accessing bus stops and rail stations, making this a direct safety complement to Metro's growing bus rapid transit network.

1. A Tale of Two Rail Systems: LA Metro's Boom vs. Metrolink's Bust

The structural question is why one regional rail operator can ride a wave of investment and demand while its sibling agency is functionally insolvent, and whether that imbalance is sustainable heading into the 2028 Olympics.

Between the $1.75 SoFi shuttle program, climbing match-day ridership, and Reuters' explicit comparison of LA's approach against Boston's and New Jersey's fare controversies, the World Cup is quietly rehabilitating LA Metro's image as a system perpetually criticized for low ridership. 3. SB 79 Implementation Fights Are Becoming a Proxy War Over Transit Itself

The SCAG methodology dispute and Metro's own Board vote opposing SB 79 reveal that the law's biggest battles aren't about housing design — they're about how transit agencies define and count their own infrastructure. With the law taking effect July 1, how SCAG resolves the CEQA/NEPA completion question will directly determine the housing capacity unlocked near every rail line discussed in this briefing, including the proposed E Line extension.

4. Large Infrastructure Projects Are All Running Behind Optimistic Timelines

LAX SkyLink's slip toward a fall 2026 opening and CA High-Speed Rail's ballooning cost estimates (now disputed between $126B and $231B figures) are different projects but the same pattern: ambitious SoCal infrastructure consistently takes longer and costs more than initial public commitments suggested. Pacific Surfliner's comparatively modest, incremental expansions stand out precisely because they avoid this dynamic.
