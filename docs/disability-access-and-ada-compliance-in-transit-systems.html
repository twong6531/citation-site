ADA and Transit: 30 Years of Accessibility Progress and Gaps

The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 fundamentally transformed transit in the United States, requiring that fixed-route systems be accessible to people with disabilities and that paratransit be provided where fixed-route service operates. Thirty-plus years later, compliance is widespread but full accessibility remains unfinished.

What the ADA Requires

The ADA requires all new transit vehicles purchased after 1990 to be accessible to people with mobility disabilities — including wheelchair lifts or ramps, securement areas, and audio-visual stop announcements. All new transit facilities must meet accessibility standards. Existing facilities must be made accessible unless it is technically infeasible.

For fixed-route bus and rail service, agencies must provide complementary paratransit — door-to-door service for people who cannot use fixed-route transit due to disability — within three-quarters of a mile of any fixed route and during the same service hours. This paratransit obligation is one of the most expensive in transit: paratransit trips can cost agencies $40–$80 each compared to $3–$5 for a fixed-route bus trip.

Rail Accessibility Challenges

Retrofitting historic rail systems for accessibility has been enormously costly and technically challenging. New York's subway — with 472 stations, most built before 1940 — is only about 30% accessible, meaning two-thirds of stations have no elevator. The MTA's $5.5 billion Enhanced Station Initiative has added elevators at dozens of stations, but full accessibility will take decades and billions more.

Boston's MBTA Green Line, which runs historic streetcars through tunnel segments, has struggled to comply with ADA requirements for level boarding. Several stations lack the vertical clearance for standard accessible platforms. The MBTA has faced DOJ enforcement actions over accessibility failures.

Paratransit: The Expensive Obligation

Paratransit services — such as Access-A-Ride in New York and Dial-A-Ride in various cities — are lifelines for transit-dependent people with disabilities but strain agency budgets. New York City spends over $700 million annually on paratransit, more than many entire transit systems' total budgets.

Reform efforts focus on making fixed-route transit accessible enough that fewer riders require the more expensive paratransit option. Each station elevator added, each stop with a level boarding platform, and each bus operator trained in disability access potentially reduces the demand for costly paratransit trips. Curb cut and sidewalk investment outside agency control also affects how many people can reach transit stops independently.

Beyond Wheelchair Access

Accessibility extends beyond mobility devices. Vision-impaired riders need audible stop announcements, tactile platform edges, and wayfinding assistance. Deaf and hard-of-hearing riders need visual information displays. Riders with cognitive disabilities benefit from simple, clear maps, consistent layouts, and wayfinding designed for varying literacy levels.

Agencies increasingly design for a 'universal access' standard that improves usability for all riders — parents with strollers, travelers with luggage, elderly riders — not only those with documented disabilities. The San Francisco BART accessibility program has documented that its elevator and level-boarding improvements benefit roughly 30 riders without disabilities for every rider with a disability.

Key Takeaways
  • Only about 30% of NYC subway stations are accessible; full compliance will take decades and billions of dollars.
  • Paratransit trips cost agencies $40–$80 each vs. $3–$5 for fixed-route bus — making it one of the most expensive ADA obligations.
  • New York spends over $700 million annually on paratransit service.
  • BART found elevators and level boarding benefit ~30 riders without disabilities for every rider with one.
