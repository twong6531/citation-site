Free Fare Transit: What Cities That Tried It Found

Making transit free sounds like an obvious way to increase ridership and serve low-income riders. A growing number of cities — from Tallinn, Estonia to Kansas City, Missouri — have tried exactly that. The results are more complicated than either advocates or skeptics predicted.

Who Has Gone Fare-Free

Fare-free transit has been adopted at various scales. Tallinn, Estonia eliminated fares citywide in 2013, becoming one of the first major cities to do so. Luxembourg eliminated fares on all its national transit in 2020. In the U.S., Kansas City eliminated fares on its bus system in 2020, followed by Olympia, Washington; Albuquerque, New Mexico; and Richmond, California.

Colleges and universities have operated free transit for students for decades — Stanford, UC Berkeley, and Cornell use this model for campus shuttles. Some agencies offer free transfers between connecting routes without calling it 'free transit.' The scale of experiment ranges from campus shuttles to entire national systems.

Ridership and Revenue Effects

Fare elimination consistently increases ridership — the only question is by how much. Tallinn saw ridership increase 10–14% after eliminating fares, concentrated among lower-income residents who had previously paid. Kansas City reported ridership increases of 20–30% on bus routes after fare elimination, with strong growth among previously unbanked or fare-evading riders.

The revenue loss is real but often smaller than expected. Fare revenue typically represents 15–30% of operating costs for bus systems — not the 70–80% some critics assume. For a system running at 20% farebox recovery, fare elimination requires finding 20% more in alternative funding, usually from local taxes or redirected operating budget.

Crowding, Quality, and New Ridership

A persistent concern with fare elimination is that it increases ridership without increasing capacity, degrading the experience for existing riders. Early results from Kansas City and elsewhere found that peak-hour crowding increased on some routes but that off-peak hours — where most of the new ridership appeared — had ample capacity.

The profile of new riders matters. Research in Tallinn found that fare elimination attracted principally lower-income residents who previously walked or used informal transit, not drivers switching from cars. This is both the equity success story and the environmental limitation: if fare-free transit doesn't significantly pull drivers out of cars, its congestion and emissions benefits are modest.

The Case Against and Alternatives

Critics of fare-free transit argue that the funds spent on revenue replacement could more effectively be used to improve service frequency and coverage. A $10 million annual investment in fare elimination for a small city could instead fund 20% more bus hours — serving more riders with better service rather than slightly cheaper service for existing riders.

Targeted income-based fare programs offer a middle path. Seattle's ORCA LIFT, which charges qualifying low-income riders $1 per trip, dramatically reduces the fare burden on the population most in need without eliminating revenue entirely. Income-based pricing preserves some revenue while addressing the equity goals that motivate fare-free proposals.

Key Takeaways
  • Tallinn saw 10–14% ridership growth after fare elimination in 2013; Kansas City reported 20–30% growth.
  • Fare revenue is typically 15–30% of operating costs for bus systems — replacing it requires finding that share from other sources.
  • New fare-free riders in Tallinn were primarily lower-income residents, not car drivers switching to transit.
  • Targeted income-based programs like Seattle's ORCA LIFT ($1/trip for qualifying riders) may achieve equity goals at lower cost.
