Summary

The academic literature consistently points to a significant and sustained increase in the real operating costs of public transit, particularly within the United States. A central theme across the research is the pervasive influence of labor costs and stagnant productivity, often framed through the lens of Baumol's cost disease. This economic phenomenon describes how labor-intensive service sectors, unable to achieve the same productivity gains as manufacturing, experience rising costs as wages increase in line with the broader economy. The papers collectively establish that transit agencies face this challenge acutely, leading to higher per-unit service costs over time.

A primary driver of these escalating costs is the growth in labor expenses. Research indicates that rising wages and benefits for transit employees account for a substantial majority of the increase in real operating costs per vehicle hour. This is exacerbated by consistently stagnant or even declining labor productivity within the transit sector, especially in bus operations, where technological advancements often cannot replace human labor or significantly increase output per worker. Factors contributing to this limited productivity include increasing traffic congestion, which slows service speeds, the expansion of less efficient services like paratransit, and the inherent labor-intensive nature of delivering public transportation. The structure of labor negotiations, unionization, and associated contracts are frequently cited as influencing both wage growth and labor flexibility.

Beyond these core issues, the body of research identifies several other contributing factors to rising transit costs, such as increased safety and security regulations, the higher maintenance demands of aging infrastructure and fleets, and the growing complexity of service delivery to meet diverse customer expectations. Notably, when comparing the US context to other developed nations, an even starker picture emerges. US transit agencies exhibit significantly higher operating costs due to factors like more generous labor compensation and less flexible labor practices, a more stringent regulatory environment (especially for older systems), less competitive tendering processes, and sprawling land use patterns that result in lower ridership density and higher per-passenger costs.

Collectively, this body of research highlights a strong consensus on the long-term trend of rising transit costs and the dominant role of labor expenses coupled with limited productivity growth. The applicability of Baumol's cost disease to the transit sector is a consistent finding, while the US appears to be an amplified case of this broader phenomenon. The research suggests that effectively addressing rising transit costs requires a multifaceted approach, including strategies to manage labor cost growth, enhance operational efficiency and productivity through technological adoption and service redesign, and critically evaluate regulatory frameworks, funding models, and urban planning decisions that impact transit viability.
