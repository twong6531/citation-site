Summary

Public transportation costs are climbing. Researchers examined why, specifically looking at a phenomenon called "Baumol's Cost Disease." This idea suggests that industries relying heavily on human labor, like transit, face steadily rising costs. Their productivity does not improve as quickly as manufacturing, but they still need to pay competitive wages.

One study found that labor productivity in US transit agencies stayed largely flat between 1997 and 2013. This was particularly true for bus operations, where the number of passengers transported per worker did not increase much. At the same time, average wages for transit employees grew faster than the average wage across the entire economy. This combination of stagnant output per worker and increasing pay creates constant upward pressure on expenses.

Transit agencies attempt to counter these rising costs. They invest in new technologies like automated fare collection systems. They also outsource some services to external companies. Surprisingly, even with these efforts, public transit expenses continue to outpace general inflation. This research means public transportation will likely remain an increasingly expensive service to provide. Policymakers must consider this fundamental challenge when planning future transit funding and services.
