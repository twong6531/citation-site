Summary

The presented research collectively examines the significant and multifaceted issue of rising costs within the public transit sector, primarily focusing on the United States and North America. A key distinction emerges between the drivers of escalating *operating costs* and exorbitant *capital or construction costs*. A dominant theme concerning operating costs is the pervasive influence of labor expenditures and stagnant labor productivity, often framed through the lens of Baumol's cost disease. For capital projects, the research highlights unique institutional, regulatory, and political factors that inflate construction expenses to levels far exceeding international norms.

Regarding operating costs, the papers consistently identify labor as the primary cost driver. Analyses reveal that labor productivity in the transit sector, particularly in bus operations, has been largely stagnant or even declining over decades, while average labor costs, including wages and benefits, have risen significantly. This dynamic leads to higher operating costs per ride and declining cost-efficiency, characteristic of Baumol's cost disease—where labor-intensive services with limited automation experience rising costs relative to other sectors that benefit from productivity gains. Further investigation into this phenomenon points to unionization as a strong predictor of higher staffing levels and lower labor productivity. Other contributing factors include the provision of less productive service in low-density areas or off-peak hours, declining ridership, a notable lack of technological adoption to achieve labor savings, and funding mechanisms that may inadvertently reduce pressure for stringent cost control.

In contrast, the research on transit construction costs unveils a distinct set of challenges, particularly in North America, where project costs are exceptionally high compared to international benchmarks. This "cost puzzle" is not primarily explained by factors like difficult geology or standard labor union influence. Instead, the inflated construction expenses are attributed to institutional weaknesses such as a lack of dedicated internal agency capacity, over-reliance on external consultants, inefficient project delivery methods (e.g., traditional design-bid-build), complex procurement regulations, and costly, protracted community engagement and political processes that often lead to design changes and delays. These findings suggest that the high capital costs stem more from systemic, governance-related issues rather than inherent construction difficulties.

Collectively, this body of research suggests that rising transit costs are not a monolithic problem but rather a symptom of distinct, though sometimes interconnected, structural challenges in both day-to-day operations and major infrastructure development. There is strong agreement on the central role of labor costs and productivity in driving operating expenses, with specific mechanisms like unionization and service design offering deeper explanations. The clear divergence in the drivers for operating versus capital costs underscores that effective solutions must be tailored. Ultimately, the research collectively suggests that ensuring the financial sustainability and expansion of public transit requires fundamental, systemic reforms across several domains: addressing labor productivity and staffing levels through technology adoption and optimized service design, and undertaking significant institutional reforms to streamline project delivery, enhance internal capacity, and manage regulatory and political processes more efficiently for capital projects.
