Light Rail, Heavy Rail, Commuter Rail: What's the Difference?

Three modes that all run on tracks and carry passengers are often grouped together in public conversation but serve fundamentally different markets, operate at different scales, and cost very different amounts to build and run. Confusing them leads to bad planning decisions.

Light Rail: The Urban Middle Ground

Light rail transit (LRT) is electrically powered, typically runs on dedicated track (though sometimes at-grade in mixed traffic), and carries 100–300 passengers per vehicle. Cities use it for medium-capacity urban corridors: Portland's MAX, Denver's RTD light rail, Charlotte's LYNX Blue Line. Vehicles draw power from overhead catenary wire.

Light rail can operate at street level with grade crossings, which lowers construction costs but limits speed and reliability. It can also run underground downtown and at-grade in suburbs — the hybrid approach used by Boston's Green Line and San Francisco's Muni Metro. Vehicle lengths run 90–200 feet; platforms are built to match.

Heavy Rail: High Capacity Urban Transit

Heavy rail — subways, metros — is grade-separated, high-frequency, and high-capacity. Trains run in exclusive tunnels or elevated structures with no grade crossings. New York's subway, Chicago's L, Washington's Metro, and BART are heavy rail systems. Capacity runs 1,500–2,000 passengers per train; systems move 20,000–60,000 passengers per hour per direction at peak.

Heavy rail requires enormous capital investment — tunneling, station construction, advanced signaling — but delivers unmatched urban capacity. The New York subway carries over 3 million riders on a typical weekday. No other technology moves that many people in that dense a geography.

Commuter Rail: The Long-Distance Option

Commuter rail connects suburban and exurban communities to a central city, typically running diesel or electric trains on existing freight rail corridors. Service is peak-oriented: frequent trains during rush hours, minimal service midday and weekends. Metra in Chicago, LIRR in New York, Caltrain in San Francisco, and MBTA Commuter Rail in Boston are examples.

Commuter rail trains are heavier and longer than light rail — often running double-deck cars at 85 mph on mainline track. They stop less frequently and cover more distance. A Caltrain trip from San Jose to San Francisco covers 48 miles; a light rail trip would be measured in single-digit miles.

Choosing the Right Mode

Mode selection depends on corridor density, trip distance, capital budget, and ridership projections. Light rail fits medium-density corridors with 5,000–15,000 daily boardings and moderate construction budgets. Heavy rail is justified for high-density urban cores with consistent 20,000+ daily boardings per corridor. Commuter rail serves long-distance suburban markets where peak-hour demand is high and off-peak travel is limited.

Mistake frequently made: building heavy rail in a corridor that only justifies light rail (overbuilding), or building light rail in a corridor that will need heavy rail capacity within 20 years (underbuilding). The decision is effectively irreversible once infrastructure is in the ground.

Key Takeaways
  • Light rail runs 100–300 passengers per vehicle; heavy rail moves 1,500–2,000 per train at 20,000–60,000 per hour.
  • Heavy rail requires full grade separation; light rail can run at grade with crossings.
  • Commuter rail covers distances of 20–100 miles and runs on existing freight corridors at speeds up to 85 mph.
  • Mode choice is essentially irreversible — infrastructure built for light rail cannot easily be upgraded to heavy rail.
