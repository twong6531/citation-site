Homelessness and Transit Safety: Beyond Enforcement

When transit agencies list their top safety concerns, homelessness-related incidents increasingly appear alongside crime and accidents. The framing matters: treating homelessness as a safety problem rather than a social service problem shapes how agencies respond — and whether those responses actually help.

The Operational Challenge

Unsheltered people in transit systems create operational challenges for agencies. Vehicles used for sleeping accumulate waste and require additional cleaning. Behavior associated with untreated mental illness or substance use — yelling, blocking aisles, intimidating other riders — affects the experience of other passengers even without meeting the threshold for criminal conduct.

Agencies that treat every homeless-related incident as a law enforcement matter quickly overwhelm police resources and produce a cycle of displacement without resolution. Studies of enforcement-only approaches in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and New York consistently find that sweeps produce temporary movement without reducing the underlying population using transit.

What Outreach-Based Models Have Achieved

LA Metro's PATH Forward initiative, launched in 2022, embedded multidisciplinary outreach teams on trains and platforms — social workers, substance counselors, housing navigators, and peer support specialists. By the program's one-year mark, teams had conducted over 100,000 contacts, connected thousands of individuals to services, and placed hundreds in temporary and permanent housing.

The model differs from enforcement in its goal: rather than removing people from transit, it aims to connect them to resources that reduce their need to use transit as shelter. Success is measured by housing placements and service connections, not by how few homeless people appear in transit.

Coordination With City Shelter and Housing Systems

Transit outreach teams are most effective when connected to a functioning city shelter and housing system with capacity to absorb the people they refer. When shelter beds are full or housing waitlists run to years, outreach teams can connect people to services but cannot produce housing — limiting the program's impact.

Seattle's King County Metro coordinates closely with city homeless services, pre-identifying available shelter beds and housing vouchers before deploying outreach. This tight coordination allows outreach workers to make concrete offers — 'I have a bed for you tonight' — rather than general referrals that people may not follow through on.

The Funding and Responsibility Question

Transit agencies argue that homelessness response should be funded by social service agencies, not by transit budgets. LA Metro spent over $100 million on homelessness-related programs in 2022–2023, drawing from transit capital and operating funds. Critics argue this diverts money from service improvements; advocates argue that failing to address homelessness in transit also costs ridership and operating efficiency.

Several states and cities have begun providing dedicated homelessness-in-transit funding that compensates agencies for outreach programs without drawing from fare box revenue — recognizing that transit systems have become de facto emergency shelter and should be compensated accordingly for that role.
