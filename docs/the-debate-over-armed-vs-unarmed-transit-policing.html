Armed vs. Unarmed Transit Police: A Debate With No Easy Answer

After several high-profile incidents involving police on transit — including the killing of Oscar Grant at Fruitvale BART Station in 2009 and multiple incidents during the 2020 pandemic — transit agencies across the country have faced demands to reconsider armed police presence on their systems.

The Current Model: Dedicated Transit Police

Most large U.S. transit agencies maintain dedicated transit police departments with full arrest powers. New York City Transit Police merged with the NYPD in 1995 but dedicated transit officers remain a significant presence on the subway. BART has its own independent police department. Chicago CTA contracts with the Chicago PD for transit enforcement.

Transit police handle a range of situations: fare evasion enforcement, theft, assault, disorderly conduct, and responses to mental health crises. Critics argue this model overapplies law enforcement responses to situations — mental health crises, homelessness, intoxication — that would be better handled by specialized social services.

The Evidence on Police Presence

Research on the effect of police presence on transit crime is mixed. A 2019 study of New York City subway crime found that increased NYPD presence in specific stations correlated with short-term crime reductions but that crime often displaced to nearby stations or streets rather than declining in total. A RAND Corporation study of transit policing strategies found little evidence that patrol presence alone reduced serious crime.

What police presence does demonstrably affect is perceived safety — particularly for riders who feel reassured by uniform presence. Whether this perception shift justifies the cost and the harms of aggressive enforcement is the crux of the policy debate.

Unarmed Alternatives and Their Results

Several agencies have experimented with unarmed alternatives. LA Metro's safety ambassador program deployed unarmed, uniformed ambassadors trained in de-escalation and outreach on rail and bus. A 2022 evaluation found ambassadors handled 90% of interactions without police involvement and generated significantly higher satisfaction scores from riders than uniformed police in comparable situations.

BART's unarmed welfare check program, which sends social workers rather than police for certain mental health calls, resolved 85% of responses without police escalation. Denver's STAR (Support Team Assisted Response) program, which operates similarly for street calls and some transit incidents, showed similar results.

The Political Dimension

Transit policing policy is politically contentious. Rider advocacy groups and racial justice organizations argue that armed transit police cause disproportionate harm to Black riders through fare evasion enforcement and unnecessary escalation. Business improvement districts and downtown employers argue that reducing police presence will deter ridership and harm economic recovery.

Most agencies have landed on a hybrid approach: maintaining armed police for serious crime response while deploying unarmed ambassadors or social workers for quality-of-life and mental health situations. Whether this represents genuine reform or superficial layering of new programs on top of unchanged enforcement culture remains contested.

Key Takeaways
  • Research finds increased police presence produces short-term local crime reductions but often displaces crime rather than reducing it in total.
  • LA Metro's unarmed safety ambassadors handled 90% of interactions without police involvement in 2022 evaluations.
  • BART's welfare check program sending social workers for mental health calls resolved 85% of responses without police escalation.
  • Most agencies now use hybrid models: armed police for serious crime, unarmed ambassadors or social workers for quality-of-life issues.
