Executive Summary

Today's batch of transit research splits cleanly into three currents: real-world policy evaluation (NYC congestion pricing's spatially uneven ridership gains), algorithmic infrastructure for journey planning at scale (T-REX's sub-10ms continental routing, TransitLM's map-free LLM routing), and equity-aware operational planning (transit desert remediation under uncertainty, justice-informed AMoD-transit integration, and immigrant mode-choice behavior). The throughline is that transit research is increasingly comfortable embracing uncertainty and heterogeneity head-on — using foundation models for counterfactual policy evaluation, POMDPs for sequencing investment under unobserved demand, and mixed logit models to capture behavioral heterogeneity across rider subgroups — rather than relying on single-point estimates and one-size-fits-all network designs.

Emerging Themes

Uncertainty-aware and data-driven planning

Paper 01 · arXiv 2606.17530v1

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen, Nina Cao, Yunhan Zheng, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
Using time-series foundation models to build uncertainty-aware counterfactual demand forecasts, this study finds NYC's 2025 congestion pricing program raised bus and subway ridership across the city while shrinking overall travel demand mainly inside the cordon, with adaptation patterns differing sharply by neighborhood.
Why It Matters

Congestion pricing is one of the few transit funding tools with real political momentum in the US, and this is among the first rigorous system-wide evaluations of NYC's program; the equity findings will shape how peer cities (Boston, LA) design and defend similar policies.

Key Insights
  • Bus and subway ridership rose significantly above forecasted no-policy baselines after cordon pricing began
  • The travel-demand reduction was geographically concentrated inside the Congestion Relief Zone, while transit ridership gains extended well beyond Manhattan's core
  • Socio-demographic subgroups adapted unevenly, raising spatial equity concerns even though aggregate ridership rose
  • Time-series foundation models can generate credible counterfactuals for policy evaluation when no clean control group exists
Methodology

Probabilistic counterfactual forecasting using time-series foundation models applied to bus, subway, and aggregate trip-volume data, compared against observed post-policy demand; followed by socio-demographic subgroup analysis.

Results

Significant ridership increases on bus and subway relative to projected counterfactual; modest net decline in total travel demand; effects spatially heterogeneous with equity implications across neighborhoods.

Limitations

Counterfactual forecasting substitutes for a true control group and depends on model calibration; the paper covers only the early post-implementation period, so longer-run adaptation and induced demand effects are unknown.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and city governments can use ridership-gain evidence to justify congestion pricing politically, but should pair it with targeted equity interventions in underserved neighborhoods that adapted less favorably.

Who Should Care

Transit agency planners, congestion-pricing policy advocates, city transportation departments considering similar cordon programs, transportation economists

Comparison to Prior Work

Extends earlier London/Stockholm congestion-pricing literature by applying modern foundation-model forecasting to handle confounded, non-experimental US data, and by foregrounding spatial equity rather than only aggregate efficiency.

Paper 02 · arXiv 2606.15315v1

ChatPlanner: A Large Language Model Framework for Personalized Public Transit Routing

Tingting Yang, Chenhao Xue, Jun Chen
ChatPlanner combines fine-tuned LLMs with retrieval-augmented generation to translate conversational rider preferences into routing parameters, producing personalized transit itineraries that standard route planners overlook.
Why It Matters

Most trip planners (Google Maps, Transit App) optimize a generic objective; this points toward a future where riders can simply describe what they want ("avoid stairs," "minimize transfers at night") and get a tailored route, which could materially improve accessibility and rider satisfaction.

Key Insights
  • Fine-tuning enforces structured, valid routing-parameter output, while RAG resolves vague or conversational preference language
  • Combining both techniques yields the highest accuracy in extracting routing parameters and interpreting nuanced preferences
  • Eight rider personas and five contextual scenarios were used to build a preference-scoring benchmark
  • Case studies show ChatPlanner surfaces valuable route alternatives existing planners miss entirely
Methodology

Built fine-tuned LLMs with RAG over a custom preference-aware dataset (8 personas x 5 contexts); integrated extracted preferences into the objective function of a transit routing algorithm; ran three validation experiments on feasibility, extraction accuracy, and solution quality.

Results

ChatPlanner reliably generates feasible routes; the fine-tuning + RAG combination outperforms either technique alone on preference-extraction accuracy and route quality.

Limitations

Evaluated on synthetic personas/contexts rather than real rider populations; no live deployment or A/B test against existing commercial planners is reported.

Practical Implications

Transit apps could integrate conversational preference capture as a front-end layer on top of existing routing engines without replacing core pathfinding infrastructure.

Who Should Care

Transit app developers, accessibility advocates, MaaS (Mobility-as-a-Service) platform designers

Comparison to Prior Work

Moves beyond rule-based personalization (e.g., toggle filters for 'fewer transfers') by using natural language as the preference interface, building on the broader trend of LLM-as-controller architectures seen in other domains.

Paper 03 · arXiv 2606.08371v1

Risk-Aware Planning for Transit Desert Remediation Under Demand Uncertainty

Polina Khoroshevskaya, Ashish Kumar Perukari
Framing transit-desert remediation as a partially observable Markov decision process with financial tail-risk constraints, this paper shows a belief-aware planner can remediate over half of underserved tracts across 25 cities within five years, outperforming static optimization especially under moderate budgets.
Why It Matters

Transit deserts affect tens of millions of people, and agencies have historically lacked a rigorous framework for deciding where to deploy new service when ridership data doesn't exist yet for unserved areas — this offers a financially disciplined, data-driven sequencing strategy.

Key Insights
  • A POMDP with CVaR constraints lets planners balance expected coverage gains against financial tail risk from low-ridership deployments
  • The belief-aware planner remediated a median of 53.6% of transit-desert tracts after five years across 25 cities
  • Gains over static optimization average 5.0 percentage points, reaching 16 of 25 cities, with the largest improvement (+9.9 points) at moderate budget levels
  • Population density and existing transit density are the strongest predictors of per-tract remediation cost (R²=0.41)
  • The approach remains robust even with 50% miscalibration in prior demand estimates
Methodology

POMDP formulation with Conditional Value-at-Risk constraints; priors over latent demand built from demographic, land-use, and employment data; belief updated sequentially as new deployments generate ridership observations; tested via simulation across 25 cities with a unified operating-cost/capex/fare-revenue financial model.

Results

Median 53.6% transit-desert remediation after 5 years; +5.0 point average improvement over static optimization; robustness confirmed under substantial demand-prior miscalibration.

Limitations

Results are simulation-based rather than from real deployments; the financial model's assumptions about fare revenue and opex may not generalize across very different transit agency cost structures.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and MPOs could adopt sequential, belief-updating service expansion plans instead of one-shot static network redesigns, especially when budgets are moderate rather than abundant or scarce.

Who Should Care

Transit planning agencies, MPOs, equity-focused transportation researchers, federal/state transit grant programs

Comparison to Prior Work

Goes beyond static transit-desert identification studies (which only map gaps) by proposing an explicit sequential decision framework that accounts for the chicken-and-egg problem of unobservable latent demand.

Paper 04 · arXiv 2606.00572v1

Spatiotemporal Multi-Task Graph Transformer for Trip-Level Transit Prediction

Oluwaleke Yusuf, Adil Rasheed, Frank Lindseth
SMT-GraphFormer reframes bus ridership forecasting as sequence-to-sequence prediction along a route's stop sequence, using graph embeddings and a mixture-of-experts decoder to jointly predict boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell time, beating stop-level tabular benchmarks on Trondheim transit data.
Why It Matters

Accurate trip-level (not just stop-level) ridership prediction is foundational for digital-twin transit planning, real-time crowding alerts, and schedule optimization — better models here translate directly into better-run buses.

Key Insights
  • Framing prediction as sequence-to-sequence along the stop sequence captures within-trip evolution that stop-level models miss
  • A multi-gate mixture-of-experts decoder lets the model specialize per task while sharing a context encoder
  • Treating delay and dwell time as encoder-side surrogate tasks improves the primary boarding/alighting predictions
  • Alighting prediction sees the largest gain (+0.24 R²) over stop-level tabular benchmarks
  • The framework is horizon-agnostic, supporting digital-twin scenario analysis
Methodology

Multi-task graph transformer architecture with graph embeddings for multi-relational stop similarity, a weather/temporal context encoder, and a mixture-of-experts decoder; trained and evaluated on real public bus transit data from Trondheim, Norway, with ablation studies isolating each component's contribution.

Results

Outperforms stop-level tabular benchmarks across boarding, alighting, delay, and dwell prediction; +0.24 R² gain on alighting specifically; ablations confirm each architectural component contributes.

Limitations

Single-city validation (Trondheim) limits generalizability to other transit network topologies and ridership regimes; computational cost of the transformer architecture versus simpler models isn't discussed.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies running or building digital twins could adopt this trip-level sequential approach for more accurate real-time and scenario-based ridership forecasting than current stop-by-stop models.

Who Should Care

Transit operations researchers, agencies building digital-twin planning tools, ML practitioners in transportation

Comparison to Prior Work

Improves on prior stop-level tabular ML approaches by explicitly modeling within-trip sequential structure and inter-target dependencies, rather than treating each stop/target as independent.

Paper 05 · arXiv 2605.25617v1

Justice-informed Planning of Intermodal Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems under Operational Constraints

Giacomo Ganassoli, Francesco Mazzeo, Cecilia Pasquale, Silvia Siri, Mauro Salazar
This paper builds network-flow optimization models for intermodal AMoD-plus-transit systems that explicitly bake in rider budget and safety constraints, finding via a Manhattan case study that free public transit can deliver justice outcomes nearly as good as a fully free system without sacrificing efficiency.
Why It Matters

As robotaxi fleets expand, cities need operational—not just theoretical—tools to ensure autonomous mobility doesn't simply re-create transportation inequities; this is a rare paper that prescribes solutions rather than only diagnosing injustice.

Key Insights
  • Justice-informed objectives can be optimized jointly with standard efficiency metrics without a fundamental tradeoff in this case study
  • Monetary budget constraints are the binding limiter on how much social justice an AMoD system can deliver as a for-profit TNC
  • Free public transit achieves justice outcomes nearly equivalent to a fully free intermodal system at much lower cost
  • Mesoscopic network-flow models can characterize a multimodal AMoD+transit+active-mode system in a tractable way
Methodology

Network flow optimization models for intermodal AMoD systems incorporating budget, safety, and infrastructure capacity constraints; compares utilitarian-efficiency versus justice-informed objective functions; validated through a real-world Manhattan case study.

Results

Free public transit nearly matches the justice/sufficiency levels of a fully free intermodal AMoD system; justice-informed operations achievable without compromising standard efficiency metrics when paired with the right fare policy.

Limitations

Single-city (Manhattan) case study with its specific transit-rich context may not generalize to car-dependent or transit-poor cities; assumes cooperative system-level optimization rather than competing private operators in practice.

Practical Implications

Cities considering robotaxi integration should pair it with free or heavily subsidized public transit rather than relying on AMoD alone to achieve equitable mobility access.

Who Should Care

City transportation policy makers, AMoD/robotaxi operators, transportation justice researchers, free-fare transit advocates

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinguishes itself from prior transport-justice literature (largely descriptive/diagnostic) by delivering prescriptive, operational optimization models that planners can actually apply.

Paper 06 · arXiv 2605.22355v1

TransitLM: A Large-Scale Dataset and Benchmark for Map-Free Transit Route Generation

Hanyu Guo, Jiedong Yang, Chao Chen, Longfei Xu, Kaikui Liu, Xiangxiang Chu
TransitLM releases a 13-million-record dataset spanning four Chinese cities and shows that an LLM continually pre-trained on it can generate structurally valid transit routes and implicitly ground GPS coordinates to stations, entirely without a traditional map-based routing engine.
Why It Matters

If route planning can be learned end-to-end from data rather than engineered map infrastructure, it could dramatically lower the cost of deploying trip planners in cities with poor GTFS/mapping data coverage, especially in the Global South.

Key Insights
  • 13M+ transit route planning records across 120,845 stations and 13,666 lines in four Chinese cities
  • An LLM trained on this corpus produces structurally valid routes at high accuracy without an explicit routing engine
  • The model implicitly grounds arbitrary GPS coordinates to appropriate stations, learning a form of geospatial reasoning
  • Dataset and benchmark are openly released on HuggingFace with accompanying evaluation code
Methodology

Continual pre-training of an LLM on the TransitLM corpus; evaluation across three benchmark tasks with complementary metrics assessing route validity, station grounding, and route quality.

Results

High structural-validity accuracy for generated routes; effective implicit GPS-to-station grounding without explicit mapping logic.

Limitations

No comparison of travel-time optimality or transfer efficiency against established routing algorithms (e.g., RAPTOR-class engines); China-only data raises questions about transferability to transit systems elsewhere.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies and app developers in data-poor regions could potentially bootstrap a usable trip planner from historical query/route logs rather than building full GTFS-based routing infrastructure from scratch.

Who Should Care

ML researchers building geospatial LLM applications, transit agencies in regions lacking robust digital mapping infrastructure, MaaS startups

Comparison to Prior Work

Contrasts with classical graph-based transit routing algorithms (e.g., T-REX, RAPTOR) by replacing explicit search with learned generation, trading provable optimality for flexibility and lower infrastructure requirements.

Paper 07 · arXiv 2605.20263v1

The NetMob26 Dataset: A High-Resolution Multi-Source View of Public Bus Mobility in Niterói

Felipe Domingos, Humberto T. Marques-Neto, Bruno Pereira, Clayson Celes, Steffen Knoblauch, Vinícius F. S. Mota
NetMob26 releases a richly multi-sourced dataset from Niterói, Brazil combining GPS telemetry, 7.2 million ticketing transactions, route/weather data, and socio-demographic context to enable research on bus transit efficiency, demand forecasting, and reliability.
Why It Matters

High-quality, multi-source transit datasets from mid-sized Global South cities are rare; this fills a real gap for researchers studying transit equity and operations outside the usual US/Europe/China data ecosystem.

Key Insights
  • Combines four distinct data sources: bus GPS telemetry, ~7.2M ticketing transactions, route/stop/weather data, and urban socio-demographic information
  • Preprocessing pipeline includes anonymization of passenger identifiers and removal of operational inconsistencies
  • Access is gated behind an NDA and Terms of Use for challenge participants, limiting open reuse
  • Designed explicitly to support demand forecasting, accessibility analysis, and weather-impact studies
Methodology

Operational data collection from March 2026 across GPS telemetry, ticketing systems, and auxiliary infrastructure/weather/socio-demographic sources, followed by cleaning, anonymization, and structured release for a data challenge.

Results

Dataset paper; no predictive modeling results, but documents mobility patterns observed in the underlying system during the collection window.

Limitations

Restricted access (NDA-gated) limits broader reproducibility and reuse outside the official challenge; single-month collection window may miss seasonal variation.

Practical Implications

Researchers entering the NetMob Data Challenge gain a rare combined supply-and-demand transit dataset; longer term, similar releases from other Global South cities could broaden the evidence base for equity-focused transit research.

Who Should Care

Transit data scientists, NetMob Challenge participants, researchers studying Global South urban mobility

Comparison to Prior Work

Distinguishes itself from typical single-source transit datasets (GPS-only or ticketing-only) by fusing supply-side and demand-side data with socio-demographic context in one package.

Paper 08 · arXiv 2605.18687v1

Implementation-Based Incentive Design for Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand and Transit Systems

Xinling Li, Runyu Zhang, Gioele Zardini
Rather than searching for municipal policies that induce a desirable equilibrium between selfish AMoD and transit operators, this paper computes the minimum transfer payments needed to make a target operating profile incentive-compatible for both operators, demonstrated via a NYC case study.
Why It Matters

Regulating robotaxi/transit competition through equilibrium-prediction models is notoriously fragile; a payment-based implementation approach sidesteps equilibrium-selection problems and gives regulators a more robust lever to align private operator incentives with public goals.

Key Insights
  • Implementation-based regulation avoids the behavioral assumptions and equilibrium-selection ambiguity of standard game-theoretic regulation
  • The minimum incentive-compatible transfer decomposes into separate deviation-gain payments for the AMoD operator and the transit operator
  • Computing the social target, AMoD best response, and PT best response each require distinct large-scale, congestion-aware optimization formulations
  • A NYC case study shows the dominant source of incentive misalignment shifts with congestion levels
Methodology

k-implementation theory applied to multimodal transport regulation; tailored mixed-integer convex formulations, convex relaxations, and sequential convex approximations developed for each of the three required optimization oracles (social target, AMoD best response, PT best response); validated on a NYC case study.

Results

Framework computes tight bounds on implementation payments; reveals that the dominant driver of incentive misalignment between AMoD and transit operators changes as congestion varies.

Limitations

Computational complexity of solving three large-scale optimization problems may limit real-time regulatory applicability; assumes regulators can credibly commit to and enforce transfer payments.

Practical Implications

City regulators overseeing both transit agencies and robotaxi operators could use payment-based mechanisms instead of blunt policy levers (caps, fees) to steer the system toward socially desirable outcomes.

Who Should Care

Transportation economists, city regulators of AMoD/TNC operators, transit-AMoD coordination researchers

Comparison to Prior Work

Departs from the dominant equilibrium-prediction regulatory literature by adopting mechanism-design-style implementation theory, a less common but more robust framing for multi-operator transport regulation.

MEDIUM CONFIDENCE
Paper 09 · arXiv 2606.14713v1

Recurring Public Transit Schedules: Stable Identification from GTFS and Similarity Analysis

Evgeny Makarov, Georgy Taubkin
This paper formalizes 'DayTypes' — stable, recurring sets of trips that share a passenger-facing timetable — and develops a GTFS-based extraction and comparison method, finding substantial schedule compression and hierarchical nesting across Japanese and Canadian transit feeds.
Why It Matters

GTFS, the global standard for transit data, has no native concept of a recurring weekday/weekend/holiday schedule pattern; this gap quietly complicates scheduling, demand modeling, and schedule-change communication for every agency that uses GTFS, which is nearly all of them.

Key Insights
  • DayTypes are defined as the complete set of Route Pattern trips operating on a given date, providing a missing recurring-supply object absent from raw GTFS
  • A stable extraction method uses H3 route-pattern keys to identify DayTypes reliably across feeds
  • Validation found a median of just four nonempty DayTypes per agency, showing strong schedule compression
  • DayTypes exhibit hierarchical nesting between service classes (e.g., weekday subsuming weekday-except-holiday)
  • Country-level differences emerged in how persistently schedules remain exactly disjoint across Japanese versus Canadian feeds
Methodology

Formal definition of DayTypes from GTFS Route Pattern trip sets; stable extraction algorithm using H3 geospatial route-pattern keys; schedule-comparison framework with exact, time-tolerant, and structural-comparability metrics; validated on real Japanese and Canadian GTFS feeds.

Results

Median of four nonempty DayTypes per agency; confirmed hierarchical nesting between service classes; observed country-level variation in schedule disjointness persistence.

Limitations

Validation limited to Japanese and Canadian feeds, which may not represent scheduling conventions in other regions (e.g., agencies with highly irregular event-based service changes); the method depends on GTFS data quality and completeness.

Practical Implications

GTFS tooling vendors and transit agencies could adopt DayTypes as a standard derived object to power vehicle scheduling, schedule-change detection, and demand-assignment models without re-deriving recurring patterns ad hoc each time.

Who Should Care

GTFS tooling developers, transit scheduling analysts, agencies managing timetable consolidation efforts

Comparison to Prior Work

Fills a structural gap left open by the GTFS specification itself and prior GTFS-analysis tooling, which typically treats each service_id independently rather than deriving a stable recurring-schedule abstraction.

Paper 10 · arXiv 2605.00083v1

Comparative Analysis of Polygon-Based and Global Machine Learning Models for Bus Occupancy Prediction

Daniel Azenkot, Michael Fire, Eran Ben Elia
Clustering a city into spatial polygons and training a separate local ridership-forecasting model per cluster matches the accuracy of a single global model while better capturing localized urban dynamics that a one-size-fits-all approach misses.
Why It Matters

Most agencies default to global ridership models for simplicity; this suggests a low-cost architectural change (spatial clustering + local models) could yield more targeted, locally accurate service-planning insights without sacrificing overall accuracy.

Key Insights
  • Bus stops in close spatial proximity share similar ridership dynamics, motivating cluster-based local modeling
  • A localized, cluster-based modeling strategy achieves accuracy comparable to a single global model
  • The framework integrates ridership data with spatial (attractive destinations), meteorological, and temporal features
  • Localized models offer a path toward more targeted, area-specific service improvements than global models can provide
Methodology

Spatial clustering of bus stops/regions; multi-dimensional feature integration including spatial points-of-interest, weather, and temporal variables; trained separate local forecasting models per cluster and compared against a single global model.

Results

Localized polygon-based models achieve accuracy comparable to global models while offering more spatially targeted insight into ridership dynamics.

Limitations

Comparable accuracy rather than a clear improvement means the practical case rests on interpretability/targeting benefits rather than raw predictive power; cluster boundary choice could meaningfully affect results but sensitivity isn't deeply explored.

Practical Implications

Agencies could adopt cluster-based local models specifically when they want neighborhood-level service planning insight, even if a global model would suffice for system-wide forecasting accuracy alone.

Who Should Care

Transit service planners, ridership forecasting teams, agencies pursuing equity-focused or neighborhood-level service redesign

Comparison to Prior Work

Tests a clustering-based alternative against the dominant global-model paradigm in ridership forecasting literature, contributing comparative evidence rather than a wholly new technique.

Paper 11 · Paper of the Day · arXiv 2605.18778v1

T-REX: Fast and Dynamic Journey Planning for Continental-Scale Public Transit Networks

Jonas Sauer, Patrick Steil, Sascha Witt
T-REX applies multi-level overlay partitioning to Trip-Based Transit Routing, pruning irrelevant local transfers to answer Pareto-optimal journey queries on a full European transit network in under 10 milliseconds — a 20-80x speedup over prior algorithms — while supporting real-time schedule updates in seconds.
Why It Matters

Continental-scale, real-time journey planning is a genuine hard computer-science problem underlying every cross-border trip planner in Europe; this is a substantial, immediately deployable algorithmic advance for that infrastructure.

Key Insights
  • A multi-level network partition lets T-REX identify only the transfers relevant to long-distance travel during a short precomputation phase
  • Focusing pruning on transfers rather than trips is a key driver of the speedup over prior overlay-based algorithms
  • Queries on a full European network resolve in under 10ms, 20x faster than plain Trip-Based Routing and 80x faster than algorithms without any preprocessing
  • Precomputation takes only two minutes and real-time schedule updates can be incorporated within seconds, supporting live disruption-aware journey planning
  • Memory footprint remains moderate despite the continental scale of the network
Methodology

Multi-level overlay extension of Trip-Based Public Transit Routing (TB); redesigned query algorithm with transfer-focused pruning and improved memory layout; benchmarked against TB and prior overlay-based algorithms on a full European-scale transit network, optimizing arrival time and number of trips (Pareto front).

Results

Sub-10ms query times on continental-scale networks; 20x speedup over TB, 80x over no-preprocessing algorithms; two-minute precomputation; multi-second real-time update incorporation.

Limitations

Benchmarked primarily on European network data; real-world deployment considerations (e.g., handling live vehicle positions, partial outages) beyond schedule updates aren't deeply explored.

Practical Implications

Cross-border and national trip-planning platforms (e.g., Deutsche Bahn, Trainline, Google Maps transit backend) could adopt T-REX-style overlays to support real-time, interactive continental journey planning at low latency.

Who Should Care

Transit routing engine developers, national rail/transit data platforms, algorithms researchers in transportation

Comparison to Prior Work

Directly improves on prior overlay-based Trip-Based Routing algorithms and on plain TB/RAPTOR-class approaches, claiming the first algorithm meeting interactive real-time requirements at true continental scale.

Paper 12 · arXiv 2604.15754v1

Urban transit network design using spanning tree: A case study of Canberra transit network

Satoshi Suguira, Kam-Fung Cheung, Michael G. H. Bell, Hitomi Nakanishi, Fumitaka Kurauchi, Supun Perera, Yogi Vidyattama
Using a tabu-search heuristic built around spanning-tree network design, this paper redesigns the Canberra bus network to minimize total passenger-kilometers, then relaxes the spanning-tree link constraint with a greedy algorithm to find further improvements.
Why It Matters

Network redesign is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-capital ways for a transit agency to improve service, and spanning-tree-based heuristics offer a computationally tractable middle ground between ad hoc redesign and full mixed-integer optimization that's intractable at scale.

Key Insights
  • Spanning-tree structure provides a simple, tractable way to frame network redesign as a mixed-integer optimization problem
  • Exact optimal minimum-passenger-kilometer spanning trees are intractable for large networks, motivating heuristic search
  • A tabu-search heuristic efficiently produces promising spanning-tree network designs validated on real Canberra bus data
  • Relaxing the strict spanning-tree link constraint via a greedy algorithm to add extra links further reduces total passenger-kilometers
Methodology

Mixed-integer optimization formulation of transit network design as a minimum-passenger-kilometer spanning tree problem; tabu-search heuristic for tractable solving; greedy link-addition algorithm to relax the spanning-tree constraint; validated using real Canberra bus network data.

Results

Tabu-search heuristic produces effective spanning-tree network designs; greedy link relaxation yields further passenger-kilometer reductions beyond the pure spanning-tree solution.

Limitations

Single mid-sized city (Canberra) case study; passenger-kilometer minimization as the sole objective ignores other planning priorities like equity, frequency, or operating cost.

Practical Implications

Mid-sized transit agencies undertaking network redesigns could use this tabu-search-plus-greedy-relaxation pipeline as a practical, computationally feasible alternative to full network optimization.

Who Should Care

Transit network planners, mid-sized city transportation departments, operations-research transportation academics

Comparison to Prior Work

Offers a more computationally tractable alternative to full mixed-integer network design optimization, trading some optimality for scalability via the spanning-tree framing plus heuristic relaxation.

Paper 13 · arXiv 2604.15564v1

Mobility Behaviour of Immigrants in Canada: Analyzing Mode Choice Using GPS Panel Data and Mixed Logit Models

Tareq Alsaleh, Bilal Farooq, Zachary Patterson
Using GPS panel data from over 80,000 trips and mixed logit models, this study finds new immigrants in Canada value in-vehicle travel time about 66% less than Canadian-born residents, but that greater social/economic integration is associated with reduced transit use and increased car reliance.
Why It Matters

Immigrant populations are a major and growing share of transit ridership in many North American cities, and this paper quantifies a counterintuitive risk: policies that successfully integrate immigrants economically and socially may simultaneously push them off transit and into cars, undermining ridership and equity goals.

Key Insights
  • New immigrants exhibit roughly 66% lower value of in-vehicle travel time than Canadian-born residents (immigrant-to-Canadian-born ratio of 0.34), consistent across model specifications
  • A one-standard-deviation increase in a composite integration index decreases the probability of choosing transit by about five percentage points
  • Reducing access time to transit is roughly three times more effective than fare reductions at shifting immigrants toward transit use
  • Emerging e-mobility alternatives face no inherent preference penalty in the model, competing purely on level-of-service attributes
  • Out-of-sample cross-validation accuracy of 80-82% across model specifications
Methodology

Joint revealed-preference/stated-preference framework using a custom mobile app collecting GPS panel data (80,000+ trips, 100 participants); multinomial logit and mixed logit models estimated; composite integration index built across economic, social, civic, and health dimensions; five-fold cross-validation for out-of-sample accuracy.

Results

Immigrants show much lower in-vehicle time sensitivity than Canadian-born residents; higher integration correlates with reduced transit use and greater car reliance; access-time improvements outperform fare cuts in shifting immigrant mode choice toward transit.

Limitations

Sample of 100 participants, while rich in trip-level detail, is small for generalizing across Canada's diverse immigrant population; self-selection into a custom mobile app could bias the sample toward more tech-engaged respondents.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies serving immigrant-heavy corridors should prioritize reducing access time (better last-mile connections, more frequent local feeder service) over fare discounts to retain transit ridership as immigrants economically integrate.

Who Should Care

Transit agencies in immigrant-receiving metros, transportation equity researchers, urban planners designing newcomer settlement services

Comparison to Prior Work

Adds granular GPS panel and stated-preference evidence to a literature that has mostly relied on cross-sectional travel surveys, and introduces a novel composite integration index as an explanatory variable for mode choice.

MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

Contrarian Insights

Successful immigrant integration into Canadian economic and social life is associated with LOWER transit use and higher car reliance — meaning settlement-support success and transit-ridership goals can actively work against each other unless access-time investments are made deliberately.

In the justice-informed AMoD study, simply making public transit free achieves nearly all the equity benefit of making the entire intermodal system (including robotaxis) free — suggesting cities don't need to subsidize AMoD directly to capture most of the justice gains.

The localized, cluster-based bus occupancy models only matched (not beat) global model accuracy — a reminder that architectural sophistication doesn't always translate into a measurable accuracy win, even when it offers other planning benefits.

Actionable Takeaways

1. Agencies pursuing or defending congestion pricing should proactively pair it with targeted equity interventions in neighborhoods showing weaker adaptation, not just tout aggregate ridership gains.

2. When planning transit-desert remediation under tight-to-moderate budgets, sequential belief-updating investment plans outperform static optimization by a meaningful margin (~5-10 points) — agencies should consider phased, data-updating rollouts over one-shot redesigns.

3. For immigrant-heavy service areas, invest in reducing transit access time (last-mile connections, feeder frequency) rather than fare discounts — it's roughly three times more effective at sustaining transit mode share.

4. GTFS-consuming tools and agencies should consider adopting the DayTypes abstraction to simplify schedule-change detection and vehicle scheduling rather than re-deriving recurring patterns ad hoc.

Long-Term Implications

If LLM-native routing (TransitLM, ChatPlanner) and ultra-fast classical algorithms (T-REX) keep advancing in parallel, trip planning infrastructure may bifurcate into a flexible, preference-rich LLM layer for personalization sitting atop a hyper-optimized classical routing core for raw pathfinding speed — giving riders both faster answers and more tailored ones. Meanwhile, the equity-operationalization trend (transit deserts, justice-informed AMoD, immigrant mode choice) suggests the next decade of transit research will increasingly treat equity as a constraint to optimize under, not an afterthought to measure post hoc — a maturation that mirrors how congestion and reliability became hard planning constraints a generation ago.
