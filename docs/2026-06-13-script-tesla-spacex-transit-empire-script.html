HOOK (0–30s)

Yesterday, SpaceX went public on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. They raised $75 billion — the largest IPO in human history, more than Saudi Aramco in 2019. [Source: CNBC, June 12, 2026] Stock closed up 19%. Market cap hit $2.2 trillion. And almost nobody covered what it means for the 9.9 million Americans who ride public transit every single day.

INTRO — ESTABLISHING STAKES (30–90s)

SpaceX is a rocket company. Tesla makes cars. The Boring Company digs tunnels. Separately, they look like very different businesses. But they share an owner. And that owner has been quietly assembling every layer of an alternative transportation system — underground, autonomous, privately controlled, and completely outside the regulatory frameworks that govern public transit.

SpaceX just went public with a $75 billion war chest. A Tesla-SpaceX merger is being openly discussed at the executive level. [Source: Fortune, May 2026] And at this exact moment, America's public transit systems are collapsing under a $6.6 billion funding crisis. [Source: CBO / GovTech, 2026] That's not a coincidence. That's a market opening.

SECTION 1 — BUILDING THE EMPIRE (1:30–3:00)

2016: The Boring Company is founded. Musk's stated goal: tunnel under cities to move cars at 150 mph.

2021: The Las Vegas Convention Center Loop opens. 1.7 miles of tunnels, Teslas ferrying passengers. It has since grown to a planned 68-mile network. [Source: The Boring Company] Because the Loop is privately funded, it bypassed the environmental reviews and federal oversight required for any comparable public transit project. ProPublica reported that the Vegas Loop has "little oversight." [Source: ProPublica]

2024–2025: Tesla's Full Self-Driving rolls out unsupervised. Robotaxi opens to the public. [Source: Tesla News, 2026]

June 12, 2026: SpaceX IPOs at $135 a share. Trades up to $161. $2.2 trillion market cap on day one. SpaceX's president, Gwynne Shotwell, goes on record saying a Tesla merger would make Musk's life "easier." [Source: Yahoo Finance, 2026]

SECTION 2 — PUBLIC TRANSIT IS COLLAPSING RIGHT NOW (3:00–5:00)

While Musk's empire was being assembled, public transit in America was being quietly defunded. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act funding expires this year. The COVID-era federal relief money that propped up operating budgets since 2021? Gone. [Source: CBO, 2026]

  • Philadelphia (SEPTA): 45% service cuts. 21.5% fare increase. 32 bus routes eliminated. [Source: Mass Transit Magazine, 2026]
  • Chicago (CTA): Faces up to 40% service cuts. $200M funding gap in 2026, could balloon to $790M in 2027. [Source: CBS News Chicago, 2026]
  • Nationally: Transit agencies face a $6.6 billion shortfall through FY2026. [Source: GovTech, 2026]

Here's the structural issue: public transit requires democratic approval — ballot measures, environmental reviews, federal grants, ADA compliance. A Boring Company tunnel can break ground without a public hearing if it's privately funded. That asymmetry is the entire story.

SECTION 3 — THREE SCENARIOS (5:00–7:00)

Scenario A: Parallel systems. Boring Company tunnels serve downtown cores. Public transit continues serving low-income corridors. Cities end up with a two-tier mobility system — fast private transit for those who can pay, degraded public service for everyone else.

Scenario B: Selective substitution. Cash-strapped cities, facing $6.6 billion in deficits, begin contracting with Musk's companies to fill service gaps. Federal antitrust frameworks, written before autonomous tunnels existed, don't intervene.

Scenario C: Regulatory capture. A merged SpaceX-Tesla entity, with $3.4 trillion in market cap, shapes transit policy directly. The IIJA reauthorization gets written to favor autonomous vehicle infrastructure over bus and rail.

OUTRO (7:00–8:00)

SpaceX's IPO is a finance story. The Tesla merger speculation is a Wall Street story. But put them together, with a $6.6 billion transit funding gap, and it becomes an urban infrastructure story — the kind that determines whether a nurse in Chicago can get to work, or whether she has to buy a car she can't afford.
