Transit Crime Statistics: What Gets Counted and What Doesn't

Transit crime statistics are among the most politically sensitive numbers an agency publishes. They affect public perception, ridership, and funding. They are also among the most methodologically complex, because what counts as a crime, whether it gets reported, and how it gets categorized vary significantly between agencies and over time.

What Gets Reported and What Doesn't

Transit crime statistics capture only reported incidents. Research consistently estimates that 50–70% of crimes on transit — especially harassment, theft, and minor assaults — go unreported. Victims don't report because they don't believe anything will happen, because interacting with police feels risky, or because they didn't recognize the incident as reportable.

Agencies that make reporting easier — smartphone apps, anonymous text lines — see reported crime rates increase even when underlying crime may be stable or declining. This creates a counterintuitive result: agencies that improve reporting infrastructure appear to have higher crime, discouraging the improvement.

Per-Rider vs. Per-Mile Statistics

Raw crime counts are almost meaningless without normalization. An agency carrying 2 million riders per day will have more total incidents than one carrying 200,000 regardless of safety quality. Agencies normalize by passenger trips (crimes per million riders) or by vehicle revenue miles (crimes per million miles).

The choice of denominator matters. An agency that primarily runs long-distance commuter rail will look safer per-trip than one running urban rapid transit, because each commuter rail trip is one boarding regardless of distance while the urban system makes multiple stops. New York City's subway looks crime-heavy per-trip but looks much safer per-mile traveled than many comparison systems.

Defining Crime Categories Consistently

Different agencies categorize crimes differently, making comparison unreliable. One agency may count physical altercations as 'assaults' while another categorizes some as 'disturbances.' Fare evasion may or may not appear in crime statistics. Quality-of-life incidents — public intoxication, drug use — may be categorized as crimes, civil violations, or health events depending on the agency and jurisdiction.

Federal reporting requirements through the National Transit Database collect some crime data, but the definitions are broad enough that variation in local reporting practices dominates any real trend signal. Academic researchers analyzing transit crime typically work directly with agency incident databases rather than published statistics.

Transparency and Public Trust

Agencies face a dilemma: publishing detailed crime data feeds media narratives that can suppress ridership, but suppressing data damages trust. New York City Transit has moved toward more granular public crime reporting — incidents by line, station, and category — with the explicit rationale that transparency builds long-term trust more than selective disclosure.

Several agencies have hired independent oversight bodies to review and publish crime statistics: Washington Metro's Inspector General and LA Metro's independent auditor both produce regular public safety reports. Independent reporting provides credibility and insulates the agency from accusations of political manipulation of safety statistics.

Key Takeaways
  • 50–70% of transit crimes go unreported; agencies that make reporting easier see apparent crime increases even if underlying crime is stable.
  • Crimes per million riders is more meaningful than raw counts; crimes per mile traveled provides yet a different picture.
  • Crime category definitions vary enough between agencies that published statistics cannot be reliably compared across systems.
  • New York City Transit has moved toward more granular public crime reporting — by line and station — as a transparency strategy.
