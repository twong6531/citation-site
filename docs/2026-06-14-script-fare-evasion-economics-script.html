CAPTION

Transit agencies crack down on fare evasion and spend millions doing it — then recover a fraction of that in actual revenue. The economics of enforcement rarely appear in the political debate. NYC, DC Metro, and LA Metro have all tried different approaches: full barriers, proof-of-payment, and citation blitzes. The data suggests the blitz is the worst of the three options.

LONG-FORM YOUTUBE (5–8 MINUTES)

HOOK (0:00–0:30)

In 2022, New York's MTA announced that fare evasion was costing the agency $690 million a year — roughly the annual operating budget of the entire Cleveland transit system. [Source: MTA Fare Evasion Quarterly Report, 2022] So the MTA hired 500 more officers, at a two-year cost of $249 million. [Source: MTA Board Budget Documents, 2022] Was that actually going to make the math work? Spoiler: it didn't.

INTRO / STAKES (0:30–1:30)

Fare evasion generates enormous political heat relative to its actual fiscal weight. What almost never enters the debate is the actual economics: how much does enforcement cost, dollar for dollar? What do you recover? And what are the indirect costs — riders who stop riding because the system feels hostile, legal challenges, racial disparities that create political liability?

Today we walk through the math for three systems: New York City (full barriers + police), Los Angeles (proof-of-payment + fare inspectors), and Washington DC Metro (between the two, repeatedly changed direction).

SECTION 1: HOW WE GOT HERE (1:30–3:00)

American transit agencies have been fighting fare evasion since they've been collecting fares. In the 1980s and 90s, European systems began experimenting with proof-of-payment (POP) — passengers buy a ticket before boarding, inspectors make random sweeps. Edmonton's LRT was the first North American system to try it in 1980. [Source: Wikipedia, Proof of Payment]

LA Metro built its rail system as a proof-of-payment network in the 1990s. Evasion rates ranged from 5% to over 20% on some lines. [Source: LA Metro Fare Compliance Reports; LA Times, 2019–2022] NYC took the opposite approach: turnstiles everywhere, police everywhere. Washington DC Metro has full barriers but still struggled with enforcement gaps.

SECTION 2: THE ENFORCEMENT MATH (3:00–5:00)

Here's where it gets counterintuitive. When transit agencies talk about fare evasion, they focus on the revenue loss. What they don't put in the same press release: the cost of enforcement.

NYC's fare enforcement surge: $249 million over two years for 500 additional officers. Roughly $250,000 per officer per year including salary, benefits, training, and overhead. [Source: MTA Budget Documents, 2022]

A 2019 NYC Comptroller analysis found that processing a single fare evasion summons through the civil court system cost significantly more than the fine recovered. When evasion was still treated criminally, each arrest cost the system an estimated $500 to $2,000+ in court time, public defender costs, and officer overtime. [Source: NYC Comptroller Scott Stringer, "An Assessment of the NYPD Subway Fare Evasion Enforcement," 2019] The fine, if collected, was $100.

Washington DC decriminalized fare evasion in 2018, reducing the penalty from up to 10 days in jail to a $50 civil citation. The explicit rationale: over 90% of citations were issued to Black residents — wildly disproportionate to the offense. [Source: DC Council records, 2018]

The indirect costs: when transit feels hostile, some riders who would have evaded simply don't ride at all. TransitCenter has documented that aggressive enforcement disproportionately discourages low-income riders and riders of color from using the system. [Source: TransitCenter, "Who's On Board"] This is a fiscal problem, not just an equity one.

SECTION 3: WHAT THE DATA SHOWS (5:00–7:00)

New York: Evasion rate ~13%. Enforcement cost of the 2022 surge alone: $249M. Net fiscal benefit: analysts characterized it as at best break-even, likely negative when full costs are counted.

LA Metro: Evasion rates sometimes cited at 20%+ before gate installation. The TAP gate program cost $154 million in capital investment. [Source: LA Metro] Enforcement approach criticized for inconsistency and racial profiling complaints.

Washington DC: Full-barrier stations, but estimated tens of millions annually in evasion losses from back-door bus boarding and broken gates. After decriminalization in 2018, evasion rates rose modestly. [Source: WMATA Inspector General, Fare Evasion Audit, 2022]

The research on proof-of-payment systems: an inspection rate of ~10-15% of riders is sufficient to deter most evasion. Below that, the rational-actor calculation shifts toward skipping payment. [Source: Cervero, "Transit Metropolis," 1998]

The question transit agencies almost never seriously ask: if we have $250 million to spend on transit improvement, is enforcement the highest-value use of that money? Or does the same money invested in service frequency, reliability, or fare affordability generate more net revenue by growing ridership?

OUTRO (7:00–8:00)

Fare evasion is real, the revenue loss is real, and agencies aren't wrong to take it seriously. But the default response — more officers, more citations, more visible enforcement — consistently fails to close the gap, and often costs more than it recovers. The agencies getting this right are the ones asking the full question: not "how do we collect more fares?" but "how do we maximize net transit revenue?" Those aren't always the same question.
