Transit-Oriented
Development

A strategy for building compact, walkable, mixed-use communities centered on high-quality public transit — designed to reduce car dependence, cut emissions, and create more livable cities.

“The essence of TOD is not just about transportation — it’s about creating livable, sustainable communities.” — Peter Calthorpe, urbanist and TOD pioneer
01 — What is it

The Core Idea

Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) concentrates housing, jobs, shops, and services within a half-mile walking radius of a transit hub — train station, light rail stop, or bus rapid transit terminal. The idea is simple: put the places people need to go next to the systems that take them there, and design the streets in between to be walkable and human-scaled.

What It Looks Like

A TOD district typically has a transit station at its center, surrounded by dense mixed-use buildings — ground-floor retail with apartments or offices above. Streets are narrow enough to cross comfortably, blocks are small, and parking is limited. As you move outward from the station, density gradually decreases. The result is a neighborhood that functions well without a car.

Where It Comes From

The term was popularized by architect Peter Calthorpe in the early 1990s, though the underlying pattern — build dense around transit — is as old as the streetcar suburbs of the 1800s. Modern TOD formalizes those principles with equity, sustainability, and mixed-income housing as explicit goals.

🌍Environment
01
Cuts Carbon Emissions Dramatically
TOD reduces reliance on personal vehicles — the single largest source of urban CO₂. Dense, transit-connected neighborhoods generate far less greenhouse gas per resident than sprawling suburbs.
Why it matters
City centers built around transit emit up to 3× less CO₂ per capita than car-dependent suburbs — making TOD one of the most effective climate tools at a city’s disposal.

02
Saves Trillions in Infrastructure Costs
Sprawl is expensive to service — every road, pipe, and power line must stretch further. Compact TOD clusters demand within a smaller footprint, dramatically reducing what governments spend building and maintaining infrastructure.
Why it matters
Compact urban growth could save over $17 trillion in global infrastructure costs between 2015 and 2050 — funds that can be reinvested in housing, schools, and transit itself.

🏥Health
03
Safer Streets, Cleaner Air, Healthier Lives
Walkable TOD neighborhoods encourage daily physical activity and expose residents to less traffic pollution. Fewer cars on the road means fewer crashes, less exhaust, and better air quality for everyone.
Why it matters
1.35 million people die in traffic crashes globally each year. Compactness reduces fatalities by 1.5% for every 1% increase in density — a measurable, scalable lifesaving effect.

⚖Equity
04
Connects Low-Income Workers to Opportunity
Car ownership is expensive and not universally accessible. TOD gives residents — especially lower-income households — reliable access to jobs, schools, healthcare, and services without depending on a personal vehicle.
Why it matters
In Mexico City, commuting consumes up to 25% of daily wages for low-income workers — TOD slashes that burden by putting transit at the center of where people live.
✓
Links workers to employment centers without requiring car ownership
✓
Creates construction, maintenance, and transit operations jobs locally
✓

🏙Urbanism
05
Activates Streets and Local Economies
Mixed-use TOD fills ground floors with shops, cafes, and services that benefit from constant foot traffic. Streets become places — not just corridors for cars — generating commerce, community, and civic life at the neighborhood scale.
Why it matters
Chicago’s Wicker Park TOD catalyzed sustained economic growth around the Division Blue Line station, combining 99 mixed-income apartments with office space and ground-floor retail.

Gives People Their Time Back
Long car commutes are a daily tax on time and mental health. TOD compresses distances and provides frequent, reliable transit — so residents spend less of their lives in traffic and more time living.

In Dar es Salaam, a new BRT system cut average commute times from 2 hours to 45 minutes — giving workers back over an hour every single day.
