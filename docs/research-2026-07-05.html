Executive Summary

This week's transit research reveals an accelerating convergence of AI, electrification, and equity — from NYC's congestion pricing generating measurable ridership gains to physics-anchored models exposing the cold-climate vulnerabilities of battery-electric bus fleets. A striking throughline is governance: whether managing AI aggregators for V2G operations, ensuring mobility-first reserve certification for hydrogen bus depots, or catching bias in commercial GPS mobility data, the papers collectively show that technical innovation without institutional frameworks risks replicating — or amplifying — inequity. The most actionable finding is the NYC congestion pricing result: the first U.S. cordon pricing program demonstrably boosted transit ridership, providing evidence that pricing interventions can be a transit funding and ridership strategy simultaneously.

Emerging Themes

Zero-Emission Fleet Resilience Engineering

Three separate papers (cold-wave e-bus, hydrogen hub certification, V2G aggregator governance) converge on the same insight: electrification introduces new failure modes that require new engineering frameworks, not just new vehicles. The operational complexity of zero-emission fleets — balancing battery state, hydrogen availability, grid services, and passenger service — demands purpose-built certification and management tools.

AI-Driven Planning Needs Equity Guardrails

Papers on elderly mobility modeling, GPS data bias, and causal scooter hub planning all reveal that AI planning tools trained on majority-group data systematically exclude marginalized populations. More capable models do not fix the problem — only targeted demographic data collection and representation requirements do. This is a policy gap regulators have not yet addressed.

Pricing as Transit Strategy

The NYC congestion pricing study and the AAM subsidy design paper together suggest that pricing mechanisms — both to discourage car use and to subsidize multimodal connections — are increasingly central to transit planning. The era of treating pricing as a revenue instrument separate from ridership strategy is ending; these papers show the two are inseparable.

Contrarian Insights

Public transit gains and spatially uneven travel demand changes after NYC congestion pricing

Donghang Li, Dingyi Zhuang, Yunlin Li, Chenan Shen

NYC's first-in-nation cordon congestion pricing program, launched January 2025, measurably boosted bus and subway ridership while redistributing trip demand unevenly across the city.

Why It Matters

This is the first empirical evaluation of how a full U.S. cordon pricing scheme reshapes transit ridership in real time, using novel time-series foundation models as counterfactuals — a methodological breakthrough for policy evaluation.

Key Insights

  • Post-policy bus and subway ridership increased significantly above counterfactual forecasts
  • Outer-borough destinations gained relatively more trip demand than Manhattan core destinations
  • Time series foundation models provide probabilistic counterfactuals where traditional control groups fail
  • Congestion pricing generates spillovers across modes and locations, making siloed analysis misleading
  • Spatial heterogeneity in demand response means one headline number obscures critical equity patterns

Methodology

Time series foundation models generate probabilistic counterfactual forecasts with calibrated uncertainty using pre-intervention data, then compare actual post-policy data against those forecasts for bus, subway, and aggregate trip volume.

Results

Bus and subway ridership statistically exceeded counterfactual forecasts post-January 2025; outer-borough trip volumes grew relative to Manhattan core; aggregate trip volume showed spatially heterogeneous patterns.

Limitations

Foundation model counterfactuals rely on assumption that pre-intervention trends would have continued; external shocks concurrent with pricing could confound results; cannot fully separate congestion pricing effect from NYC economic trends.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies in congestion-priced cities should prepare capacity for ridership surges, especially in outer-borough corridors. Equity monitoring must be spatially granular — aggregate gains can mask localized burdens.

Who Should Care

Transit planners, urban economists, MTA leadership, congestion pricing advocates and opponents, equity researchers

vs Prior Work

Advances beyond Stockholm and London studies by using ML counterfactuals rather than synthetic control, and by being the first to analyze a U.S. cordon pricing program at this spatial granularity.

Computing Headway Bounds under Worst-Case Bunching in Fixed-Line Transit Systems

Michael Yuhas, George Gunter, Jose Paulo Talusan, et al.

A new dynamic programming framework computes tight upper and lower bounds on bus headway under any holding policy, revealing exactly which conditions produce worst-case bunching.

Why It Matters

Bus bunching is transit's most persistent service quality failure; this paper provides the first method to provably bound worst-case headway — enabling rigorous planning rather than heuristic mitigation.

Key Insights

  • Maximum and minimum headway bounds are achievable — specific event sequences that produce them can be identified
  • More control points do not always improve worst-case performance depending on placement
  • Holding policies trade riding time for headway regularity with quantifiable costs
  • Applied to Nashville TN network, bounds reveal which routes are structurally vulnerable to bunching
  • Number of vehicles and stop placement have non-obvious interactions affecting worst-case bunching

Methodology

Dynamic programming over time-space networks with bounded dwell and travel times; models arbitrary number of control points, vehicles, and deterministic holding policies; computes tight bounds by identifying adversarial event sequences.

Results

Bounds are tight — the framework produces the specific dwell/travel time realization achieving each bound. Nashville case study identifies routes where even optimal holding fails to prevent significant headway deviation.

Limitations

Assumes deterministic travel time bounds; stochastic analysis would require extension; does not model passenger behavior changes in response to holding; single-route analysis excludes network-level interactions.

Practical Implications

Agencies can use this tool in planning stage to compare route designs, holding strategies, and fleet sizes before deployment, eliminating the need for costly simulation studies for worst-case analysis.

Who Should Care

Transit operations researchers, bus network planners, dispatchers, software developers building scheduling tools

vs Prior Work

Prior work focused on average-case bunching and stochastic simulation; this is the first approach computing guaranteed worst-case bounds analytically.

When the Timetable Breaks: Physics-Anchored Scientific Machine Learning for Cold-Wave-Robust Battery-Electric Bus Operations

Yifan Wang

A physics-anchored ML framework predicts battery-electric bus energy failure cascades during cold waves and shows that opportunity charging — not just better forecasting — is the key intervention.

Why It Matters

As agencies race to electrify bus fleets, winter operational failures are an existential threat to electrification goals; this paper provides actionable, validated tools for managing cold-climate e-bus timetable resilience.

Key Insights

  • Cabin heating is the dominant cold-weather energy drain, not traction, and creates cascading block failures
  • Pure ML models degrade 1.5-4x versus physics-anchored hybrid in out-of-support cold tail temperatures
  • Opportunity charging is the most effective single intervention, reducing failure probability from 0.759 to 0.112
  • Fuel-fired cabin-heating bridge is low-cost complement that further reduces risk without major infrastructure
  • Standard seasonal energy margins systematically hide cold-wave vulnerabilities in fleet planning

Methodology

Physics-hybrid universal differential equation building-load twin validated against EnergyPlus; bounded monotone residual ensemble; Monte Carlo block-feasibility simulation over 60 real TTC vehicle blocks; deconfounded ablation study for intervention ranking.

Results

Achieves 0.213 kWh RMSE over full year, lowest of all tested models; combined policy reduces cold-wave failure probability from 0.759 to 0.112 across eight cold-wave days at Toronto TTC.

Limitations

Validated on Toronto TTC data; transferability to other cities or bus models requires revalidation; fuel-fired heater introduces emissions, partially compromising zero-emission goals.

Practical Implications

Cold-climate agencies should audit their electrification plans against cold-wave scenarios, prioritize opportunity charging infrastructure at key layover points, and consider auxiliary cabin heat as bridging technology.

Who Should Care

Transit electrification planners, cold-climate agencies (Minneapolis, Chicago, Toronto, Montreal), fleet procurement officers, EV charging infrastructure planners

vs Prior Work

Extends prior e-bus range-anxiety literature from individual vehicle to fleet-level block feasibility, adding novel physics-anchored cold-weather modeling not present in prior traction-only models.

When Agents Meet Electric Bus Fleet Operations: Pricing Behavior, Trade-offs, and Policy Implications in an Aggregator Framework

Jônatas Augusto Manzolli, Ali Eslami, Luis Miranda-Moreno

AI-powered aggregators can coordinate electric bus V2G operations effectively but may extract value from transit operators unless governance explicitly mandates transparent pricing and value-sharing rules.

Why It Matters

As AI-driven energy intermediaries enter transit, this paper is the first to demonstrate the conflict between agentic efficiency and public-mission values in EV fleet management.

Key Insights

  • Agentic aggregators successfully reduce operational complexity and improve V2G flexibility utilization
  • Profit-oriented agentic pricing can extract value from public transport operators — the same capability cuts both ways
  • Real-time re-optimization under disturbances (service delays, price shocks) is where agentic value is highest
  • Transparent coordination modes and auditable tariff-setting are necessary preconditions for public-fleet deployment
  • Aggregator governance is a policy gap that regulators have not yet addressed for transit contexts

Methodology

Mixed-integer optimization core for scheduling feasibility; supervisory agent layer for disturbance detection and re-optimization triggers; depot case study with profit-based vs operation-based coordination modes under five disturbance scenarios.

Results

Agentic aggregation maintains feasible schedules under most disturbance scenarios; profit-oriented mode generates higher aggregator revenue at cost of public transport operator; operation-based mode provides lower but more equitable outcomes.

Limitations

Single-depot case study; does not model strategic behavior of multiple competing aggregators; tariff regulation scenarios not fully explored.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies considering V2G programs should demand operation-based coordination contracts with auditable pricing before signing aggregator agreements; regulators should develop V2G governance frameworks for public fleets.

Who Should Care

Transit agency CFOs, energy regulators, EV fleet operators, public-private partnership lawyers, climate policy researchers

vs Prior Work

First paper to explicitly model the governance tension between agentic aggregators and public transit missions in V2G literature.

Mobility Safe Adaptive Reserve Certification for Electric Vehicle Hydrogen Bus and Building Resilience Hubs

Yifan Wang

A new mobility-first certification framework for shared zero-emission hubs guarantees 100% hydrogen bus service protection while still delivering backup power to critical buildings during grid outages.

Why It Matters

Multi-modal zero-emission depots are emerging as dual-purpose infrastructure, but without proper reserve certification they risk stranding buses to serve buildings — this framework resolves that conflict with validated math.

Key Insights

  • Mobility-blind hydrogen export policies delivered zero bus protection in tested scenarios despite serving 39% of building demand
  • The certified mobility-first policy is the only approach achieving 100% commitment delivery and bus protection simultaneously
  • Adaptive conformal inference raised late-period empirical coverage from 0.687 to 0.831 under seasonal load shift
  • Resilience value in shared zero-emission hubs depends entirely on service-aware certification, not raw export capacity
  • Standard promises (mean-resource) delivered only 45% of commitments in testing

Methodology

Physics-hybrid building-load digital twin; one-sided split conformal reserve calibration; adaptive conformal inference for seasonal drift; mobility-first scheduling constraint; evaluated over 495,221 real EV charging sessions and EnergyPlus-simulated building loads.

Results

Mobility-first policy: 100% commitment delivery, 100% bus protection, zero mean bus-hydrogen shortfall, 20.5% critical-building demand served. Mobility-blind: 0% bus protection, 39.2% building demand served.

Limitations

Results depend on AC Transit GTFS data and California energy context; hydrogen bus economics vary by region; building backup scenarios are simplified.

Practical Implications

Transit agencies building zero-emission depots with grid backup functions should require mobility-first certification before signing building backup contracts.

Who Should Care

Transit capital planners, resilience engineers, utility regulators, hydrogen infrastructure developers, emergency management agencies

vs Prior Work

Novel application of conformal prediction methods to transit-energy integration, extending prior work on EV grid services to multi-modal hydrogen-bus context.

Towards Inclusive Mobility Modeling: Characterizing and Evaluating Elderly Trajectory Patterns in Urban Systems

Zhengxuan Wang, Haohan He, Mengying Zhou

Elderly riders have fundamentally different and more localized mobility patterns than young riders, and AI models trained on majority populations systematically misrepresent their behavior.

Why It Matters

As cities use AI to plan transit, systematic underrepresentation of elderly mobility creates infrastructure that serves young riders while failing aging populations — the fastest-growing rider demographic.

Key Insights

  • Elderly activity spaces are 24% smaller than young riders (958m vs 1,189m median trip radius)
  • Elderly mobility entropy is 2.3x lower, reflecting more repetitive, predictable trip patterns
  • Models trained on full population overestimate elderly step length by 4.5% and dwell time by 8.9%
  • More powerful LLMs do not automatically improve subgroup representation with limited demographic data
  • Off-peak asymmetric temporal patterns of elderly riders are systematically missed by majority-trained models

Methodology

Analysis of 2016-2020 Jersey City Citi Bike data; entropy and spatial metrics computed per demographic group; Markov chain and QLoRA-fine-tuned Qwen3-4B models compared across three training settings (full, young-only, elderly-only).

Results

Elderly-specific models substantially outperform full-population models on elderly mobility metrics; higher-capability models do not close the demographic gap without targeted training data.

Limitations

Citi Bike data represents self-selecting bike-share users, not full elderly transit population; Jersey City may not generalize to other cities; age proxies from registration data may be imprecise.

Practical Implications

Urban planners should demand demographic-disaggregated mobility models for transit planning; AI vendors should be required to report subgroup performance metrics, not just aggregate accuracy.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, transit accessibility officers, aging services agencies, AI ethics researchers, smart city vendors

vs Prior Work

First paper to directly compare LLM and Markov model performance on elderly vs young mobility generation, revealing the limits of model capability as a substitute for representation.

From Causal Discovery to Implementation: An Agentic AI Framework for E-Scooter Mobility Hub Planning Across 29 German Cities

Meng Jin, Melanie Handrich, Simone Martinenz, Nicholas Hoeser

An AI framework using causal discovery on open GBFS data across 29 German cities reveals that core and peripheral e-scooter hub demand have fundamentally different causal drivers, enabling transferable siting templates.

Why It Matters

Existing scooter siting relies on correlational demand models — this is the first causal framework that explains WHY certain locations succeed, enabling cities without proprietary data to make better decisions.

Key Insights

  • Core demand causally driven by activity access and transit proximity — confirming transit-complementary hypothesis
  • Peripheral demand responds primarily to built form — density, land use — not transit access
  • City typology (large, university, industrial, hilly) systematically shapes which causal drivers dominate
  • Causal Template Library enables transferable siting without requiring expensive proprietary trip data
  • Two hub sites in Heilbronn Germany under construction based on framework findings — rare real-world validation

Methodology

LLM-orchestrated causal discovery pipeline adapting algorithm selection to local data conditions; 57 city-cluster units across 29 cities; planning tool scoring candidate sites with calibrated recommendations.

Results

City-type-specific causal patterns identified; planning tool generates practitioner-ready site reports; Heilbronn implementation validates real-world applicability.

Limitations

GBFS data captures only shared scooter trips, not full micromobility demand; causal discovery assumes no hidden confounders; Germany-specific results may not transfer globally.

Practical Implications

Cities planning micromobility integration should use causal (not correlational) demand models; open GBFS data is sufficient for rigorous planning without expensive third-party datasets.

Who Should Care

Micromobility operators, city planners, transit integration specialists, open data advocates

vs Prior Work

First causal (vs correlational) micromobility demand model; first city-typology-specific analysis across European cities.

Distance-based subsidy rate design to incentivize ride-hail access to advanced air mobility hubs

Zhenglei Ji, Hai Yang, Joseph Y. J. Chow

Air taxi operators will need to subsidize ride-hail trips to vertiports whenever air taxi costs exceed $12/mile, with subsidy rates varying significantly by Manhattan vertiport location due to spatial demand heterogeneity.

Why It Matters

Advanced air mobility can only scale if first/last-mile ground access is solved; this paper establishes the economic mechanics of AAM-ridehail cooperation and identifies the subsidy threshold that makes it viable.

Key Insights

  • AAM operators must subsidize ride-hail access when air taxi operating costs exceed $12 per mile
  • Ridership and profit contributions vary dramatically across candidate Manhattan vertiport locations
  • Demand zones with highest AAM airport access potential concentrate in specific NYC taxi zones under lower fare scenarios
  • Distance-based subsidy outperforms flat-rate subsidies in balancing ridership and operator profitability
  • Passenger value-of-time heterogeneity requires separate subsidy designs for different customer segments

Methodology

High-volume for-hire vehicle NYC data integration; mode-choice model with heterogeneous value-of-time groups; joint optimization of subsidy rate and air taxi fare; spatial demand analysis across NYC taxi zones.

Results

Distance-based subsidies enable viable AAM-ridehail coalitions at operating costs above $12/mile; Manhattan spatial heterogeneity means optimal vertiport locations require demand-aware subsidy design.

Limitations

NYC-specific analysis; AAM operating cost projections uncertain; ride-hail platform cooperation assumptions may not hold in competitive market.

Practical Implications

AAM operators should build subsidy mechanisms into business plans from launch; vertiport siting must account for ground-mode access costs as primary criterion.

Who Should Care

AAM companies (Joby, Archer, Lilium), urban air mobility planners, ride-hail platform strategists, airport authorities

vs Prior Work

First paper to formalize the AAM-ridehail subsidy problem as a joint optimization with distance-based rates and heterogeneous value-of-time.

Virginia Tech Transportation Safety Index (VTTSI)

Jason Cusati, Cheng-Shun Chuang

VTTSI is a real-time intersection safety scoring system fusing connected-vehicle telemetry with crash history to produce updated safety scores every 15 minutes on a 0-100 scale.

Why It Matters

Current intersection safety analysis uses crash data that is months or years old; real-time scoring enables dynamic signal timing, enforcement targeting, and rapid response to emerging dangerous conditions.

Key Insights

  • Traditional crash-based safety methods lag by months to years, missing rapidly changing operational conditions
  • Combining speed/conflict behavioral data with crash history outperforms either source alone
  • CRITIC-weighted MCDM integrating SAW, EDAS, CODAS metrics produces more robust scores than single-method approaches
  • 15-minute update cycles enable actionable intervention within operational shift windows
  • Empirical Bayes crash stabilization corrects for low sample-size bias in crash-history metrics

Methodology

Multimodal connected-vehicle telemetry fusion with multi-year VDOT crash history; Empirical Bayes crash stabilization; speed and conflict behavior uplift factors; CRITIC-weighted MCDM combining SAW, EDAS, CODAS; cloud-native real-time architecture.

Results

System produces interpretable 0-100 safety scores updated every 15 minutes; validated against VDOT historical crash data; framework operational for Virginia intersections.

Limitations

Requires connected-vehicle penetration for telemetry inputs; effectiveness depends on CV market share; Virginia-specific calibration may need adjustment for other states.

Practical Implications

DOTs should integrate real-time safety scoring into signal operations centers; law enforcement can use live scores for deployment targeting; transit agencies can use scores to flag intersections for operator alerts.

Who Should Care

State DOTs, traffic management centers, law enforcement, transit safety officers, connected vehicle platform providers

vs Prior Work

First framework combining real-time connected-vehicle conflict data with Empirical Bayes crash history in a production-quality operational safety index.

Perceiving exposure segregation with open urban imagery

Yunke Zhang, Ruolong Ma, Xin Zhang, Yu Shang, Fengli Xu, Tong Xia, Yong Li

The VISAGE framework detects socioeconomic segregation patterns in US cities from street and satellite imagery, finding that fences, gated enclosures, and monofunctional zoning physically encode social isolation.

Why It Matters

Built-environment features that generate transit deserts are the same ones that produce segregation; this paper shows the physical mechanisms are readable from public imagery, enabling scalable policy diagnosis.

Key Insights

  • Defensible architectural forms (fences, gated enclosures) are the strongest visual predictors of social isolation
  • Monofunctional zoning systematically predicts higher segregation versus mixed-use infrastructure
  • Inclusionary housing policies leave detectable visual signatures in street imagery (Pearson r=0.770 with mixing)
  • VISAGE framework scales to 10,030 communities across 31 cities without proprietary data
  • Physical regulators of social mixing can now be identified from open imagery, enabling systematic planning audits

Methodology

Large multimodal model (VISAGE) trained on satellite and street-level imagery; interpretable visual codebook from sociological theory; validated against mobility-derived segregation measures across 31 US cities.

Results

Visual codebook explains substantial variance in mobility-based segregation (r=0.770); defensible architecture and monofunctional zoning emerge as dominant negative predictors; mixed-use infrastructure as positive predictor.

Limitations

Causal direction unclear — segregation may cause built environment, not vice versa; Google Street View temporal coverage varies; urban context outside US may differ.

Practical Implications

Urban planners can use open imagery audits to identify segregation-perpetuating built form before expensive planning studies; transit investment prioritization can incorporate visual-segregation scores.

Who Should Care

Urban planners, equity researchers, transit equity advocates, housing policy officials, computer vision researchers

vs Prior Work

First paper to operationalize sociological segregation theory as a visual codebook and validate it against observed mobility data at city scale.

One country, multiple portraits: representativeness in GPS-based mobility data is source-specific and spatially dependent

Carmen Cabrera, Francisco Rowe, Miguel González-Leonardo, Juan Ignacio Vilchis-García

GPS mobility data from different platforms systematically misrepresents different populations — multi-app aggregators miss rural and low-income areas while Facebook data covers them more evenly.

Why It Matters

Transit planners using commercial mobility data to design routes may be systematically planning around wealthier, better-connected populations, amplifying inequality precisely where equity planning is most needed.

Key Insights

  • Coverage bias differs fundamentally between Facebook and multi-app (Veraset) data — they are not interchangeable
  • Multi-app aggregators concentrate coverage in larger, wealthier, more digitally connected municipalities
  • Facebook provides more spatially even coverage, better representing peripheral and rural areas
  • Digital access and material resources drive multi-app bias; demographics drive Facebook bias
  • Spatial autocorrelation in coverage bias means neighboring municipalities show similar under/over-representation

Methodology

Comparison of two GPS mobility data sources against 2020 Mexican Population Census for 2,478 municipalities; explainable ML (XGBoost with SHAP) to identify bias drivers; spatial autocorrelation analysis; geographic resolution across urban-rural continuum.

Results

Substantial and source-specific coverage bias confirmed; multi-app data more biased in rural and low-income areas; Facebook more evenly distributed but with demographic structure bias; spatial modeling explains remaining variation.

Limitations

Mexico-specific findings; temporal coverage limited; Facebook data access may change due to platform policy; does not address data quality (accuracy) beyond coverage.

Practical Implications

Agencies using commercial GPS data for planning must understand source-specific bias and apply corrections; multi-source fusion with bias adjustment is necessary for equitable mobility analysis.

Who Should Care

Transportation planners, equity researchers, smart city data scientists, government statisticians, GPS data vendors

vs Prior Work

First systematic cross-source comparison of GPS coverage bias at national scale with explainable ML attribution and spatial dependence modeling.
