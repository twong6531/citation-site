Value Capture: Making Transit Projects Pay for Themselves

A new subway station typically increases nearby property values by 10–25%. In most American cities, that windfall goes entirely to private landowners. Value capture financing is any mechanism that redirects some of that publicly created value back to fund the transit that generated it.

The Economic Logic of Value Capture

Transit infrastructure creates accessibility — the ability to reach jobs, schools, and amenities — and this accessibility gets capitalized into land prices. Research on Boston's Green Line extension found property values within a quarter-mile of new stations increased 10–15% relative to comparable properties. London's Elizabeth line (Crossrail) generated estimated uplift of £5.5 billion in property values along its corridor.

The standard critique of public transit investment is that it subsidizes private wealth creation. Value capture turns this argument on its head: if the transit creates the value, then capturing some of that value to pay for the transit is both economically efficient and politically defensible.

Tax Increment Financing (TIF)

Tax Increment Financing designates a transit corridor or station area as a TIF district. The property tax base at the time of district creation becomes the 'base.' Any incremental property tax revenue above that base — generated by rising property values after transit investment — is captured and directed to transit-related debt service or infrastructure costs.

Chicago's CTA has used TIF financing for station renovations. Denver used TIF to help finance the Union Station redevelopment adjacent to its commuter rail hub. Critics note that TIF can divert property tax revenue from schools and other services that also depend on that tax base.

Special Assessment Districts and Developer Contributions

Special assessment districts levy an additional charge on properties within a defined area on the logic that those properties directly benefit from the transit investment. London's Crossrail was partly funded through a Business Rate Supplement that levied an additional 2 pence per pound of rateable value on commercial properties in Greater London.

Developer contributions — impact fees or negotiated 'section 106' payments in the UK — require new developments near transit stations to contribute to transit infrastructure costs. Hudson Yards in New York was partly financed through a special assessment on the development site, generating over $3 billion to fund the No. 7 subway extension.

Rail Plus Property Development

The most aggressive value capture model — exemplified by Hong Kong's MTR — grants the transit authority development rights around stations, allowing it to build and profit from mixed-use development. MTR develops apartments, offices, and retail above or adjacent to stations and uses those profits to cross-subsidize transit operations.

In the U.S., BART's TOD program, Washington Metro's Joint Development, and the Los Angeles MTA's Joint Development Policy approximate this model. These programs generate meaningful revenue — BART's TOD projects generate over $10 million annually in lease payments — but far less than Hong Kong's model, where the transit-property relationship is deeper and the real estate market more extreme.

Key Takeaways
  • Transit stations typically increase nearby property values 10–25%, creating value capture opportunities.
  • London's Crossrail Elizabeth line generated an estimated £5.5 billion in property value uplift along its corridor.
  • New York's No. 7 subway extension was partly funded by a $3 billion special assessment on Hudson Yards development.
  • Hong Kong's MTR profits from transit-adjacent real estate development — a model requiring high density and development pressure.
