**SECTION 1: What Social Media Used to Be — and the Moment It Changed (1:30–3:00)**  
**VISUAL:** Archival screenshots of early Facebook (2007–2010 UI); engagement metric dashboards; News Feed algorithm announcement slides  

**VOICEOVER:**  
Facebook launched News Feed in 2006. It was, by today's standards, a radical act of transparency — a chronological stream of everything your friends did, unfiltered, unranked. Users hated it at first. Then couldn't imagine life without it. [Source: Facebook Blog, 2006]  

The first algorithmic intervention came quietly. In 2009, Facebook introduced a ranking signal — EdgeRank — that began deprioritizing posts deemed "low engagement" in favor of posts that got more comments and likes. The logic was sensible: show people the things most worth seeing. The execution was a wedge.  

**VISUAL:** Graph showing content type distribution — text updates, family photos, news links — over time; friend content shrinking  

By 2018, the shift had become structurally visible. Facebook announced an explicit pivot: the algorithm would deprioritize "passive content" — posts you read but didn't react to — in favor of content that prompted active engagement. In practice, this meant outrage, controversy, and emotionally provocative content rose. Quiet personal updates from friends fell. [Source: Mark Zuckerberg, Facebook post, January 2018]  

That same year, Instagram began showing posts out of chronological order, ranked by predicted engagement. TikTok had just launched in international markets with an algorithm that had no social graph at all — just a pure interest-prediction engine that served content from total strangers from the start. The "For You" page was the product. The social graph was an afterthought.

**ON SCREEN TEXT:** "TikTok's founding premise: you don't need friends. You need a better prediction model."

**SECTION 2: The Mechanism — Why "Fracking" Is the Right Metaphor (3:00–5:00)**  
**VISUAL:** Infographic comparing hydraulic fracking economics to engagement optimization — inputs, extraction yields, externalities  

**VOICEOVER:**  
The essay's central argument is that the fracking metaphor is technically precise, not just poetic. Here's why.

Conventional oil extraction — like conventional media — required high-quality deposits: good writing, good journalism, genuinely interesting content. There was a floor on what got made, because low-quality content couldn't compete.  

Fracking breaks that constraint. You can extract energy from formations that would previously have been uneconomical — if you're willing to use high-pressure injection, accept the environmental damage, and externalize those costs onto someone else. The extraction works. The damage is real. The company that fracked doesn't pay for it.

**VISUAL:** Engagement rate data — viral outrage content vs. thoughtful long-form content; screenshot of BBC investigation findings  

The same logic applies to algorithmic feeds. The "deposit" being extracted is human attention and neurochemical response. The algorithm has discovered that controversy, social comparison, fear, and rapid novelty generate higher response rates than quality content does. So it optimizes for those signals — regardless of what that does to the cultural substrate, to the user's mental state, to the actual quality of information being spread. [Source: BBC Worklife, 2026]  

The externalities — anxiety, shortened attention spans, eroded ability to focus or create, degraded social relationships — don't appear on any platform's P&L. They're fracked and left behind.
