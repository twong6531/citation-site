### SECTION 1 — BACKGROUND: BUILDING THE EMPIRE (1:30–3:00)

[VISUAL: Timeline graphic — 2016 to 2026]

Let's trace how we got here.

**2016:** The Boring Company is founded. Musk's stated goal: tunnel under cities to move cars at 150 mph. Transportation experts called it impractical. He built it anyway.

**2021:** The Las Vegas Convention Center Loop opens. 1.7 miles of tunnels, Teslas ferrying passengers. It has since grown to a planned 68-mile network beneath the city. [Source: The Boring Company]

Here's the regulatory detail that matters: because the Loop is privately funded, it bypassed the environmental reviews, public comment periods, and federal oversight required for any comparable public transit project. ProPublica reported that the Vegas Loop has "little oversight" — a $600 million infrastructure project that a city of 2 million people uses, with no federal vetting. [Source: ProPublica]

**2024–2025:** Tesla's Full Self-Driving rolls out unsupervised. Robotaxi opens to the public via official app. [Source: Hansshow/Tesla News, 2026]

**2025:** SpaceX buys $506 million of Tesla Megapack batteries. The Boring Company is paid $1 million by SpaceX to build tunnels in Bastrop, Texas — the same town where SpaceX has its launch facility. The companies are already one operational organism. [Source: FX Empire, 2026]

**June 12, 2026:** SpaceX IPOs at $135 a share. Trades up to $161. $2.2 trillion market cap on day one.

And SpaceX's president, Gwynne Shotwell, goes on record saying a Tesla merger would make Musk's life "easier." [Source: ECIKS / Yahoo Finance, 2026]

### SECTION 2 — THE CORE PROBLEM: PUBLIC TRANSIT IS COLLAPSING RIGHT NOW (3:00–5:00)

[VISUAL: Map of US with cities flashing red — Chicago, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Baltimore]

While Musk's empire was being assembled, public transit in America was being quietly defunded.

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act injected funding from 2022 to 2026. But that expires this year. And the COVID-era federal relief money that propped up operating budgets since 2021? Gone. [Source: CBO, 2026]

The result is catastrophic, and it's happening right now:

- **Philadelphia (SEPTA):** 45% service cuts. 21.5% fare increase. 32 bus routes already eliminated. [Source: Mass Transit Magazine, 2026]
- **Chicago (CTA):** Faces up to 40% service cuts. The RTA says the funding gap is $200 million in 2026 — and could balloon back to $790 million in 2027. [Source: CBS News Chicago, 2026]
- **Nationally:** Transit agencies face a $6.6 billion shortfall through FY2026. Ridership has only recovered to 85% of pre-pandemic levels. [Source: GovTech, 2026]

[VISUAL: Bar chart — transit funding shortfall per city, stacked against Boring Company expansion map]

Here's the structural issue. Public transit requires democratic approval — ballot measures, environmental impact statements, federal grant applications, ADA compliance reviews. A new bus line in Chicago takes years and costs millions just to approve. A Boring Company tunnel can break ground without a public hearing if it's privately funded.

That asymmetry is the entire story.

When a public system is forced to cut 40% of its service, the question isn't just "who fills the gap?" The question is: **on what terms?**

A bus line is a public utility. It can't price out low-income riders. It has to serve every neighborhood, including the ones that aren't profitable. A Tesla robotaxi doesn't have that obligation. A Boring Company Loop ticket costs what the market will bear.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side price comparison graphic — CTA bus fare ($2.50) vs. projected Loop fare ($10-25 per trip, estimated from Vegas pricing)]

### SECTION 3 — IMPLICATIONS AND WHAT COMES NEXT (5:00–7:00)

[VISUAL: Three scenarios displayed as branching paths]

Let me give you three scenarios, ordered from least to most disruptive.

**Scenario A: Parallel systems.** Boring Company tunnels serve downtown cores. Tesla robotaxis handle suburbs. Public transit continues serving low-income corridors. Cities end up with a two-tier mobility system — fast private transit for those who can pay, degraded public service for everyone else. This is the most likely near-term outcome.

**Scenario B: Selective substitution.** Cash-strapped cities, looking at $6.6 billion in deficits, begin contracting with Musk's companies to fill service gaps. Las Vegas has already demonstrated this is politically viable — the Loop is positioned as a feature, not a threat. Cities learn to rationalize the trade-off. Federal antitrust frameworks, written before autonomous tunnels existed, don't intervene. [Source: California Policy Center analysis]

**Scenario C: Regulatory capture.** A merged SpaceX-Tesla entity, with $3.4 trillion in market cap and a CEO with deep ties to the current federal administration, shapes transit policy directly. The IIJA reauthorization — due now — gets written to favor autonomous vehicle infrastructure over bus and rail. DOT's definition of "public transit" quietly expands to include private robotaxi networks. Federal transit grants begin flowing toward platforms that work with Tesla's data stack.

[VISUAL: Timeline showing IIJA expiration in 2026 + lobbying spend chart for mobility companies]

None of these scenarios requires conspiracy. They just require incentives and inertia.

What the data tells us: the public transit crisis is real, the private mobility infrastructure is operational, the capital is newly liquid, and the regulatory gap is significant. The pieces are in place.

**What cities can do:**
1. Demand that any Boring Company or Tesla contract include public-interest obligations — route coverage mandates, ADA compliance, fare caps.
2. Treat transit funding as infrastructure, not discretionary — the IIJA reauthorization fight happening right now is the lever point.
3. Pay attention to how "public-private partnership" gets defined in the next five years. A PPP that privatizes profit and socializes risk is just privatization.
