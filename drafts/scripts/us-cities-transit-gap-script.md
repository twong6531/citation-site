**SECTION 1: Background — America's Transit Golden Age and How It Ended (1:30–3:00)**  
**VISUAL:** Archival photos of US streetcar systems in 1920s–1940s; animated ridership chart peaking in 1946 then collapsing  

**VOICEOVER:**

[Source: American Public Transportation Association historical data]

Then came the dismantling.

**Decision 1: The Streetcar Buyout (1938–1950)**  
**VISUAL:** Map of cities where National City Lines operated; timeline of acquisitions  
Between 1938 and 1950, a consortium including General Motors, Firestone Tire, Standard Oil of California, Phillips Petroleum, and Mack Trucks invested in a holding company called National City Lines. By 1947, NCL controlled 46 transit systems in 45 cities across 16 states. [Source: Wikipedia — General Motors streetcar conspiracy]  
They converted streetcar lines to buses — GM buses, on Firestone tires, running on Standard Oil fuel.  
In 1949, they were convicted of monopolizing bus supply sales to their subsidiaries. GM's fine: $5,000. [Source: US v. National City Lines, 1949]  
Historians debate how decisive this was — streetcars were already under pressure from fixed fares, inflation, and rising car ownership. But in cities like Los Angeles, the last Red Car ran in 1961. It took LA 49 years to build back a comparable light rail spine.

**ON SCREEN TEXT:** "GM's fine for dismantling American transit: $5,000."

**Decision 2: The Highway Trust Fund Shuts Transit Out (1956–1982)**  
**VISUAL:** Animated graphic — gas tax revenue flowing 100% into highway construction; transit bucket empty  
**VOICEOVER:**  
The Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956 created the Interstate Highway System. The original cost estimate was $25 billion. The actual cost came to $114 billion — $634 billion in today's dollars. [Source: Wikipedia — Interstate Highway System]  
The federal government covered 90% of that. States paid 10%.  
And the fund that paid for it — the Highway Trust Fund — collected a federal gas tax from every American driver. Every cent went to roads.  
Transit was legally excluded until 1982, when the Surface Transportation Assistance Act finally carved out one cent of the nine-cent gas tax for a new Mass Transit Account. [Source: Surface Transportation Assistance Act, 1982]  
That's an 8-to-1 highway-to-transit ratio built directly into the tax code — and it ran for 26 uninterrupted years before transit got a dollar.

**ON SCREEN TEXT:** "26 years of highway-only federal investment before transit got a cent."

**Decision 3: FHA Loans Subsidized Suburban Sprawl, Not Urban Density**  
**VOICEOVER:**  
Starting in 1934, the Federal Housing Administration guaranteed mortgages — but its underwriting standards explicitly favored suburban, single-family developments and redlined urban and mixed-race neighborhoods. [Source: National Housing Act, 1934; FHA underwriting manuals]  
The effect: a massive, federally subsidized population transfer from transit-walkable cities to car-dependent suburbs. By 1970, more Americans lived in suburbs than cities for the first time in history.  
Transit systems need density to function. You need roughly 30 housing units per acre to support frequent bus service. Most postwar suburbs built at 3–8 units per acre. That's below the viability threshold for any transit that runs more often than once an hour.

You can't run transit through a parking lot. Freeway construction physically eliminated the urban density that transit depends on.

**Decision 5: Transit Finally Got Federal Money — But Only for Capital, Not Operations**  
**VISUAL:** Graph showing US transit capital vs. operating subsidies vs. peer nations  
**VOICEOVER:**  
When federal transit funding finally arrived — with the Urban Mass Transportation Act of 1964, a $375 million program — there was a catch. [Source: Urban Mass Transportation Act, 1964]  
Federal dollars could build transit infrastructure. They couldn't pay for operating it — the drivers, the maintenance, the daily cost of actually running service.  
European and Asian transit systems receive substantial operating subsidies as a matter of public policy. The US decision to fund capital but not operations created a permanent structural problem: cities could get federal money to build a rail line, but had to find local dollars to run it. Many couldn't. Systems degraded. Ridership fell. The political case for more investment weakened.
