**[1:30–3:00 — SECTION 1: What Is an Axial Flux Motor, and Why Has It Been Locked in Hypercars?]**
*(Voiceover + graphics heavy)*

"Let's start with the physics, because it's actually elegant.

In a conventional radial flux motor, the gap between your rotor and stator runs perpendicular to the axis of rotation — think of a cylinder spinning inside another cylinder. [Source: Wikipedia, Axial Flux Motor, 2026] Axial flux flips that geometry. The gap runs parallel to the rotation axis. Your rotor and stator face each other like two plates on a disc. That's why engineers call it a pancake motor.

That geometry has a massive consequence: torque scales with the cube of the rotor's diameter. In a radial motor, it only scales quadratically. [Source: Wikipedia, Axial Flux Motor, 2026] In plain terms: make the disc a little wider, you get a lot more torque. Make the motor thinner, you lose almost nothing.

The result is extraordinary power density. YASA — the Oxford-founded company that Mercedes-Benz acquired — achieved 42 kilowatts per kilogram. To put that in context, a competitive conventional EV motor delivers around 16 kW/kg. [Source: Wikipedia, Axial Flux Motor, 2026] YASA's motor produces the same output at less than 40% of the weight.

**Second: Weight savings cascade.** A lighter motor means less unsprung weight per axle. Less unsprung weight means less stress on the chassis, longer vehicle life, and — critically — more payload capacity for batteries. **Third: Thermal efficiency in stop-and-go.** Axial flux motors have a larger magnetic surface area relative to their volume, which improves heat dissipation. [Source: Wikipedia, Axial Flux Motor] In urban transit — constant acceleration and braking, tight schedule, no time for thermal recovery — that's not a minor footnote. It's durability.
