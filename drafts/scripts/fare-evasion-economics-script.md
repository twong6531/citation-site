### SECTION 1: Background and How We Got Here (90s–3 min)

*(GRAPHIC: Timeline — 1970s through 2020s, showing evolution of fare collection technology)*

**VO:**  
American transit agencies have been fighting fare evasion for as long as they've been collecting fares. But the tools — and the costs — have changed dramatically.

The original answer was the turnstile, introduced in the early 20th century. A physical barrier that required payment to pass. Simple. Expensive to install and maintain, but it put the enforcement in the infrastructure rather than in human beings paid hourly.

In the 1980s and 90s, European transit systems began experimenting with a different model: **proof-of-payment**, or POP. Passengers buy a ticket or validate a pass before boarding. They're not checked at a gate — instead, fare inspectors make random sweeps. Edmonton's LRT was the first North American system to try it in 1980. [Source: Wikipedia, Proof of Payment]

The appeal was economic: no turnstile infrastructure, faster boarding, cheaper to operate. The tradeoff was obvious: without a physical barrier, evasion is easier. The question was whether the reduced capital and operating costs offset the increased evasion.

*(GRAPHIC: Side-by-side diagram — Barrier system vs. Proof-of-Payment system. Show where the enforcement "touch points" are in each model.)*

LA Metro built its rail system as a proof-of-payment network in the 1990s. No barriers — just validators on platforms. Random inspector sweeps. The system was fast and relatively cheap to build. But the evasion rates that emerged were among the highest of any major US transit system — estimates ranged from 5% on some lines to over 20% on others. [Source: LA Metro Fare Compliance Reports; cited in LA Times coverage, 2019–2022]

NYC took the opposite approach: turnstiles everywhere, police everywhere. But the cost of that enforcement infrastructure — not just the hardware, but the human enforcement layered on top — turned out to be its own fiscal drain.

Washington DC Metro has full barriers — paid areas defined by fare gates at every station — but has struggled with enforcement at the fare gate itself, and saw evasion rates spike sharply after the COVID pandemic disrupted ridership and staffing patterns. [Source: WMATA Inspector General Reports, 2021–2023]

### SECTION 2: The Core Problem — The Enforcement Math (3–5 min)

*(GRAPHIC: Cost breakdown diagram — elements of enforcement cost)*

**VO:**  
Here's where it gets counterintuitive.

When transit agencies talk about fare evasion, they focus on the revenue loss: the $2.90 fare that was never paid, multiplied by millions of boardings. NYC's $690 million figure comes from this calculation: estimated evasion rate × total boardings × fare price. [Source: MTA Quarterly Fare Evasion Report, Q4 2022]

What they don't usually put in the same press release: the cost of enforcement.

Let's build the math for New York.

**The NYPD Transit Bureau** — the unit responsible for subway enforcement — has thousands of officers. Their total budget runs into the hundreds of millions annually. Not all of that is fare enforcement; transit police do crime prevention, emergency response, and other duties. But when the MTA specifically funded a fare evasion crackdown in 2022, it allocated $249 million over two years to add 500 officers. That's roughly $250,000 per officer per year when you include salary, benefits, training, equipment, and overhead. [Source: MTA Budget Documents, 2022; NYPD salary and benefit scales, NYC Comptroller]

Each of those officers is writing citations and making arrests. How much does processing each case cost the system?

*(GRAPHIC: Flow chart — Arrest → Processing → Court appearance → Adjudication → Fine collection. Dollar amounts at each step.)*

A 2019 analysis by the New York City Comptroller's Office found that processing a single fare evasion summons through the civil court system cost the city significantly more than the fine recovered. When evasion was still treated as a criminal matter — up through the early 2010s — each arrest cost the criminal justice system an estimated $500 to over $2,000 in court time, public defender costs, and officer overtime. [Source: NYC Comptroller Scott Stringer, "An Assessment of the NYPD Subway Fare Evasion Enforcement," 2019]

The fine, if collected, was $100. Collection rates for transit summonses are notoriously low — many recipients simply don't pay, particularly if they lack a fixed address or are unbanked.

New York eventually decriminalized fare evasion in stages, moving from criminal summonses to civil infractions, partly to reduce this court burden. [Source: NYC Local Law changes, 2017–2019]

Washington DC went further: in 2018 the city decriminalized fare evasion entirely, reducing the penalty from up to 10 days in jail and a $300 fine to a $50 civil citation. [Source: DC Code § 35-251, amended 2018] The explicit rationale was that prior enforcement had been overwhelmingly concentrated on Black residents — over 90% of citations and summonses were issued to African Americans — and the criminal penalties were wildly disproportionate to the offense. [Source: Wikipedia, Fare Evasion; DC Council records, 2018]

*(GRAPHIC: Bar chart — Three columns: (1) revenue lost to evasion, (2) direct enforcement cost, (3) indirect costs [ridership chilling effect, legal costs]. The third column has a question mark — it's real but hard to quantify.)*

**The indirect costs are where the math gets really uncomfortable for crackdown advocates.**

When transit feels hostile — when riders know that jumping a turnstile means an encounter with police — some portion of riders who would have evaded simply don't ride at all. This matters more than it sounds. Transit agencies lose not just the evaded fare but the potential future fares from that rider, any ancillary revenue (retail at stations, advertising tied to ridership), and the network effect of having a fuller, better-utilized system.

TransitCenter, a transit research and advocacy organization, has documented that aggressive enforcement disproportionately discourages low-income riders and riders of color — precisely the populations most dependent on transit — from using the system. [Source: TransitCenter, "Who's On Board," multiple editions]

This is a fiscal problem, not just an equity one. Transit systems with lower ridership have higher per-rider costs, lower farebox recovery ratios (see our explainer on that), and weaker political support for funding.

### SECTION 3: Implications and What Comes Next (5–7 min)

*(GRAPHIC: Comparison table — NYC, DC Metro, LA Metro: System type, evasion approach, estimated annual evasion cost, enforcement cost, net fiscal outcome)*

**VO:**  
So what's the alternative?

Let's look at what the data actually shows across the three systems.

**New York** has the most aggressive enforcement posture and the most extensive physical barriers. Its evasion rate sits around 13% — high for a barrier system, and partly a product of the emergency exit doors and turnstile design that make hopping relatively easy. [Source: MTA Fare Evasion Quarterly Report, 2022] The enforcement cost to fight that 13% is substantial — the $249M surge alone, before ongoing operational costs. Net fiscal benefit of the surge: unclear, but multiple transit analysts characterized it as at best break-even and likely negative when full costs are counted. [Source: Gothamist, NY Focus transit coverage, 2022–2023]

**LA Metro** has a different problem. Its proof-of-payment system has historically produced higher evasion rates — sometimes cited at 20%+ on certain lines before the gate installation program. [Source: LA Metro Inspector General Fare Compliance Report, 2019] The agency has been retrofitting fare gates since 2008; the TAP gate program cost $154 million in capital investment. [Source: LA Metro Wikipedia article] But LA Metro's enforcement approach — fare inspectors plus local police — has been criticized for inconsistency and for generating racial profiling complaints. The agency is still working through the tradeoff between gate capital costs and inspector operating costs.

**Washington DC** sits in an interesting middle ground. WMATA has full-barrier stations — no proof-of-payment — but still estimated annual evasion losses in the tens of millions of dollars, much of it from back-door bus boarding and broken gates. After decriminalizing evasion in 2018, DC saw evasion rates rise, though WMATA's own data suggests the increase was modest and partially driven by pandemic-related service changes. [Source: WMATA Inspector General, Fare Evasion Audit, 2022]

*(GRAPHIC: Decision tree — "Should you crack down on fare evasion?" flowing through: How much are you losing? → How much does enforcement cost? → What's the ridership effect? → Is the net positive?)*

**So what's the right answer?**

The honest answer is: it depends on the system, and the math has to be done honestly.

Full barrier systems reduce evasion but require sustained capital investment and regular maintenance. Turnstiles break. Emergency exits get propped open. In NYC, the MTA has been running repair and hardening programs on its turnstile infrastructure for years because the hardware itself is a leak in the barrier system. [Source: MTA Capital Program documents]

Proof-of-payment systems are cheaper to build and operate, and speed up boarding — but require a credible inspection threat to function. The research on POP systems suggests that an inspection rate of around 10-15% of riders is sufficient to deter most evasion; below that, the rational-actor calculation shifts toward skipping payment. [Source: Cervero, Robert, "Transit Metropolis," 1998; European fare compliance studies] Achieving 10-15% coverage requires more inspectors than most US agencies deploy.

The hybrid approach — barriers plus inspectors plus occasional surges — is what most US agencies actually run, and it tends to be the most expensive option because it carries the costs of both models without fully realizing the benefits of either.

What the political debate almost never seriously engages with is the **opportunity cost question**: if an agency has $250 million to spend on transit improvement, is deploying it on enforcement the highest-value use of that money? Or does the same money invested in service frequency, reliability, or fare affordability programs generate more net revenue by growing ridership?

The fare evasion crackdown is a compelling political story — it's visible, it's action, it targets a behavior people find annoying. The economics, when you run them carefully, are a lot less compelling.
